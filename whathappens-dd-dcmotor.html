The DC-Motor Speed–Torque Line: Why a Motor Is a Torque Source in Disguise

Hand someone a motor and a battery and ask the only two questions that matter — how fast will it spin, and how hard will it pull? The honest, complete answer is a single straight line. Two clean facts — torque follows current, and the motor fights back with a voltage that grows with speed — collapse all the messy electromechanics into one line from stall torque to no-load speed. Learn to read that line and you can size, predict and debug almost any DC drive without ever touching a differential equation.

Hand someone a motor and a 12-volt battery and ask the only two questions that matter — how fast will it spin, and how hard will it pull? — and the honest, complete answer turns out to be a single straight line. A motor looks as though it ought to be hopeless to predict: current makes torque, motion makes a voltage, the two fight through the winding’s resistance and inductance, and the whole thing feeds back on itself into a tangle of coupled electromechanics you feel you ought to solve rather than reason about. But a brushed DC motor obeys only two linear laws — torque follows current (T = kt·I), and a spinning motor fights back with a voltage proportional to speed (Vemf = ke·ω) — and from those two facts the entire tangle collapses into one line in the speed–torque plane, running from a no-load speed set purely by voltage down to a stall torque set by voltage over resistance. Learn to read that line and you can size, predict and debug almost any DC drive without writing a differential equation: raise the voltage and the whole line slides up parallel to itself, the load decides where on it you sit, and two parabolas riding on top — output power and I2R heat — tell you where it is efficient and where it cooks. From that one move the whole subject falls out: the no-load and stall ends, why maximum power sits uselessly hot at mid-line, why the real limit is a thermal box far below stall, and what gearing is actually for. Then the piece turns to the half the textbooks skip: how you size a drive, how it bites when it misbehaves, what to spec, and a 12 V gearmotor for a small robot sized end to end.

1The constraint: predicting a motor looks hopeless

Start with exactly what you want. You have a job — lift this, spin that, roll a robot forward — at some speed, against some load, from some battery, and you want to know before you build it how fast the motor will run, how much current it will draw, how hot it will get, and whether it can even start under that load. These are not idle questions; each one is a part you have to buy or a fuse you have to rate, and getting any of them wrong shows up as a stalled, smoking, or sluggish machine.

But a motor is a genuine tangle. Current through the windings makes torque; the resulting motion makes a voltage; that voltage and the applied voltage fight through the winding’s resistance and inductance; and the current that results is what made the torque in the first place, so the whole thing feeds back on itself. Written out as differential equations it looks like something you sit down and integrate, not something you reason about at the bench. And yet experienced practitioners size motors in their heads in a few seconds, with nothing but a datasheet and a battery voltage. The question this piece answers is whether that tangle really does collapse into something you can simply see — and it does, into one line.

2The reframe: two clean facts, nothing else

It collapses because a brushed DC motor, underneath all the coupling, obeys just two linear laws, and everything else is their consequence. The first is that torque follows current. The magnetic force on a current-carrying winding sitting in the motor’s field is simply proportional to the current through it, so the torque is proportional to the current: T = kt·I, where the torque constant kt is a fixed property of the magnets and the winding. Double the current, double the torque — that is all torque is. There is no hidden nonlinearity to find; the motor is, at heart, a device that turns amps into newton-metres at a fixed exchange rate.

The second law is that the motor fights back with a voltage proportional to speed. A spinning motor is also a generator: its moving windings cut the same field and produce a “back-EMF” that opposes the supply, Vemf = ke·ω. The faster it turns, the harder it pushes back against the battery. And in SI units a small miracle holds: the back-EMF constant ke and the torque constant kt are numerically the same number — the same electromechanical coupling, read once as volts-per-rad/s and once as newton-metres-per-amp. Now the whole circuit is just the supply V driving current through the winding resistance R against that back-EMF:

I = V − ke·ωRcurrent is whatever is left after the back-EMF opposes the supply

That one line is the key reframe. The current a motor draws is not a fixed property you look up — it is whatever is left over after the back-EMF opposes the supply, divided by the resistance. At standstill the back-EMF is zero and the current is enormous; at full speed the back-EMF nearly equals the supply and the current dwindles to almost nothing. The motor self-regulates its own current through its own speed, which is exactly why it does not simply draw a constant current and melt. Hold that thought, because it is the hinge the whole subject swings on.

3The core: it’s a straight line

Now combine the two facts and watch the tangle fall apart. Torque is current times kt, and current is the back-EMF equation above, so T = kt·(V − ke·ω)/R. Solve that for the speed and every mystery resolves into a single relation:

ω = Vke − Rkt·ke·Tspeed falls linearly as torque rises — a straight line

That is a straight line in the speed–torque plane, and it tells you the whole steady-state story of the motor. Read off its three features. The no-load speed ω0 = V/ke is where it settles with nothing to push against (T = 0): the motor speeds up until its back-EMF just equals the supply and the current — and therefore the torque — falls to nothing. It is set purely by voltage. The stall torque Ts = kt·V/R is the other end: hold the shaft still (ω = 0) and there is no back-EMF, so the full supply drives current through R alone — maximum current, maximum torque, maximum heat. And between them runs a line of slope −R/(kt·ke): a low-resistance (“stiff”) motor has a nearly flat line whose speed barely sags as you load it, while a high-resistance motor droops steeply.

So a DC motor is really a torque source you command with current (T = kt·I), wearing the disguise of a speed device. The speed–torque line is simply the map of every steady state it is allowed to occupy. Raise the voltage and the whole line shifts up, parallel to itself — both the no-load speed and the stall torque scale with V — which is exactly what PWM speed control does, chopping the effective supply up and down (Deep Dive #07’s H-bridge is how you actually vary that V). The load, meanwhile, decides where on the line you sit.

One line holds the whole motor
ω = Vke − Rkt·ke·T

The speed–torque line runs from no-load speed V/ke (at zero torque) to stall torque kt·V/R (at zero speed). Torque is just current (T = kt·I); speed just sets the back-EMF. Change the voltage and the line slides up or down bodily; the load decides where on it you sit. Everything else in the subject — power, heat, efficiency, inrush, gearing — is a position or a slope on this one line.

One line from no-load speed to stall torque

Drag the applied voltage and watch the whole line slide up and down bodily — both the no-load speed where it meets the axis and the stall torque where it crosses the other move together, because both scale with V. Now drag the load torque and ride the operating point along the line: as you load it the speed sags down the slope exactly as −R/(ktke) predicts, the current climbs (it is just T/kt), and the output-power parabola drawn alongside crests at the midpoint — half the no-load speed, half the stall torque — then collapses to zero at stall. Keep an eye on the efficiency readout: it peaks nowhere near that power peak, but up in the high-speed, low-torque corner. The line is the motor; everything you care about is a point on it.

4Limit checks — read the line at its ends, and the box inside it

The line earns its keep by behaving sensibly at both extremes, and reading it at its ends is the fastest way to internalise what a motor actually does. Then two parabolas riding on the line turn that picture into the real operating limit.

Limit check — read the line at its ends No load (T → 0): speed rises to V/ke and the current falls to almost nothing — just enough to overcome friction. Fast, cool, lazy. Stall (ω → 0): there is no back-EMF, so all the voltage is across R, the current hits its maximum V/R and the torque its maximum kt·V/R — but the output power is zero (nothing is moving) and 100 % of the input becomes heat in the winding. A motor held near stall cooks itself in seconds, and the stall current is often 5–10× the rated current. Maximum output power sits at the midpoint of the line — half the no-load speed, half the stall torque — where Pout = T·ω peaks as a parabola. You rarely want to live there: it is hot and only about half-efficient. Maximum efficiency is not at maximum power — it sits up near no-load, at a modest torque, because efficiency is killed by the I2R heating, which grows with torque². Cruise motors live in the high-speed, low-torque corner. Raise V and the whole line translates upward — more speed and more available torque, but also more stall current and heat. Add series R (or feed it from a weak driver) and the line droops more steeply: the same no-load speed if V is held, but less stall torque and a softer, saggier response.

Two parabolas ride on this line and govern everything you actually care about. Output power Pout = T·ω is a downward parabola — zero at both ends (no torque at no-load, no motion at stall) and peaking mid-line. Dissipation is I2R = (T/kt)2·R, so the heat grows with the square of torque and piles up entirely at the high-torque end. Put those two together and the motor’s true limit is not the line itself but a thermal box inside it: the continuous (rated) torque is whatever the winding can shed as heat indefinitely, far below stall, while the full line all the way up to stall is only reachable in brief peak bursts before the winding overheats.

That is why every datasheet draws two regions — a continuous band and an intermittent one — and why “it has plenty of stall torque” is a trap: you can touch stall torque for a moment, you cannot live there. Gearing, finally, is simply a lever that moves your load along the line: a reduction ratio N multiplies torque by N and divides speed by N, letting you place a fast, weak, efficient motor exactly where a slow, strong load actually needs to sit — which is where the operating point really comes from.

The operating point is where the motor line meets the load

The motor line is only half the picture; the load draws its own curve, and the operating point is where the two cross. Switch the load type — a constant-torque load (a hoist) is a vertical line, a fan or propeller is a rising ω2 curve, a viscous drag rises with speed — and watch the intersection jump to a different place on the motor line. Now drag the voltage and the motor line slides, carrying the operating point with it. The continuous-duty region is shaded: when the intersection sits inside it the verdict reads safe; push the load up or the voltage down until the crossing drifts toward stall and it flips to overload — the motor is now outside its thermal box, drawing a current it cannot shed. That shaded box, not the stall torque, is the limit you design to.

5Where it lives: sizing a drive, and how it bites

The speed–torque line is the first thing you draw when sizing any DC or BLDC drive, a gearmotor, a servo or an actuator — and the first thing you reach for when one misbehaves. The design sequence is fixed, not a flash of insight: find the load (the torque it needs and the speed you want, and the worst case — starting torque, peak torque); pick a supply voltage to set the no-load speed above your target; check that the operating point, where your load torque meets the line, sits in the continuous region with margin; check the current there against the driver and the thermal limit; and add gearing to move a mismatched load onto a good part of the line. When it misbehaves, the symptoms map straight back to the line — and almost every one is really “you are sitting somewhere on the line you did not mean to”:

Runs slower than expected under load
You are far down the sloped part of the line — high R, or a load torque close to stall — and the speed sags with load exactly as the slope predicts. Raise the voltage or gear it down to move the operating point back up toward no-load.
Motor or driver gets very hot, maybe smells
You are operating near stall / above continuous torque: the current is T/kt, the heat is I2R, and you have left the thermal box. Reduce the load, gear down, or size up — the line is fine, your point on it is not.
Won’t start under load (just buzzes and heats)
The stall torque at your voltage is below the load’s breakaway torque: Ts = kt·V/R simply isn’t enough to move it, so it sits at zero speed drawing stall current. More voltage or more gearing.
Trips the current limit on startup
Inrush = stall current = V/R at the instant of switch-on, because the back-EMF is still zero — often 5–10× the running current. Soft-start or current-limit it; do not just fuse for the running current.
Top speed too low
The no-load speed V/ke is set by voltage (and the winding). You need more volts, or a lower-ke winding — not vaguely “more power.”

6How the line shapes the whole machine

Nearly every drive decision is a move on this one line, and the knobs are not independent. Voltage sets the ceiling — the no-load speed — and scales the whole line, so it is your speed knob (delivered as PWM). Winding resistance R sets the stiffness: low R means a flat line, so speed holds under load and efficiency is high, but the inrush is big — and this is the motor’s quality figure of merit, the motor constant km = kt/√R, torque per square-root-watt of heat. The winding turns (which set ke and kt together) trade speed for torque at a given voltage: more turns means a higher ke, hence a lower no-load speed but more torque per amp. Gear ratio relocates the load onto the efficient stretch of the line and multiplies torque, at the cost of speed and a little loss. And the thermal design — winding, housing, airflow, heatsink — sets how much of the line below stall you can actually use continuously. Speed, torque, efficiency, heat and inrush are not five independent dials; they are all positions and slopes on this single line, and moving one moves the others.

7Where it’s used, and what to spec

The same line governs brushed DC motors, BLDC and PMSM machines (with the line drawn per-phase behind field-oriented control — Deep Dive #02), gearmotors, servos and linear actuators; steppers and induction motors have their own curves, but the sizing instinct transfers intact. When you choose a motor, these are the numbers that actually decide the outcome:

Rated (continuous) torque & speed
The point on the line you can hold forever. Your operating point must sit here, with margin — this, not stall torque, is the number you size to.
Stall torque & stall current
The ceiling and the inrush. Size the driver and the protection for the stall current, not the running current.
No-load speed & rated voltage
The top of the line; the no-load speed at your supply must comfortably exceed your target running speed.
kt / ke (torque & back-EMF constants)
Torque per amp and volts per rad/s. They let you predict the current and the speed directly, without a test rig.
Terminal resistance R (and km = kt/√R)
The slope and stiffness of the line, and the honest efficiency figure of merit. Compare competing motors by km, not by nameplate watts.
Thermal rating / continuous vs peak region
How long you may exceed continuous torque. This is the real limit — the line without it is a lie by omission.
Gear ratio & efficiency (for a gearmotor)
Where it places the load on the line, and what the reduction costs you in loss and backlash.

8Field notes

What the datasheet won’t tell you
  • A motor is a torque (current) source, not a speed source. Command the current and you command the torque directly and instantly; the speed is simply whatever the load and the back-EMF settle to. This is why servo loops almost always put a current/torque loop on the inside — it is the variable you really control.
  • Stall is a thermal event, not an operating point. You can brush against stall torque for a moment; live there and the I2R cooks the winding in seconds. Stall current is 5–10× rated — budget the driver and fuse for it, and never DC-hold a motor stalled.
  • Inrush is the stall current, every single time you switch on. At t = 0 the back-EMF is zero, so the current is V/R. Soft-start, current-limit, or in-line resistance — otherwise you trip protection or weld contacts on every start.
  • Efficiency peaks nowhere near maximum power. Max power is the hot, lossy mid-line; the efficient zone is high-speed and low-torque. Size so your cruise point lives up there and your peak point only dips toward mid-line briefly.
  • Compare motors by km = kt/√R, not by “watts.” The motor constant is torque per √(heat) — the honest, voltage-independent quality number. A bigger nameplate wattage sitting on a worse km is simply a worse motor.
  • Gearing is the cheapest torque you will ever buy. N× torque for 1/N speed, and it usually beats buying a bigger motor — but it adds backlash, reflects the motor’s inertia up by N2, and spends a little efficiency.
  • The line sags when the winding is hot. Copper resistance rises about +0.4 %/°C, so a hot motor has a steeper line — less torque and less speed than the cold datasheet promises. Design with the hot-winding R, not the 25 °C value.
  • “It has enough stall torque” is a trap. Stall torque is the ceiling you can touch, not the shelf you can stand on. Size to continuous torque with margin, and treat stall as inrush and emergency only.

9Build it: sizing a 12 V gearmotor to drive a small robot wheel

Enough principle — here is a real drive sized end to end, so you can copy the shape and reuse it. The job: move a ≈ 5 kg robot at ≈ 1 m/s on 100 mm wheels, up a gentle ramp, from a 12 V battery. Every line below is one number you read straight off the speed–torque line; the derivation already did the hard part.

Worked example — a 12 V gearmotor for a small robot
Load torque & speed at the wheel
Wheel radius r = 0.05 m. To push the robot up the ramp takes a worst-case drive force of maybe ≈ 10 N, so the wheel torque is Twheel = F·r = 10·0.05 = 0.5 N·m. The wheel speed for 1 m/s is v/r = 1/0.05 = 20 rad/s ≈ 190 rpm.
Pick a motor + gear
A small 12 V motor runs ≈ 6000 rpm no-load and makes maybe 0.02 N·m continuous — nowhere near 0.5 N·m directly. Add a gearbox: the ratio N must bring 6000 rpm down toward ~190–400 rpm (the loaded speed sags), so N ≈ 20:1 gives ~300 rpm no-load at the output and multiplies torque ×20 → ≈ 0.4 N·m continuous at the wheel — the right range (go 30:1 for margin).
Check the operating point on the line
Reflect the load back to the motor shaft: 0.5 N·m ÷ 20 = 0.025 N·m at the motor. Confirm the motor sits in its continuous region there, not up near stall. If the motor-shaft torque exceeds the continuous rating, increase N (more reduction) or size up.
Current & inrush
Running current ≈ Tmotor/kt — an amp or so. But the stall / inrush current is V/R (e.g. 12 V / 1.5 Ω = 8 A) and it hits every start. Size the driver (an H-bridge, Deep Dive #07) and the fuse for that, with a soft-start — not for the ~1 A running current.
Thermal sanity
Continuous dissipation ≈ I2R at the running current; confirm the motor can shed it. Climbing the ramp is a peak event, brief — so short excursions above continuous torque are fine as long as they stay short.
Speed knob
Vary the wheel speed with PWM on the supply, which slides the line up and down. Closed-loop, regulate the current (torque) in the inner loop and let speed fall out of it.

That is the method made concrete: a mass, a speed, a wheel size, a ramp and a battery voltage hand you the wheel torque, the gear ratio, the operating point, the inrush, and — loudly — the fact that your real risk is not the running load but the 8 A that slams the driver on every start. The plug-in set is small and you already have all of it: torque T = kt·I and back-EMF Vemf = ke·ω (SI: ke = kt); current I = (V − ke·ω)/R; the line ω = V/ke − (R/(kt·ke))·T; no-load speed ω0 = V/ke, stall torque Ts = kt·V/R, stall current Is = V/R; output power Pout = T·ω (peaking at ω0/2, Ts/2) and heat Ploss = I2R = (T/kt)2·R; the quality number km = kt/√R; and gearing Tout = N·Tmotor, ωout = ωmotor/N.

Bring it up in this order. The sequence matters; skipping a step just relocates the pain — usually to the moment the winding cooks or the driver trips:

  1. Measure the constants. Spin the motor unloaded at a known voltage and read the no-load speed → ke = V/ω0. Stall it briefly (or measure R with a meter) and read the stall current → confirm Is = V/R and kt = Ts/Is. Now you own its line.
  2. Draw the line and mark your load. Plot the no-load speed and the stall torque, mark where your load torque lands, and confirm the speed there meets your target.
  3. Check the operating point is in the continuous box — comfortably below stall, with a current the winding can shed. If it isn’t, re-gear or size up before going further.
  4. Set up the driver for inrush. Size the H-bridge / driver and protection for the stall current, and add current-limiting or a soft-start so startup neither trips nor welds.
  5. Add PWM speed control and close the current loop. Vary the voltage for speed, regulate the current (torque) on the inside, and confirm the operating point slides along the line as predicted.
  6. Thermal soak test. Run the real duty cycle to temperature and confirm the winding stabilises — remembering that R, and the whole line, sag once it is hot.

The forks that actually change the design. Most parameters you can trim later; these you decide up front:

Direct-drive vs geared
Direct-drive is simple, backlash-free and fast, but needs a big low-km motor to make torque; gearing gets torque cheaply from a small efficient motor at the cost of backlash, reflected inertia (N2), and a little loss. Most robots gear.
High-speed / low-ke winding vs low-speed / high-ke
Pick the winding so your target speed sits comfortably below no-load at the voltage you have: too high a ke and you cannot reach speed; too low and you lack torque per amp.
Voltage (speed) vs gear ratio (torque)
Need more speed → more volts; need more torque → more reduction. Trade the two to land the operating point in the continuous, efficient zone rather than brute-forcing either.
Brushed vs BLDC
Brushed is cheap and trivially driven (just a voltage) but wears its brushes; BLDC is efficient, long-lived and higher-km but needs commutation electronics (and FOC — Deep Dive #02). The line reasoning is identical for both.
The last mile — what you still do on the bench This page gets you to the right motor class, a sound gear ratio, a safe driver sizing, and an operating point you can defend — about 90 % of the job. The last 10 % lives on the bench and in the duty cycle. Measure the real constants and the hot-winding R: datasheet kt, ke and R are nominal and cold, so the line you actually get is a bit lower — and worse when hot. Verify ke from the no-load speed and R with a meter on your unit. Characterise the real load — breakaway and starting torque, friction, and the true worst case (the ramp, the jam, the cold grease) are usually well above the “nominal” torque; size to the worst case, not the average. Do the thermal duty-cycle math: continuous versus peak is really about time at torque, and an RMS-torque calculation over your actual cycle tells you whether a brief stall-ish peak is fine or fatal. And validate inrush and protection on the bench — scope the startup current, confirm the soft-start and current limit behave, and that nothing browns out. Tools to stand on: the motor’s datasheet curves, a dynamometer or a simple brake plus a current meter to measure the real line, an H-bridge driver with current sense (Deep Dive #07), and an RMS-torque / thermal spreadsheet. The two laws in Part I never change; the last mile is pinning down your motor’s real constants and your load’s real worst case.

10Check yourself

Question 1

Two 12 V motors have the same no-load speed, but motor A’s speed barely drops when you load it while motor B’s sags a lot. Which has the lower winding resistance, and which will draw the bigger inrush current at switch-on?

Motor A has the lower resistance. The slope of the speed–torque line is −R/(ktke), so a flatter line — speed holding under load — means a smaller R; A is the stiff, efficient motor. But that same low R gives A the bigger inrush, because the stall / inrush current is V/R — smaller R, larger V/R. So the very motor that holds its speed gracefully is also the one that slams the biggest current into your driver the instant you switch it on; stiffness and inrush are the same property read two ways, and you must budget the driver and protection for A’s larger startup spike.

Question 2

You need more torque from your drive and you’re tempted to raise the supply voltage. Explain what raising the voltage actually does to the speed–torque line, and why gearing is usually the better way to get torque.

Raising V translates the whole line up, parallel to itself: both the no-load speed V/ke and the stall torque kt·V/R scale with voltage. So you do get more available torque — but you also get more speed you may not want, and a bigger stall current and more heat, and you can easily blow straight past the thermal box. Gearing does something cleaner: a ratio N multiplies torque by N while dividing speed by N, which takes a small, efficient motor’s good high-speed operating region and places it exactly where a slow, high-torque load lives. You buy the torque without inflating the current, the heat or the top speed — cheaper and cooler than brute-forcing it with volts.

Question 3

A motor’s datasheet lists a stall torque of 2 N·m. Your load needs 1.5 N·m continuously. Why is this motor a bad choice even though 1.5 < 2?

Because stall torque is the ceiling you can only touch, not a shelf you can stand on. At stall the motor is at maximum current, moving nothing, and dissipating 100 % of the input as I2R heat in the winding — it cooks itself in seconds. Your 1.5 N·m is about 75 % of that stall figure, deep outside the continuous thermal box, where the current is enormous and the heat is (T/kt)2·R. The continuous torque a motor can actually hold forever is a small fraction of its stall torque, so this one would overheat almost immediately. Size to the continuous rating with margin, and treat stall torque as inrush and emergency headroom only.
