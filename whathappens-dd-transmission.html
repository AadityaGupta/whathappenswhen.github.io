Transmission-Line Reflections: When a Wire Stops Being a Wire

The same board that worked perfectly at 1 MHz rings, overshoots, and double-clocks at 100 MHz — and nothing on it changed but the edge speed. That is the moment a piece of copper stops being a simple wire and becomes a transmission line, where signals travel at a finite speed and bounce off anything that isn’t matched. Learn the one coefficient that governs the bouncing and you can tame — or prevent — the “works slow, fails fast” bug for good.

≈ 17 min read signal integrity · high-speed digital · PCB design companion to The Op-Amp and Microcontrollers / Embedded

You lay out a board, it works, you ship it — and then you speed the clock up, or drop in a faster driver with sharper edges, and the exact same copper starts corrupting the signal: overshoot and undershoot at the receiver, ringing that rattles across the switching threshold, a rising edge that climbs in a staircase, logic that double-clocks or latches the wrong value. You changed no geometry and added no component; only the edge got faster. The puzzle is sharp: why does an inert piece of wire, perfectly fine at low speed, suddenly wreck the signal when the edges get fast? The hidden assumption that just broke is that a wire is instantaneous — that the whole trace sits at one voltage at any instant. It doesn’t: a voltage step travels down the trace at a finite speed (≈ 15 cm/ns in a PCB), so while the wave is in flight the near end can be high while the far end is still low, and the wire is a transmission line, not a lumped node. A travelling wave carries a fixed ratio of voltage to current — the characteristic impedance Z0 = √(L/C), about 50 Ω for a typical single-ended trace — and when it hits an impedance change at the far end, part of it bounces back by the reflection coefficient Γ = (ZL − Z0)/(ZL + Z0): +1 at an open end (the voltage doubles), −1 at a short, and 0 when the load is matched to Z0 (no reflection at all). The reflections re-reflect off the source and ring, stepped by the round-trip time trt = 2ℓ/v, and that ringing is the bug. From that one coefficient the whole subject falls out: when a wire is “electrically long” enough to matter (round-trip delay ≳ rise time, so it is the rise time, not the clock, that decides), and how you kill the ringing by matching an end — series termination at the driver (Rs = Z0 − Rdriver) or parallel termination at the load. Then the piece turns to the half the textbooks skip: where it lives in fast digital and RF, how it bites and how to read the symptoms, what to spec, and a 100 MHz clock on a 20 cm trace terminated end to end.

1The constraint: a wire that works slow and fails fast

Start with exactly the thing that just went wrong. You lay out a board, it works, you ship it — and then you speed up the clock, or swap in a pin-compatible but faster driver with sharper edges, and the exact same copper starts misbehaving. The receiver sees overshoot and undershoot; ringing rattles around the switching threshold; a rising edge climbs in a staircase instead of a clean step; logic double-clocks or latches the wrong value. You changed no geometry and added no component — only the edge speed went up.

The puzzle is sharp, and it is genuinely baffling the first time you meet it: why does an inert piece of wire, perfectly fine at 1 MHz, suddenly corrupt the signal when the edges get fast? Nothing about the copper changed. The mental model that served you at low speed — treat the whole trace as a single node sitting at one voltage — has quietly stopped being true, and the question this piece answers is exactly when it stops being true, why, and what to do about it.

2The reframe: a wire has a finite signal speed and a characteristic impedance

The hidden assumption that just broke is that a wire is instantaneous — that the entire trace is at one voltage at any instant. It isn’t. A voltage step launched at one end travels down the wire at a finite speed, roughly half the speed of light in a PCB dielectric, about 15 cm/ns. While that wave is in flight, the near end can be high while the far end is still sitting low — different points of the same wire are at different voltages at the same time. Once that is true, the wire is a transmission line, not a lumped node, and you have to think of the signal as something that travels and takes time to arrive.

A travelling wave has a fixed relationship between its voltage and its current, set entirely by the line’s geometry — specifically its inductance and capacitance per unit length:

Z0 = √(L/C)the characteristic impedance — ≈ 50 Ω for typical single-ended PCB traces, 75/90/100 Ω for others

Here is the key thing to hold on to: to the driver launching the edge, the line looks like a resistor of value Z0 for as long as the wave is still travelling. The driver pushes current I = V/Z0 into what feels like a pure resistance, even though it is only copper over a reference plane with nothing resistive about it. Z0 is the new character in the story — the “ohms” a wave sees while it is in motion.

3The core: the reflection coefficient

Now send the wave down the line and let it reach the far end, where the load has some impedance ZL. The incident wave insists on V/I = Z0. The load insists on V/I = ZL. Both cannot be true at the junction at once — so something new must be launched to reconcile them. A reflected wave travels back up the line, carrying exactly the voltage and current needed to satisfy both conditions at the boundary. Solve that boundary condition and the fraction that reflects is the reflection coefficient:

Γ = ZL − Z0ZL + Z0the fraction of the wave that bounces back at an impedance change

Read its three signature values and you understand reflections completely. At an open far end (ZL = ∞ — an unloaded CMOS input is close to this), Γ = +1: the wave reflects fully and in phase, so the voltage at the open end doubles. At a shorted far end (ZL = 0), Γ = −1: full reflection, but inverted. And when the load is matched (ZL = Z0), Γ = 0: no reflection at all. The line swallows the wave as if the copper went on forever. That last case is the entire cure.

The story doesn’t end at the far end. The reflected wave runs back up the line, hits the source, and re-reflects by the source’s own coefficient Γs = (Zs − Z0)/(Zs + Z0); that reflection runs forward again, and so on. Each bounce is smaller if there is any loss or partial match, and the receiver voltage climbs — or rings — its way to the final value in steps spaced by the round-trip time:

trt = 2ℓvone wave down the line and back, at the signal velocity v ≈ 15 cm/ns

That staircase, or that ringing, is nothing more than the successive reflections superimposed. It is worth setting the whole payoff apart, because everything downstream is a consequence of it.

Match the end and the ringing vanishes

Drag the load impedance from open, through matched, to short, and watch the step launch down the line, hit the far end, and come bouncing back. At an open end (Γ = +1) the far-end voltage doubles and the receiver rings; at a short (Γ = −1) it inverts; and right at the matched point, ZL = Z0, the reflection vanishes and a single clean edge is all that arrives. Watch the Γ readout cross zero exactly where the ringing goes quiet — that one value, driven to 0, is the whole of termination.

4Limit checks — when does a wire become a line?

The reflection coefficient earns its keep at the extremes, and reading it there is the fastest way to see both where the trouble comes from and — the real question — when a wire even becomes a line in the first place.

Limit check — when is a wire a line? Matched (ZL = Z0): Γ = 0, no reflection, a clean single edge arrives. The design goal. Open / high-impedance load (the default CMOS case): Γ = +1 — reflections are maximal, the voltage doubles and rings. An unterminated fast line is the worst case, and also the most common one. Slow edges / short line (rise time ≫ round-trip time): the wave fills the whole line before the edge even finishes, so every point tracks together and the line behaves as a lumped node — a wire is just a wire. This is why low-speed designs never need termination. Fast edges / long line (rise time ≪ round-trip time): the line is fully distributed, reflections arrive well after the edge, and they corrupt it. The practical threshold is simple: treat a line as electrically long, and terminate it, when the round-trip delay exceeds the rise time — equivalently, length ≳ (trise × v)/6. It is the rise time, not the clock frequency, that decides. Zero loss, perfect mismatch: the ring never fully dies, because a lossless line re-reflects forever — real lines have loss, so rings decay, but slowly enough to cause errors in the meantime.

trt ≳ triseelectrically long when the round-trip delay exceeds the rise time  (⇔ ℓ ≳ trise·v/6)
It’s the rise time, not the clock

Here the whole “works slow, fails fast” mystery is on one canvas. Drag the rise time and the length and watch the verdict flip. Slow the edge or shorten the trace and the round-trip delay is a fraction of the rise time: the line charges as one node, the panel reads lumped, and the wire is just a wire. Speed the edge up or stretch the trace until the round-trip delay crosses the rise time and the same line snaps into electrically long — the step now rings, because reflections arrive after the edge has finished. Nothing about the mismatch changed; only the ratio of round-trip time to rise time did — and that single ratio is the whole test of whether you have a transmission-line problem at all.

Since reflections come from mismatch, you kill them by matching an end — and there are two families, each matching a different end. Series (source) termination puts a resistor Rs in series right at the driver, sized so the driver’s own output resistance plus Rs equals Z0:

Rs = Z0 − Rdriverseries termination — make Rdriver + Rs = Z0 so the source is matched

The driver now launches a half-amplitude wave — a voltage divider between Rs and the line’s Z0 — which travels to the open far end, where Γ = +1 doubles it back to full amplitude, so the receiver sees a clean full-swing edge; the reflection that returns is then absorbed at the now-matched source (Γs = 0). One resistor, no DC power — but only the far end gets a clean edge, since points along the line see the half-step first. It is ideal for point-to-point. Parallel (end) termination instead puts a resistor equal to Z0 at the load — to ground, to Vcc, as a Thévenin pair, or an AC/RC version to save power. The wave is absorbed the instant it arrives, ΓL = 0, there is no reflection at all, and every point on the line is clean — but it burns DC power and loads the driver.

Everything else in high-speed design flows from this one idea. Impedance-controlled PCBs are traces deliberately built to a target Z0 by controlling width and dielectric height; keeping stubs and vias short is about not leaving little discontinuities that reflect; differential pairs are matched to 100 Ω; and the whole discipline of signal integrity — eye diagrams, overshoot and undershoot budgets, crosstalk — is, at bottom, managing where waves travel and making sure they do not bounce.

5Where it lives: fast digital/RF design, and how it bites

Transmission-line effects govern every fast interface: DDR memory, high-speed serial (USB, PCIe, Ethernet, SATA, HDMI), clock distribution, long logic runs, backplanes and cables, and all of RF. You meet it the moment edges get fast or runs get long. The design sequence is fixed, not a flash of insight: estimate the signal’s rise time; compare the line’s round-trip delay to it to decide whether the line is electrically long; if it is, set the trace to a controlled Z0 and choose a termination — series or parallel — to match an end; keep stubs and vias minimal; and verify with simulation and a scope or TDR. When it bites, the symptoms are a recognisable set, and almost every one is the reflection coefficient talking back:

Ringing / overshoot / undershoot at the receiver
An unterminated or mismatched fast line (Γ ≠ 0); the classic. Add the right termination for Z0.
Staircase edges or a “plateau” partway up
Reflections stepping the voltage over successive round-trips, or a series-terminated line seen mid-line (the half-step before the far-end doubling). Expected with source termination; a bug if unintended.
Double-clocking / false triggering
Ring crossing the logic threshold more than once near the edge — the overshoot/undershoot is landing in the switching window.
“Works at low speed, fails at high speed” / fails only with the fast part
The rise time crossed the electrically-long threshold and nothing else changed. The signature clue.
Reflections off a connector, via, or stub
An impedance discontinuity mid-path (an unmatched connector, a long via stub, a T-branch). Back-drill stubs, match connectors, avoid stubs.
EMI radiating from the ringing
The bouncing energy couples out; fixing the match usually fixes the emissions too.

6How Z0 and rise time shape the system

Two numbers set the whole high-speed personality of a board, and they are not independent. The rise time decides whether you have a transmission-line problem at all — it is the real design driver, not the clock rate. A 10 MHz clock with 300 ps edges is a transmission-line problem; a 100 MHz clock with deliberately slow edges might not be. And the characteristic impedance Z0 — set by trace width, dielectric thickness, and copper weight — is the quantity every termination, connector, and cable along the path must agree on; a controlled, consistent Z0 end to end is exactly what lets a wave travel without bouncing. Together those two drive the stack-up (the dielectric heights that give 50 and 100 Ω), the routing rules (length matching, stub limits, reference-plane continuity), the termination strategy and its power budget, and the connector and cable selection. “Fast digital design” is, in large part, the discipline of giving signals a constant-Z0 path and matching its ends.

7Where it’s used, and what to spec

It governs DDR and high-speed serial links, clock trees, RF and microwave, backplanes, cables and connectors, and any long or fast logic net; antennas and RF front-ends live and die by it. When you design or debug one, these are the numbers that actually decide the outcome:

Characteristic impedance Z0
The target (50 Ω single-ended, 100 Ω differential, 75 Ω video/RF), set by trace geometry and dielectric. Everything along the path matches to it.
Signal rise/fall time
Sets whether the line is electrically long and how tight the matching must be. Spec and control it — and don’t use edges faster than you actually need.
Termination type & value
Series (Rs = Z0 − Rdriver, point-to-point, low power) vs parallel (= Z0 at the load, multi-drop, higher power) vs Thévenin/AC. The core signal-integrity choice.
Line length vs round-trip delay
Keep it short enough to stay lumped, or terminate; length-match differential pairs and parallel buses.
Discontinuities (vias, stubs, connectors)
Minimise and back-drill; each one is a reflector. Spec connector and via impedance.
Loss (dielectric + skin) & crosstalk
At very high rates, attenuation and coupling matter as much as reflections. Budget them (insertion loss, eye mask).
Return path / reference-plane continuity
A signal’s current returns right under the trace, so a gap in the plane is a huge discontinuity. Keep the reference solid.

8Field notes

What the textbook won’t tell you
  • It’s the rise time, not the clock frequency. A “slow” bus with a modern fast-edged driver can ring hard; a fast clock with a deliberately slowed edge may be fine. Always reason from the edge — and slow edges down when you can afford to, the cheapest fix for reflections and EMI both.
  • An open far end is the worst, and it’s the default. Most logic inputs are high-impedance (Γ = +1), so an untreated fast net doubles and rings. Assume you need termination on anything fast and long until you prove otherwise.
  • Series termination gives a clean edge only at the far end. Mid-line taps see the half-amplitude step before the far-end doubling — fine for a single receiver at the end, wrong for a multi-drop bus (use parallel/Thévenin there).
  • The return current flows right under the trace. Signal integrity is a loop; a slot or split in the reference plane under a fast trace is a massive discontinuity and an EMI antenna. Never route a fast signal over a plane gap.
  • Stubs and via stubs are stealth reflectors. A short T-branch or an unused via barrel resonates and corrupts fast signals; keep stubs ≪ the rise-time length and back-drill via stubs on high-rate links.
  • A TDR is the truth machine. A time-domain reflectometer literally shows you where the impedance changes along the line (and whether it’s open, short, or a bump) — learn to read one before guessing.
  • Match where the mismatch is. A clean board with one bad connector still rings; chase the discontinuity, don’t just pile on termination.
  • 50 Ω isn’t sacred — consistency is. Any Z0 works if the whole path and its terminations agree; reflections come from changes in Z0, not from its value.

9Build it: a 100 MHz clock on a 20 cm trace

Enough principle — here is a real decision walked end to end, so you can copy the shape and reuse it. The goal: route a 100 MHz clock to a single FPGA input across a 20 cm PCB trace, and decide whether — and how — to terminate it. The driver has a ≈ 0.5 ns rise time; the receiver is a high-impedance CMOS input, so the far end is effectively open (Γ ≈ +1). Walk the numbers:

Worked example — a 100 MHz clock to an FPGA over 20 cm
Signal velocity & delay
PCB velocity ≈ 15 cm/ns, so one-way delay = 20/15 ≈ 1.33 ns; round-trip trt ≈ 2.67 ns.
Electrically long?
Compare trt (2.67 ns) to the rise time (0.5 ns): round-trip ≫ rise time, so the line is firmly electrically long → reflections will corrupt the edge. (Threshold check: length 20 cm ≫ (0.5 ns × 15 cm/ns)/6 ≈ 1.25 cm.) You must terminate. Note the clock is “only” 100 MHz — it is the rise time that damns it, not the frequency.
Unterminated behaviour
Γload = +1, so the open end doubles and rings; with a typical low-impedance driver the ring decays over several 2.67 ns round-trips — right through the receiver’s threshold. Double-clocking risk.
Pick the termination
One receiver at the far end, point-to-point → series (source) termination. If the driver output impedance is ≈ 20 Ω and the trace is Z0 = 50 Ω, add Rs = Z0 − Rdriver = 50 − 20 = 30 Ω in series at the driver. The launched half-step (with Rdriver + Rs = 50 Ω into a 50 Ω line) travels to the open end, doubles to full swing, and the return is absorbed at the matched source. Clean full-amplitude edge at the FPGA, essentially no DC power.
Alternative (multi-drop)
If several inputs tapped the line, series wouldn’t work (mid-line points see the half-step); use parallel 50 Ω at the far end — to a Thévenin Vcc/GND pair to cut DC power — accepting the DC current.
Control the Z0
Tell the fab the trace must be 50 Ω (set width and dielectric height in the stack-up), keep the reference plane solid under it, and keep the series resistor and any via stubs tiny.

That is the method made concrete: a length, a rise time, a driver impedance and a target Z0 hand you the round-trip delay, the electrically-long verdict, the termination type, and the one resistor value that fixes it — and, loudly, the fact that a 100 MHz clock is damned by its 0.5 ns edge, not its frequency. The plug-in set is small, and the whole problem lives in a handful of lines you already own:

Γ = ZL − Z0ZL + Z0reflection coefficient → +1 open, −1 short, 0 matched

Bring it up in this order. The sequence matters; skipping a step just relocates the pain — usually to the moment the receiver double-clocks or the scope shows ring you did not budget for:

  1. Find the rise time (datasheet or scope) — the real driver, not the clock period. This decides everything.
  2. Compute the round-trip delay trt = 2ℓ/v and compare it to the rise time. If trt ≳ trise it is a transmission line — terminate; if not, it is lumped — relax.
  3. Set a controlled Z0 — specify the trace geometry and stack-up to the fab (50 Ω SE / 100 Ω diff), and keep the reference plane continuous.
  4. Choose the termination — series at the source for point-to-point (Rs = Z0 − Rdriver), parallel/Thévenin at the load for multi-drop; place it at the right end.
  5. Minimise discontinuities — short stubs, back-drill high-rate via stubs, match connectors, length-match pairs and buses.
  6. Verify — simulate (IBIS/SPICE), then measure on hardware: scope the receiver for overshoot and ring, and use a TDR to find any impedance bumps along the path.

The forks that actually change the design. Most parameters you can trim later; these you decide up front:

Series vs parallel termination
Series for a single far-end receiver, point-to-point, low power (clean only at the end); parallel (incl. Thévenin/AC) for multi-drop or when every tap must be clean, at the cost of DC power and driver loading.
Terminate vs slow the edge
If you don’t need fast edges, slow them (series R at the driver, slew-rate control): it can push the line back to “lumped” and kills EMI too — often the cheapest fix. Terminate when you genuinely need the speed.
Single-ended vs differential
Differential (matched 100 Ω pairs) rejects common-mode noise and tolerates reference imperfections, and is essential at multi-Gbps; single-ended is simpler and fine when slower and shorter.
Controlled-impedance board vs not
Pay for impedance control on any fast or long interface; skip it only for genuinely slow, short nets. Reflections come from changes in Z0, so the consistency end to end is what you’re buying.

10The honest last mile

A deep dive gets you the mechanism, the electrically-long test, the right Z0 target, and the correct termination — about 90 % of the job. The last 10 % is the fab, the field solver, and the bench.

The last mile — what you still do on the bench This page gets you the mechanism cold, the electrically-long test, the right Z0 target, and the correct termination — a defensible first cut, about 90 % of the job. The last 10 % is pinning down the real numbers on a real board. Get the Z0 right with a field solver and the fab’s real stack-up — hand formulas are approximate, and the actual dielectric constant, trace etch, and copper roughness all shift it, so specify controlled impedance and confirm it with the board house. Simulate with real device models — IBIS/IBIS-AMI or SPICE with the actual driver and receiver and the routed topology (stubs, vias, connectors), not an ideal line — and check the eye and the overshoot budget. Measure on hardware — scope the receiver for ring and threshold crossings, and run a TDR to localise any discontinuity: a bad connector, a plane gap, a via stub; the board will teach you what the model missed. And at multi-Gbps, budget loss and crosstalk too — dielectric and skin attenuation and coupling can dominate reflections, so lean on eye masks, equalisation, and length and spacing rules. Tools to stand on: a 2D field solver / impedance calculator (or the fab’s), IBIS/SPICE signal-integrity simulation (a vendor SI tool, or LTspice for first cuts), a fast oscilloscope, and a TDR. The reflection coefficient in Part I never changes; the last mile is pinning down the real Z0 and finding the real discontinuity on a real board.

Reason these out from the reflection coefficient and the electrically-long test, rather than recalling them — if “works slow, fails fast” still feels like a trick, these are where it stops being one:

Check your understanding

A 25 MHz clock net that worked for years starts failing when you swap to a pin-compatible but faster buffer — same board, same frequency. In transmission-line terms, what changed, and why does the frequency being unchanged not save you?

The faster buffer has a shorter rise time, and it is the rise time — not the clock frequency — that decides whether the line is electrically long (trt ≳ trise). The round-trip delay 2ℓ/v didn’t change, but the rise time shrank below it, so reflections that used to arrive “within” the slow edge now arrive after it and ring through the switching threshold. Same board, same frequency, same mismatch (Γ = +1 at the open input) — the only thing that moved was the edge, and the edge is the whole criterion. You reason from the rise time, never the clock.

Check your understanding

You series-terminate a point-to-point line and it looks perfect at the destination, but a logic analyser tapped halfway along the trace shows a weird half-height plateau for one round-trip. Is the board broken?

No — that is exactly how series termination works. The driver launches a half-amplitude step (the voltage divider of Rdriver + Rs against Z0), and mid-line points see that half-step until the wave reaches the open far end, where Γ = +1 doubles it to full swing and that full level propagates back. Only the far-end receiver is guaranteed clean — which is precisely why series termination is for a single end-of-line receiver, not a mid-line tap. The plateau is the mechanism working, not a fault.

Check your understanding

Two traces carry identical fast signals; one rings badly and one is clean, and both are “unterminated.” The clean one happens to be very short. Explain, with the electrically-long criterion, why length alone made the difference, and what the short one is effectively doing.

For the short trace the round-trip delay trt = 2ℓ/v is ≪ the rise time, so the reflected wave returns before the edge even finishes — the whole line charges together and behaves as a lumped node, so there is effectively no distinct reflection to see. The long trace’s round-trip exceeds the rise time, so its reflections arrive after the edge and ring. The mismatch is identical (Γ = +1 at both open ends); only the short line is “electrically short” enough to hide it. Shortening a line is, in effect, a way of forcing it back to lumped.
