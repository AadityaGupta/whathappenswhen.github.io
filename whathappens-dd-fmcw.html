How to Measure a Nanosecond Without a Nanosecond Stopwatch

FMCW ranging is the trick that lets a cheap chip measure a car’s distance to the centimetre and its speed at the same instant — by refusing to time the echo at all, and listening for a tone instead.

≈ 16 min read radar · LiDAR · signal physics companion to Sensors & Perception and DSA in Embedded/Space

Point a radar at a car 150 metres away and ask the obvious question: how far? The textbook answer is a stopwatch. Send a pulse, time the echo, multiply by the speed of light. It is correct, it is how sonar and the first radars worked — and for a cheap, high-resolution automotive sensor it is almost unusable. This piece is about the sidestep that every modern car radar and a new generation of LiDAR make instead. Like the best engineering tricks, it looks like cheating until you see that the constraints left no other move. We will build it from the one hard fact that makes the stopwatch impossible, and by the end you will have re-derived why resolution is bandwidth, why you get the target’s velocity for free, and why the sample rate of a five-dollar converter is literally your maximum range.

1The constraint: distance is time, and time is too fast

Ranging by time-of-flight is one equation. A pulse leaves, reflects, returns; if the round trip took Δt, the target is

R = c · Δt2

because the light covers the distance twice. The trouble is entirely in the numbers. Light and radio travel about 30 centimetres per nanosecond, so our 150-metre target returns its echo in 2R/c ≈ 1 µs. That part is fine. The problem is resolution: to tell two reflectors 4 cm apart from one another, you must measure Δt to about a quarter of a nanosecond.

Think about what that demands. You need a transmitter that can emit a clean pulse only a fraction of a nanosecond wide (which means enormous instantaneous bandwidth and high peak power), a receiver that can resolve sub-nanosecond arrival times, and an analogue-to-digital converter sampling in the many-gigahertz range to catch the edge. Each of those is expensive, power-hungry, and awkward to put on a commodity CMOS chip bolted behind a bumper. The binding constraint isn’t the physics of light — it’s that the natural variable, time, ticks far too fast to measure cheaply at the precision we want.

So hold that thought the way a good optimiser holds a bottleneck: we are not allowed a fast clock. Everything that follows is forced by trying to get range without one.

2The reframe: if the clock won’t slow down, put the time in the frequency

Here is the move. Instead of a pulse, transmit a continuous tone whose frequency climbs steadily — a chirp. Over a sweep of duration T the frequency rises linearly across a band B, so at time t you are transmitting

ftx(t) = f0 + S · t,    S = BTthe chirp · slope S

where S is the sweep rate, or slope, in hertz per second. Now the frequency you are emitting is a running clock in disguise: each instant has its own frequency stamped on it. An echo that comes back is a faithful copy of what you sent a moment ago — so it carries the frequency of that earlier moment, which is lower than what you are transmitting now. The delay in time has become a gap in frequency. And a frequency gap is something we can measure without any fast clock at all: we can hear it.

The chirp and its echo — the delay becomes a constant frequency gap

The upper ramp is what you’re transmitting; the lower, shifted ramp is the echo, delayed by τ = 2R/c. Slide the target further out and watch the echo slide right — and the vertical gap between the two ramps, the beat, grow in proportion. Notice the gap stays constant for the whole overlap of the sweep. That constant is the whole game.

3The one equation: the beat is the range

Put a number on the gap. The echo left the antenna τ = 2R/c seconds ago, so its instantaneous frequency lags the transmitter’s by the slope times that delay. Multiply the received signal by the one you’re currently transmitting — that’s what a mixer does, and its output contains the difference of the two frequencies — and low-pass away the sum. What’s left is a single steady tone at the beat frequency:

The result everything rests on
fb = S · τ = 2S Rc   ⟹   R = c · fb2S

Range is no longer a time you must catch on a stopwatch. It is a frequency you can measure at leisure — and a low one. For a 4-GHz sweep over 40 µs, a 150-metre target produces a beat of only tens of megahertz; gentler sweeps put it in the kilohertz “audio” range. A slow, cheap converter digitises it comfortably, and a single Fourier transform reads off the range.

That is the sleight of hand in full. We never measured the nanosecond. We turned the impossible timing problem into an easy spectral one by making the transmitter’s own frequency the ruler. Everything from here is consequences.

Limit check — turn off the chirp Let the slope S → 0: a flat, unchanging tone. Then fb → 0 and the range information vanishes completely. You haven’t broken anything — you’ve built a different, older instrument. A constant-frequency radar can still see a moving target through its Doppler shift, which is exactly a police speed gun: it reads velocity and is blind to distance. All of the range lives in the slope. That’s a reassuring sign the equation is telling the truth.

4Resolution is bandwidth, and nothing else

Two reflectors at slightly different ranges produce two beat tones. You separate them the way you separate any two tones — with a Fourier transform over the chirp. And here the Fourier transform hands you a hard limit: a measurement that lasts T seconds can only distinguish frequencies that differ by at least one bin, 1/T. Convert that minimum frequency gap back into a range gap through the equation we just derived:

ΔR = c2S · 1T = c2S T = c2Brange resolution

The slope and the chirp time cancel, because S T = B. What is left is startling in its simplicity: range resolution depends only on the total swept bandwidth B. Not on power, not on how long the chirp is, not on the carrier frequency, not on how far away the target is. Want to tell two things 4 cm apart? Sweep 3.75 GHz. Want a metre? A 150-MHz sweep is plenty. Resolution is something you buy, and the only currency is bandwidth.

Two targets, one knob — resolution is c / 2B

Two reflectors sit a fixed distance apart; the spectrum shows their two beat peaks (plotted directly in range). Widen the bandwidth and the peaks sharpen and split apart; narrow it and they blur into a single blob you can no longer resolve. The crossover sits exactly at ΔR = c/2B.

This single fact explains a visible piece of the world. Automotive radars migrated into the 76–81 GHz band not because 77 GHz is magic, but because that is where regulators freed up as much as 4 GHz of contiguous bandwidth — and 4 GHz is centimetre resolution. The older 24-GHz band had a fraction of that, and could never paint the fine picture a modern imaging radar does. The resolution you can legally buy is set by the band you’re allowed to sweep.

5Velocity comes for free

Now let the target move. A closing target compresses the returning wave — a Doppler shift that adds a frequency

fd = 2vλ,    λ = c/fcarrierDoppler

on top of the range beat. So a single up-chirp actually measures fb = 2S R/c + 2v/λ — range and velocity, tangled together. From one slope you can’t say how much of the beat is distance and how much is motion. That coupling is the one wrinkle, and there are two clean ways to iron it out.

  • Sweep both ways. Chirp up, then down. The Doppler term keeps its sign while the range term flips, so the up-beat and down-beat differ. Their average gives range, their difference gives velocity. Two measurements, two unknowns, solved.
  • Fire a burst of fast chirps. The modern approach: send a rapid sequence of identical short chirps. Within each chirp an FFT gives range (“fast time”). From one chirp to the next, a moving target’s echo advances in phase by a tiny, constant step; a second FFT across the chirps (“slow time”) turns that phase ramp into velocity. The output is a 2-D range–Doppler map with every target as a bright spot at its own distance and speed.
The range–Doppler map — distance down, speed across

Range comes from the FFT within a chirp; velocity from the FFT across the chirp sequence. Move the target in distance and in speed and watch its spot track both axes independently — one frame, two measurements.

This is why an automotive radar reports, for every object, both its distance and its closing speed in a single frame — the raw material for adaptive cruise and automatic braking. And it is the headline feature of FMCW LiDAR: run the same chirp trick on a ~1550 nm laser and mix each return against a copy of the outgoing light, and you get a per-point velocity that ordinary time-of-flight LiDAR simply cannot produce. Aeva and ZF put the first FMCW LiDAR into automotive production, and NVIDIA’s Drive platform integrates it; the industry name for “range plus instantaneous velocity at every pixel” is 4-D LiDAR.

Part II · In practiceapplying it, and why each number matters

6When is it actually better? The hardware tells you

FMCW isn’t free magic; it is a specific trade that happens to fit cheap silicon beautifully. Set it against pulsed time-of-flight:

  • Low power, continuous, integrable. A pulsed system needs a high peak-power transmitter and a picosecond receiver. FMCW transmits a modest continuous signal and reads a slow beat, so the whole thing fits in low-power CMOS — which is exactly why every modern car radar is FMCW.
  • You earn sensitivity by patience. Because you integrate the entire chirp, the signal-to-noise ratio after the FFT grows with the time–bandwidth product B T. You buy range not with a brutal pulse but with a long, cheap sweep — this is called processing gain.
  • Your converter’s speed is your maximum range. The beat must stay below the Nyquist limit of the ADC, fb,max ≤ fs/2. Translate it: Rmax = c fs / 4S. A faster sampler sees further; a steeper slope pulls that ceiling in. The data sheet of a jelly-bean ADC sets how far your radar can see.
The one genuinely hard part — chirp linearity Every equation here assumed the frequency ramps perfectly linearly. It doesn’t, for free: a raw voltage-controlled oscillator sweeps with a bend in it, and any curvature smears a clean beat tone into a blur, destroying the range resolution you paid bandwidth for. Taming it — locking the sweep with a phase-locked loop, or calibrating and correcting the non-linearity in DSP — is where much of the real engineering of an FMCW chip actually goes.

7The design space, on one hand

Four quantities, four knobs, and they pull against each other:

  • Bandwidth B sets range resolution (c/2B) — the only thing that does.
  • Slope S with the ADC rate sets maximum range (c fs/4S).
  • Chirp duration and count set the velocity resolution and the fastest unambiguous speed you can read before the chirp-to-chirp phase step wraps around.
  • Total frame time — all the chirps together — sets your update rate.

You can’t max all four. A car radar wants long range and closing speeds out to roughly ±90 m/s, so it accepts coarser-than-possible resolution and spends its bandwidth and chirp timing accordingly; a short-range parking or gesture radar spends everything on resolution and refresh. But every one of these systems is the same idea you just built — turn an un-measurable time into an easy frequency — dressed for a different job.

8Where it lives in the design & troubleshooting

In a shipped radar the chirp sits at the head of a signal chain: a synthesiser ramps a VCO, a mixer beats each echo down to the audio-to-megahertz band, a cheap ADC digitises it, and two FFTs plus a detector turn the result into a list of targets. Almost every field failure traces back to one of the equations you just built — and shows up as a recognisable symptom. A short diagnostic table:

Range reads scaled / wrong
Chirp slope mismatch — the S the DSP assumes doesn’t match the VCO’s actual sweep — or a non-linear ramp. Since R = c fb/2S, a wrong S scales every distance.
Two close targets won’t separate
Bandwidth too low. Resolution is ΔR = c/2B and nothing else — widen the sweep or live with the blur.
Max range falls short
The far beat is hitting the ADC sample-rate / slope ceiling, Rmax = c fs/4S. Sample faster, or flatten the slope.
Targets smear across range bins
Chirp non-linearity — the VCO sweep isn’t a straight line, so a single target’s beat drifts in frequency and spreads over bins. Linearise the ramp.
Velocity leaks into range / ghost targets
Range–Doppler coupling left unresolved: one up-chirp can’t split 2SR/c from 2v/λ. Use a fast-chirp sequence or an up/down pair.
Phantom targets appear
Interference from another FMCW radar sweeping nearby, or TX→RX leakage and radome reflections showing up as near-range clutter.

9Field notes

What the datasheet won’t tell you
  • Chirp linearity is the whole ballgame. Every equation here assumed a perfectly straight ramp; a real VCO bends, and a bent sweep smears a clean beat into a blur that eats the resolution you paid bandwidth for. You tame it by locking the sweep with a PLL, or by calibrating and correcting the curvature in DSP — and much of a radar chip’s real engineering lives right here.
  • A cheap slow ADC works — but only behind an anti-alias filter. The beat is deliberately low, so a jelly-bean converter digitises it — yet anything past fs/2 folds back as a phantom, so you must band-limit before you sample. And a steeper slope pushes the beat up toward Nyquist, so slope and sample rate are quietly coupled.
  • Angle comes from MIMO, not from one antenna. Modern radar “images” because it builds a virtual array: multiplex several transmitters (TDM or DDM) across a few receivers and you synthesise far more effective elements than you physically have. That trick — not the range maths — is why a bumper radar resolves angle at all.
  • Windowing is not optional. A strong return’s FFT sidelobes will bury a weak target a few bins away unless you apply a Hann or Taylor window first, trading a slightly wider mainlobe for far lower sidelobes. The clean sinc peaks of the theory are what you get only if you remember to window.
  • Mutual interference is real and growing. As every car sprouts radars, one unit’s sweep crosses another’s band and throws up phantom targets. Mitigation — randomised slopes and start frequencies, detect-and-repair in DSP — is now part of the spec, not a footnote.
  • Range–Doppler coupling bites everyone once. The first time, a moving target reads at the wrong distance because its Doppler leaked into the range beat. The flip side is a gift: the Doppler of the stationary world hands you the platform’s own ego-velocity for free.
Build itthe numbers and the procedure — so you can apply it, not just follow it

10Build it: a radar waveform, sized

Enough principle — here is the actual chain of numbers for a concrete sensor, so you can copy the shape and reuse it. Take an automotive mid-range radar at 77 GHz, and watch three choices (bandwidth, chirp time, chirp count) plus one ADC hand you the entire waveform. Each line is a one-line plug-in; the method already did the hard part.

Worked example — sizing an automotive mid-range radar
Resolution → bandwidth
Resolve two cars 15 cm apart: B = c/(2·ΔR) = (3×10⁸)/(2·0.15) = 1 GHz. The whole sweep is bought here and nowhere else.
Chirp time → slope
Sweep that 1 GHz in T = 50 µs: slope S = B/T = 20 MHz/µs (2×10¹³ Hz/s). This one number sets both the beat and the range ceiling.
A 100 m target → its beat
fb = 2SR/c = (2·2×10¹³·100)/(3×10⁸) ≈ 13.3 MHz — low enough that a modest converter reads it at leisure.
Max range 120 m → ADC rate
A 120 m ceiling keeps the far beat under fb,max = 2SR/c = 16 MHz. Nyquist (fb ≤ fs/2) wants fs ≥ 32 MHz; take fs = 40 MHz for anti-alias margin, which by Rmax = c fs/4S reaches 150 m — comfortably past 120.
Velocity → chirp count
Fire N = 128 of those chirps back-to-back (Tc = 50 µs, λ = c/77 GHz ≈ 3.9 mm): resolution Δv = λ/(2N Tc) ≈ 0.30 m/s, unambiguous out to ±vmax = λ/(4Tc) ≈ ±19.5 m/s. The full frame takes N Tc = 6.4 ms.

That is the whole design on five one-liners, each a plug-in with no transforms by hand: resolution ΔR = c/2B, the beat fb = 2SR/c, the reach Rmax = c fs/4S, the speed ceiling vmax = λ/4Tc, and the speed resolution Δv = λ/2N Tc. Change the target spec and the numbers move, but the shape never does.

Size one in this order. Each step consumes the previous one’s output; skip a step and you just relocate the pain:

  1. Resolution → bandwidth. Set B = c/2ΔR from the finest two targets you must split. Everything else is spent inside this sweep.
  2. Max range → ADC rate & slope. Choose the slope S and sampler so the farthest beat stays under Nyquist: Rmax = c fs/4S.
  3. Velocity res + max → chirp time & count. Tc sets the speed ceiling (λ/4Tc); the chirp count N sets the resolution (λ/2N Tc).
  4. Angle → MIMO TX×RX count. Decide how many transmit and receive antennas to multiplex; the virtual array Ntx×Nrx sets angular resolution.
  5. Detector + windowing. Add a CFAR threshold over the range–Doppler map and a Hann/Taylor window so strong returns don’t mask weak ones.
  6. Linearise the chirp. Lock the VCO sweep with a PLL or correct it in DSP — without this, none of the resolution above survives contact with real hardware.

The forks that change the design. These you decide up front; the rest you can trim later:

FMCW vs pulsed
Cheap, continuous, low peak power, integrable in CMOS → FMCW. Need long reach with a hard range gate and can afford a high-peak-power transmitter and a fast receiver → pulsed time-of-flight.
24 vs 77/79 GHz
77/79 GHz opens up to 4 GHz of bandwidth (centimetre resolution) and shrinks the antennas; legacy 24 GHz has a fraction of the band and a coarser picture. The band you’re allowed to sweep sets the resolution you can buy.
Fast-chirp vs triangular
A rapid sequence of identical chirps yields the modern 2-D range–Doppler map — range and speed per target, cleanly separated. A simple up/down triangle solves the coupling with two measurements but can’t image a crowded scene.
TDM vs DDM MIMO
Time-division fires one transmitter at a time (simple, but spends the time budget and caps unambiguous velocity); Doppler-division runs them together on orthogonal Doppler codes (more throughput, more processing).
The last mile — what this page can’t size for you Sizing the waveform is the architecture call, and now you can make it. What it does not do is the hard RF and signal-processing craft that turns a spec into a working sensor: the front-end (the mmWave MMIC, the antenna array and radome), the chirp-linearisation loop, the CFAR detection, clustering and tracking DSP, and interference mitigation are each a discipline of its own. When you build, don’t hand-roll the RF — stand on a platform and spec against it: TI’s mmWave SDK and AWR radar chips, or MATLAB’s Radar and Phased-Array toolboxes for simulation. Your job is the waveform and the architecture call — which, after this, you own.

11Check yourself

Question 1

Your radar cleanly resolves two poles 50 cm apart but blurs two that are 30 cm apart into one. You must separate the 30-cm pair. Do you reach for more transmit power, a longer chirp, or more bandwidth — and why?

More bandwidth. Range resolution is ΔR = c/2B and depends on nothing else — power only buys you range/SNR, and a longer chirp at the same bandwidth doesn’t sharpen resolution (the T cancels). To resolve 30 cm you need B ≥ c/(2·0.3) = 500 MHz.
Question 2

You dial the chirp slope down to zero. What can the sensor still measure, and what has it lost?

With S = 0 the beat frequency collapses to zero and range is gone entirely — but a moving target still produces a Doppler tone 2v/λ. You’ve degenerated into a continuous-wave Doppler radar: it measures velocity only, a speed gun. All of the range information lived in the slope.
Question 3

Your ADC samples at 20 MHz and your slope is 50 MHz/µs. Roughly how far can you see, and how would you double it without a faster converter — at what cost?

Maximum range is Rmax = c fs/4S = (3×10⁸ · 2×10⁷)/(4 · 5×10¹³) ≈ 30 m. Halving the slope S (a gentler sweep) doubles the ceiling to 60 m with the same ADC — but if you drop the slope by dropping bandwidth, you halve your range resolution. More reach, blurrier picture: the central FMCW trade.
