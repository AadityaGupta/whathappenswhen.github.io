Bolted-Joint Preload: Why a Bolt Is a Spring You Pre-Stretch, Not a Hook You Hang Load On

Bolts snap in service at a fraction of their rated strength, back themselves out under vibration, and let flanges leak — all while being comfortably “strong enough” for the load. The fix is never a bigger bolt; it is seeing that a tightened bolt is a stiff spring, pre-stretched to clamp the parts so hard that the fluctuating external load barely reaches it. The preload — not the bolt’s strength — is what carries the load, kills the fatigue, and stops the loosening.

≈ 18 min read fasteners · mechanical design · joints companion to The Involute Gear Tooth and Failure & Systems Safety

You need to hold two parts together — a bracket, a flange, an engine head — so they will not separate, slip, or leak while a load keeps tugging them apart, and the obvious plan is to let the bolt carry the load: find the force, pick a bolt rated above it, torque it down. Yet joints built exactly that way fail in the field all the time — bolts fatigue-fracture after thousands of cycles at a fraction of their rated strength, they vibrate loose and back themselves out, flanges weep and slip — all while the bolt never came close to breaking. The naive picture, a bolt as a hook you hang the load on, predicts the wrong failures, which is the surest sign it is the wrong picture. The escape is a single reframe: a tightened bolt is not a hook, it is a stiff spring you have pre-stretched, and that stretch clamps the parts together with a force — the preload — so hard that the fluctuating external load barely reaches the bolt at all. From that one move the whole subject falls out: two opposing springs sitting in equilibrium before any load arrives, an external pull that then splits between them by their stiffnesses so the bolt picks up only a small fraction C of it, a load factor C = kb/(kb+km) that is the hinge of everything, and the counterintuitive sting that tightening a bolt harder usually makes it last longer. Then the piece turns to the half the textbooks skip: how preload thinking governs every real structural, pressure, and machine joint, how joints actually fail and why nearly every failure reads “preload,” not “strength,” what to put on the drawing, and an M10 8.8 bracket under a 0–10 kN pull worked end to end — including the ugly truth that you set a torque but you want a tension, and the two are joined only through a friction factor that scatters by a third.

1The constraint: “strong enough” bolts still fail

Start with exactly what you are being asked to do. You must hold two parts together so they do not separate, slip, or leak while a fluctuating load tries to pull them apart. The obvious plan writes itself: the bolt carries the load, so find the worst-case load and pick a bolt rated above it. It is clean, it is sizing-by-catalogue, and it is how a great many joints are quietly designed.

And joints designed exactly that way fail constantly. Bolts fatigue-fracture after thousands of cycles at loads far below their static rating — a clean, sudden break in a bolt that was “rated for twice the load.” They vibrate loose and back off until the nut spins free. Flanges leak or slip even though the bolt never came near its strength. Something is wrong with the mental model, and you can see it from the symptoms alone: if the bolt simply “held the load,” a bolt rated at twice the load would be bulletproof — and it plainly isn’t. A picture that says “add strength” while the real joints keep dying of fatigue and loosening is a picture that predicts the wrong failures. So the constraint sharpens into a real bind: you can make the bolt as strong as you like and the joint still fatigues, loosens, and leaks — which means strength was never the variable that mattered. The question this piece answers is what was.

2The reframe: a bolt is a spring you pre-stretch

Change what the bolt is. When you tighten a bolt you do not just “do it up” — you stretch it, elastically, like pulling a very stiff spring, and that stretch pulls the clamped members together with a force called the preload, Fi. The members are springs too — usually far stiffer than the slender bolt — and they get compressed by that same Fi. So a tightened joint is not a bolt holding two parts; it is two opposing springs in equilibrium: the stretched bolt pulling inward, the squeezed members pushing outward, balanced at Fi, before any external load shows up at all.

Once you see the joint that way, the whole question changes. It is no longer “can the bolt take the load” but “what happens to these two preloaded springs when an external pull is added.” And the answer is the entire subject in one sentence: the external load does not go straight into the bolt. It is shared — it stretches the bolt a little more and lets the members relax a little — split between the two springs in proportion to their stiffnesses. A load applied to a pre-clamped pair of springs is spent partly on stretching the stiff one further and mostly on un-squeezing the other, and which gets what is decided entirely by the ratio of the two stiffnesses. That is the move the rest of the piece unpacks.

3The core: the load factor, and why the bolt barely feels the load

Put it in numbers. Let kb be the bolt’s stiffness and km the members’ stiffness, and apply an external tensile load P across the joint. Because the bolt and the members are clamped together, the load divides between them by their stiffness share, and that share has a name — the joint stiffness factor, or load factor:

C = kbkb + kmthe share of any external load the bolt actually feels

Then, as long as the joint stays clamped, the bookkeeping is almost embarrassingly simple. The bolt force rises by only C·P, and the member clamp force drops by (1−C)·P:

Fbolt = Fi + C·Pthe bolt gains only a fraction C of the load
Fclamp = Fi − (1−C)·Pmost of the load is spent un-clamping the members

Here is the magic, and it lives in the size of C. In a metal-to-metal joint the members are a short, fat, enormously stiff column, while the bolt is a long, slender rod — so km ≫ kb, and C comes out small, typically 0.1 to 0.3. That means when you hang a 10 kN external load on a preloaded joint, the bolt only picks up 1–3 kN of it; the rest of the load is spent relieving the members’ compression. The pre-stretched bolt sits up at Fi and barely twitches as the external load comes and goes. And a bolt that “sees” only 20 % of a fluctuating load fatigues far, far more slowly than one that sees all of it — which is exactly why a properly preloaded joint survives while a loose one dies. Strength was never the point; the preload and the stiffness ratio were.

A bolt is a pre-stretched spring, not a hook
Fbolt = Fi + C·P,   C = kbkb + km ≈ 0.1–0.3

Tightening creates a preload Fi that clamps the members; an external load P then splits by the stiffness ratio, so the bolt force is only Fi + C·P. Because metal members are far stiffer than the slender bolt, C is small and the bolt barely feels the fluctuating load — that is what kills fatigue and loosening. The preload, not the bolt’s strength, is the design variable.

The external load splits by stiffness — the stiff members take most of it off the clamp

This is the classic joint diagram. The rising line is the bolt’s stretch, the falling line the members’ compression, and they meet at the preload Fi — two springs balanced before any load. Drag the external load and watch it split: the bolt force climbs by only C·P while the clamp force falls by (1−C)·P, the large share. Drag the stiffness ratio and watch the split tilt — stiffen the members (small C) and the bolt line goes nearly flat; soften them with a gasket (large C) and the bolt suddenly feels most of the load. Now drag the preload down and watch the clamp line walk toward zero: when it touches, the members have come apart and the diagram stops working — the joint has separated.

4Limit checks — read C and Fi at their ends

The two numbers earn their keep at the extremes, and reading them there is the fastest way to see both where the protection comes from and where the joint falls on its face.

Limit check — read C and F_i at their ends C → 0 (members infinitely stiff, or a long, soft bolt): the bolt sees none of the external load until separation — the entire load just relieves the members’ compression. Best possible fatigue case; this is the target you design toward. C → 1 (soft members — a thick gasket — or a very stiff, short bolt): the bolt sees all of the external load, Fbolt = Fi + P. Worst fatigue case, and precisely why soft gaskets make fatigue-prone joints. Preload Fi → 0 (bolt barely snug): no clamp at all. The members separate the instant any load is applied, the bolt takes the full fluctuating load directly, and it slips, loosens, and fatigues — the under-tightened bolt, not the weak bolt, is the classic failure. Preload too high (past about 90 % of proof): the bolt yields or the members crush, and you lose overload margin. The sweet spot is a high preload — typically ~75 % of the bolt’s proof load — high enough to clamp hard and protect against fatigue, with margin before yield. Separation: the joint comes apart (clamp → 0) when P > Fi/(1−C); past that point the bolt suddenly takes the full load and all the protection vanishes at once, so you always keep a margin below it.

Three consequences follow straight from those ends, and they are the whole reason the subject matters. Fatigue first. The bolt’s cyclic stress amplitude is only σa = C·(ΔP/2)/At — a fraction (C) of a fraction (the half-swing) of the external load, spread over the thread’s stress area. That is why, counterintuitively, tightening a bolt more makes it last longer: a higher preload keeps the joint closed so C can do its job, right up until yield. Self-loosening next. Preload is what presses the threads and the faces together hard enough that friction resists the tiny vibration-driven slips that unscrew a nut — the Junker mechanism — so lose the preload and the bolt simply walks out. Separation and slip last. Enough clamp lets a shear joint carry its load by friction between the faces (a slip-critical joint) rather than by the bolt shank bearing against the hole. And the sting in all of it: you cannot set tension directly. You tighten to a torque, and torque relates to preload only through an empirical nut factor K, as T ≈ K·Fi·d with K ≈ 0.2 for plain steel — and K scatters by ±25–35 % with lube, finish, and speed. Half of what you think is preload is friction uncertainty, which is exactly why critical joints reach for angle, stretch, or ultrasonic methods instead.

High preload keeps the joint closed, so the bolt rides a thin band

Here is the fatigue story made visible. Cycle the external load up and down and watch the bolt force: with a healthy preload it rides only a thin band above Fi, rising by the small slope C — the cyclic stress amplitude σa = C·(ΔP/2)/At is tiny, so the bolt effectively never fatigues. Now drag the preload down. Nothing dramatic happens at first — the band just shifts lower — until the clamp force reaches zero and the joint separates: past that point the bolt abruptly feels the full cyclic load, the thin band explodes into the whole swing from zero to P, and the cyclic stress jumps by a factor of five or ten. The lesson is the one that feels backwards: it is the loss of preload, not the presence of load, that destroys the bolt.

5Where it lives: designing a joint, and how it fails

Preload thinking governs every structural, pressure, and machine joint there is — flanges, engine head and rod bolts, brackets, wind-turbine and bridge connections, aerospace structure. The design sequence is fixed, not a flash of insight: compute the required clamp to stop the worst-case slip, separation, or leak; pick the bolt size and grade; set the target preload (about 75 % of proof); pick the tightening method (torque, torque-angle, stretch); and check the stiffness ratio C for fatigue — softening the bolt or stiffening the members if C is too high. When a joint fails it does so in a short, diagnostic vocabulary, and nearly every entry reads “preload,” not “bolt strength”:

Bolt fatigue fracture
Ran fine, then a sudden clean break after many cycles — preload too low, or C too high (a soft or gasketed joint): the bolt is feeling too much of the cyclic load. Raise the preload, stiffen the joint, or lengthen/soften the bolt to drop C.
Bolts backing off / loosening
Insufficient preload against vibration (the Junker mechanism). The cure is more preload, plus locking features or a re-torque — not just a lock washer bolted onto the same slack joint.
Joint leaks or slips in service
Clamp lost over time to embedment, creep, or gasket relaxation; the preload decayed below what the joint needs. Re-torque, harder members, a better gasket.
Bolt snapped during install
Over-torqued past yield (or galling spiking the torque so the reading lied). Check the target, the lube, and the nut factor you actually have.
Thread galling / seizing
Friction, especially with stainless — lubricate or anti-seize, slow the run-down, and re-target the torque for the new K the lube gives you.

6How preload shapes the whole joint

Almost every property of a bolted joint is set by just two numbers — the preload Fi and the load factor C — and the bolt’s static strength is nearly a footnote. The preload sets the fatigue life (through how closed the joint stays), the loosening resistance (friction against vibration), the slip resistance (the friction clamp in shear), and the separation margin. The load factor C — governed by grip length, washers, bolt slenderness, and gasket softness — sets how much of the external load the bolt ever feels, which is the other half of fatigue. And the tightening method sets the preload scatter, which decides how much margin you must carry: a sloppy nut factor forces a conservative target so the low end still clamps and the high end still stays under yield. So “design the bolt” is really “choose a preload and a stiffness ratio, then pick a tightening method accurate enough to land them.” Move any one of those and the others move with it; the catalogue strength number barely enters.

7Where it’s used, and what to spec

Preloaded joints hold structural steel and bridges, pressure vessels and pipe flanges, engines (head, main, and rod bolts), aerospace structure, machine tools, rail, and wind-turbine flanges — anywhere a joint must not separate, slip, leak, or fatigue. When you specify one, these are the numbers that actually decide the outcome:

Bolt grade / proof strength
e.g. 8.8, 10.9, 12.9 — sets the usable preload ceiling; target about 75 % of proof.
Target preload (% of proof)
The real design variable — high enough to clamp hard and protect fatigue, comfortably below yield.
Tightening method & nut factor
Torque (±30 %), torque-angle (tighter), stretch or ultrasonic (tightest) — pick for the accuracy the joint actually needs.
Grip length / washers / bolt slenderness
Tune the load factor C: longer, more slender bolts lower C and improve fatigue.
Gasket / member stiffness
Soft gaskets raise C (bad for fatigue) and creep (lose preload); choose stiff gaskets and plan a re-torque.
Thread locking / anti-loosening
Thread locker, prevailing-torque nuts, wedge-locking washers — for vibration service, matched to the duty.
Re-torque schedule & reuse policy
Account for embedment and relaxation; and do not reuse torque-to-yield bolts.

8Field notes

What the handbook footnotes bury
  • Tightening a bolt more usually makes it last longer. It feels backwards, but higher preload keeps the joint closed so the bolt rides only the small C·P band. Under-tightening, not over-tightening, is the usual fatigue culprit — right up to yield.
  • You control torque but you want tension, and the nut factor scatters ±30 %. A big chunk of your “preload” is friction uncertainty. For anything critical, use torque-angle, bolt stretch, or ultrasonic measurement — they bypass friction and read the tension itself.
  • Short, fat bolts are fatigue-prone; long, slender ones aren’t. A stiffer bolt means a higher C, so it feels more of the load. The classic fix for a fatiguing joint is a longer bolt (or a spacer / springy washer) to lower C — counterintuitive, but standard practice.
  • Joints lose 5–10 % of preload in the first hours and cycles to embedment (micro-flattening of the mating surfaces) and gasket creep. Design the margin in, or re-torque after it has settled.
  • Lubricate threads for a consistent nut factor — then lower the torque target. Lube drops K, so the same torque now gives a higher preload; forget to retarget and you yield the bolt.
  • Gaskets are fatigue killers and creepers. A soft gasket means a high C, so the bolt feels the full load, and the gasket relaxes and sheds preload besides. Use spiral-wound or metal gaskets and plan a re-torque pass.
  • A shear joint should carry load by friction, not by the bolt. Clamp hard enough that the faces do not slip (slip-critical); relying on the shank bearing in the hole invites fretting and fatigue.
  • Don’t reuse torque-to-yield bolts. Many modern engine bolts are tightened past yield on purpose, for a consistent preload, and are single-use; reuse them and you get an unknown, degraded preload.

9Build it: an M10 8.8 bracket under a 0–10 kN fluctuating pull

Enough principle — here is a real joint worked end to end, so you can copy the shape and reuse it. The job: a bracket clamped by a single M10 class-8.8 bolt, with an external tensile load that cycles from 0 to 10 kN. Every line below reads straight off the two numbers you already own, Fi and C.

Worked example — an M10 8.8 bolt, 0–10 kN external load
Bolt capacity
M10 8.8: proof strength ≈ 580 MPa, tensile stress area At ≈ 58 mm² → proof load Fp ≈ 580 × 58 ≈ 33.6 kN. Target preload Fi ≈ 0.75·Fp ≈ 25 kN.
Stiffness ratio
Estimate bolt stiffness kb = A·E/L and member stiffness km by the pressure-cone method; a typical metal joint gives km ≈ 4·kb → C = kb/(kb+km) = 1/5 = 0.2.
Bolt load under the 10 kN pull
Fbolt = Fi + C·P = 25 + 0.2×10 = 27 kN. Peak bolt stress = 27 000 / 58 ≈ 466 MPa, safely under proof (580). The bolt gained only 2 kN from a 10 kN external load.
Fatigue
Alternating stress amplitude σa = C·(ΔP/2)/At = 0.2 × 5000 / 58 ≈ 17 MPa. Tiny — that is why it survives. With zero preload the bolt would swing 0→172 MPa every cycle and fatigue out.
Separation check
The joint opens when P > Fi/(1−C) = 25/0.8 = 31 kN — far above the 10 kN load. Big margin; the joint never unclamps.
Tightening
Torque T = K·Fi·d = 0.2 × 25 000 × 0.010 = 50 N·m (nut factor 0.2). But K scatters ±30 %, so the real preload is ~17–33 kN: check the low end still clamps (it does) and the high end stays under yield (it does).

That is the method made concrete: a bolt size, a grade, and a cyclic load hand you the proof load, the target preload, the stiffness ratio, the peak bolt stress, the fatigue amplitude, the separation margin, and — loudly — the fact that your real uncertainty is not the 10 kN load but the ±30 % band on the preload you can actually land with a torque wrench. The plug-in set is small and you already have all of it:

C = kbkb + km,   kb = A·ELload factor, and the bolt stiffness that feeds it
Fbolt = Fi + C·P,   Fclamp = Fi − (1−C)·Pbolt force up a little, clamp force down a lot
Psep = Fi1−C,   σa = C·ΔP/2Atseparation load, and the bolt’s cyclic stress amplitude
Fi ≈ 0.75·Fproof,  Fproof = Sproof·At,  T ≈ K·Fi·dtarget preload, and the torque that (roughly) lands it; K ≈ 0.2 plain, ~0.12–0.16 lubed

Bring it up in this order. The sequence matters; skipping a step just relocates the pain — usually to the moment the joint leaks, loosens, or a bolt lets go after a few thousand cycles:

  1. Set the clamp requirement. From the worst-case external load, pick the preload that keeps the joint closed (a Psep margin) and resists slip or leak — that is your target Fi, not the bolt’s rating.
  2. Pick grade and check proof. Choose the grade so Fi ≈ 0.75·Fproof lands comfortably, then verify the peak bolt stress Fi + C·P stays under proof.
  3. Estimate C and check fatigue. Compute kb and km, get C, and confirm σa = C·(ΔP/2)/At is well inside the bolt’s endurance limit. If not, lengthen or soften the bolt or stiffen the members to drop C.
  4. Choose the tightening method for the accuracy you need. Torque for routine work; torque-angle or stretch/ultrasonic where preload scatter would eat your margin. Set the nut factor by lubricating consistently.
  5. Tighten, then re-torque after settling. Account for the 5–10 % embedment and relaxation loss with a re-torque pass, or design the margin in.
  6. Verify on critical joints. Spot-check the actual preload with an ultrasonic bolt gauge or a stretch measurement; do not trust torque alone where it matters.

The forks that actually change the design. Most parameters you can trim later; these you decide up front:

Torque vs torque-angle vs stretch/ultrasonic
Torque is cheap but ±30 % scatter; torque-angle (snug, then turn a set angle) is tighter; bolt-stretch and ultrasonic are tightest — they measure the tension directly — for critical joints.
Bolt grade (8.8 / 10.9 / 12.9)
A higher grade gives more preload from the same size, but more notch and hydrogen-embrittlement sensitivity and less ductility. Go up in grade before up in size when space is tight.
Tune C: long/slim bolt & washers vs short stiff bolt
To fight fatigue, lower C with a longer, more slender bolt or a compliant (Belleville) element; a short stiff bolt is fine for a static joint but poor under cyclic load.
Gasket & locking choices
Stiff (spiral-wound / metal) gaskets for a low C and less creep; wedge-locking washers, prevailing-torque nuts, or thread locker for vibration — matched to the service, not added by reflex.
The last mile — what you still do on the bench This page gets you the right preload target, a sound stiffness ratio, a bolt grade, and a tightening method — about 90 % of the job. The last 10 % is friction scatter, real stiffness, and settling. The nut factor is empirical and drifts with lube, finish, temperature, and reuse — measure it on your hardware, or sidestep it with angle, stretch, or ultrasonic on critical joints; never treat a handbook K as exact. Member stiffness (the pressure cone) is an estimate; complex or gasketed joints need VDI 2230 or FEA to pin C, and C is half your fatigue answer. Plan for preload loss — embedment and creep shed 5–10 % early, so re-torque or design the margin in, and re-torque gaskets specifically. Verify critical joints with ultrasonic bolt tension or stretch measurement; torque alone is a proxy you cannot fully trust where failure is expensive. Tools to stand on: a calibrated torque wrench with an angle gauge, an ultrasonic bolt-tension meter or stretch micrometer for the ones that matter, VDI 2230 (the bolted-joint calculation standard), and FEA for complex joints. The two-spring idea in Part I never changes; the last mile is landing a real preload you can trust on a real joint that settles.

10The honest last mile — check yourself

Three questions — reason them out from the two-spring model, don’t recall them:

Question 1

A bolt keeps fatigue-failing in a vibrating joint. A junior engineer says “use a stronger, bigger bolt.” Using the preload model, explain why that may not help — and name the two changes that actually would.

Fatigue is driven by the bolt’s cyclic stress σa = C·(ΔP/2)/At and by whether the joint stays closed — not by static strength. A bigger, stiffer bolt can actually raise C (it is now stiffer relative to the members, so it feels a larger share of the swing) and make the fatigue worse. The real fixes are the two numbers that matter: raise the preload — keep the joint closed so C does its job, up to about 75 % of proof — and lower C with a longer or more slender bolt, a spacer or compliant washer, or stiffer members, so the bolt feels less of the swing in the first place.

Question 2

Two identical joints carry the same 0→8 kN cyclic load. One has a thin rubber gasket, the other is bare metal-to-metal. Which bolt sees the bigger cyclic stress and is more fatigue-prone, and why?

The gasketed one. A soft gasket lowers the member stiffness km, which raises C = kb/(kb+km), so the bolt feels a larger share of the external swing — and the cyclic stress σa = C·(ΔP/2)/At grows right along with C. The bare metal joint has stiff members, a small C, and a tiny cyclic bolt stress. Soft gaskets are fatigue killers; worse, they also creep and shed preload over time, so the joint loses clamp just as it is asking the bolt to carry more.

Question 3

You lubricate the threads on a joint but keep the same torque spec that was written for dry threads. Why might you now snap the bolt or crush the joint?

Torque relates to preload only through the nut factor, T ≈ K·Fi·d. Lubrication lowers K (from ~0.2 dry toward ~0.12–0.16), so for the same torque the preload Fi goes up — the same twisting effort now buys far more tension because less of it is being burned as friction. That extra preload can push the bolt past its proof load into yield, or crush the members. Lubed threads need a reduced torque target to hit the same preload the dry spec intended.
