01Foundations

What fine-tuning actually changes

Analogy: a music-school graduate who can play anything (the base model) versus one you've coached for months to play in your band's exact style (the fine-tuned model). Same instrument, different reflexes.

Fine-tuning continues training a model on new data so its weights change — permanently altering how it behaves. This is different from prompting (which changes nothing) and from RAG (which adds knowledge at inference without touching weights). A base model has been pretrained on trillions of tokens to predict the next token; that gives it broad capability but no particular helpfulness, format, or alignment. Fine-tuning — the umbrella term for everything after pretraining, also called post-training — shapes that raw capability into something useful.

The crucial distinction to hold: fine-tuning is best at teaching behavior, format, style, and skills — how to respond — and is a poor and expensive way to inject large, changing facts (that's RAG's job). Teach a model to always answer in your JSON schema, to adopt your brand voice, to follow a domain's reasoning style, or to use your tools correctly: fine-tune. Give it access to a million documents that update weekly: retrieve.

This guide walks the full post-training toolkit: supervised fine-tuning (SFT) to teach behavior from examples, parameter-efficient methods (LoRA/QLoRA) to do it cheaply, preference tuning (RLHF, DPO) to align to human taste, and reinforcement learning (GRPO, RLVR) to grow reasoning — plus the distributed-training systems that make all of it run on one GPU or thousands. It's the craft of turning a general model into your model.

Why it mattersFine-tuning teaches behavior by changing weights — the right tool for style, format and skills, the wrong one for injecting large changing facts (use RAG, per the companion volume).

02Foundations · the map

Pretraining → SFT → preference → RL

Analogy: raising a prodigy: first they read the whole library (pretraining), then a tutor shows them how to answer questions (SFT), then a mentor refines their taste (preference tuning), then they practice hard problems with an answer key (RL).

Modern post-training is a pipeline of stages, each with its own data and technique. (1) Pretraining: predict the next token over trillions of tokens — enormously expensive, done once, produces a "base" model with knowledge and capability but no instinct to be helpful. (2) Supervised fine-tuning (SFT): train on curated (instruction, response) pairs so the model learns to follow instructions and adopt a format — this alone turns a base model into a usable "instruct" model.

(3) Preference optimization: SFT teaches a good answer, but not which of two answers is better; here you align the model to human preferences using RLHF or DPO, so it becomes more helpful, harmless, and on-taste. (4) Reinforcement learning: for reasoning and hard skills, let the model try, get rewarded for correct outcomes (RLVR/GRPO), and improve through trial and error — the stage behind reasoning models like DeepSeek-R1.

Two things to internalize. First, not every model needs every stage — many excellent fine-tunes stop after SFT, or SFT + DPO; the RL stage is for frontier reasoning. Second, the stages build on each other: RL on a model that hasn't been SFT'd is unstable, so the order matters. This pipeline is the spine of the whole guide — each track deepens one stage. 03Foundations

When to fine-tune vs RAG vs prompt

Analogy: fixing a leaky tap: try the washer first (a prompt), then the cartridge (RAG), and only replace the whole fixture (fine-tune) when the cheaper fixes genuinely can't do it.

Fine-tuning is powerful but it is the heaviest tool — it needs data, compute, evaluation, and ongoing maintenance — so reach for it last, after cheaper options fail. Prompting (instructions, few-shot examples) changes nothing and iterates in seconds; try it first, and it solves more than people expect. RAG injects knowledge at inference without training; use it when the problem is the model doesn't know your facts. Fine-tuning is for when the problem is the model doesn't behave the way you need — format, style, a skill, tone, tool-use — that prompting can't reliably enforce.

The sharp test: is your gap knowledge or behavior? Knowledge (facts, documents, current data) → RAG. Behavior (how it responds, consistently) → fine-tune. "The model doesn't know our 2026 pricing" is RAG. "The model won't reliably output our exact ticket schema, even when I ask nicely" is fine-tuning. Many production systems do both: a fine-tuned model for behavior that also uses RAG for knowledge.

Other fine-tune-worthy signals: you need lower latency/cost (a small fine-tuned model can match a big prompted one on a narrow task), consistency at scale (prompting is brittle across millions of calls), or a capability the base lacks (a reasoning style, a niche language). And the honest anti-signal: if your data changes constantly, don't fine-tune it in — you'd retrain forever; retrieve it. Choosing correctly here saves months, because fine-tuning the wrong problem fights the technique.

Why it mattersFine-tuning is the heaviest tool — use it for behavior a prompt can't enforce, not for knowledge (that's RAG). Getting this choice right saves months of fighting the wrong technique.

04Foundations

A training step — what actually happens

Analogy: learning to throw darts: throw (forward), see how far off you were (loss), work out which muscles to adjust (gradients), and nudge them a little (optimizer step) — thousands of times.

Under all the acronyms, training is one loop repeated millions of times. Forward pass: feed a batch of examples through the model to get predictions (for an LLM, next-token probabilities). Loss: compare predictions to the targets with a loss function (cross-entropy for language modeling) — a single number measuring how wrong the model was. Backward pass (backpropagation): compute the gradient — how much each of the billions of weights contributed to the error. Optimizer step: nudge every weight a little in the direction that reduces loss.

The optimizer is where a lot of the memory cost hides. The dominant one, AdamW, keeps two extra numbers per weight (a running mean and variance of gradients — "momentum" and "adaptive scale") plus, in mixed precision, a high-precision master copy — which is why optimizer state can be several times the size of the model itself (Track 08). The learning rate controls how big each nudge is; too big and training diverges, too small and it crawls.

Everything else in fine-tuning is a variation on which weights you update and what loss you use. Full fine-tuning updates all weights; LoRA updates a tiny added set; SFT uses next-token loss on completions; DPO uses a preference loss; RL uses a reward-based objective. Hold this loop — forward, loss, backward, step — and every technique in this guide is a specific choice of loss and of which parameters feel the gradient.

05Foundations

Why full fine-tuning is expensive — and PEFT

Analogy: renovating a house by gutting every room versus adding a few smart fixtures. The gut job needs scaffolding everywhere (memory); the fixtures get 95% of the result for 1% of the disruption.

Full fine-tuning updates every weight, and the memory bill is brutal. For each parameter you must hold: the weight itself, its gradient, and the optimizer state (AdamW's two moments plus a master copy). In mixed precision that's roughly 16–18 bytes per parameter — so a 7B model needs ~120GB and a 70B model over a terabyte of GPU memory just for the training state, before activations. That's many high-end GPUs for even a "small" model, which puts full fine-tuning out of reach for most.

There's a second cost: full fine-tuning produces a whole new copy of the model per task (tens to hundreds of GB to store and serve), and it risks catastrophic forgetting — overwriting the base model's general skills. For narrow adaptation these are steep prices to pay for changing a model that mostly already works.

Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) is the escape: freeze the giant pretrained model and train only a tiny number of new parameters. The star is LoRA (next track), which trains ~0.1–1% of the weights, cuts memory dramatically (no optimizer state for frozen weights), avoids forgetting (the base is untouched), and yields tiny, swappable adapters instead of full model copies. PEFT is what democratized fine-tuning — and it's why a 70B model can be adapted on a single GPU.

06Data & SFT

Supervised fine-tuning and loss masking

Analogy: teaching by worked examples: you show the student the question AND the ideal answer, but you only grade them on the answer — not on their ability to copy back the question.

Supervised fine-tuning (SFT), also called instruction tuning, is the workhorse first stage: train the model on curated (prompt, ideal-response) pairs so it learns to produce responses like your examples. Technically it's the same next-token loss as pretraining, but on structured demonstration data formatted with a chat template (special tokens marking system / user / assistant turns) so the model learns the conversational structure your serving stack will use.

The critical detail most tutorials gloss over is loss masking. A training example contains both the prompt and the completion, but you only want the model to learn to generate the completion, not to reproduce the prompt. So you mask the prompt tokens — set their loss to zero — and compute loss only on the completion tokens. Get this wrong (train on the whole sequence) and the model wastes capacity learning to echo questions, and quality suffers.

SFT is remarkably effective and is where most fine-tuning projects begin and often end. A few thousand high-quality, correctly-formatted, loss-masked examples can transform a base model's behavior on your task. The two things that make or break it are the data (next scenario — quality dominates) and the format (chat template + masking done right). 07Data & SFT · the real work

Data is the product

Analogy: a Michelin kitchen: the technique matters, but the dish lives or dies on ingredient quality. A thousand perfect examples beat a million mediocre ones.

The single biggest lever in fine-tuning is not the method — it's the data. Repeated results (famously the "LIMA" finding — "Less Is More for Alignment") show that a small set of high-quality, diverse examples beats a large noisy one. The model already has the capability from pretraining; SFT is teaching it which behavior to surface, and a few thousand clean, on-target demonstrations do that better than a million scraped ones full of contradictions and junk.

What "quality" means concretely: examples that are correct (no wrong answers to learn from), consistent in format and style (the model imitates patterns, so mixed formats teach mixed behavior), diverse (cover the real distribution of inputs, not ten variations of one case), and free of leakage/duplication (dedup, or the model over-weights repeated examples). Curation — filtering, deduplicating, balancing, and often having strong models or humans grade candidate examples — is where most of the real work goes.

This flips the usual instinct: don't start by collecting as much data as possible; start by making a small, excellent set and grow it deliberately. And build a data flywheel — capture production failures, curate them into new training examples, retrain, repeat — so the dataset improves with use. In fine-tuning, "better data" is almost always a higher-ROI move than "better method." Whoever owns the data pipeline owns the quality.

Why it mattersData quality dominates method — a few thousand clean, consistent, diverse examples beat a million noisy ones, so curation, not collection, is where fine-tuning is won.

08Data & SFT

Synthetic data and distillation

Analogy: an apprentice learning by copying a master's work, then eventually generating original pieces in that style — and even critiquing their own drafts to improve.

High-quality human data is scarce and expensive, so much modern fine-tuning data is synthetic — generated by models. Distillation is the classic form: a strong "teacher" model generates responses (or full reasoning traces) that a smaller "student" is fine-tuned to imitate, transferring capability cheaply. Most small open instruct models are, in part, distilled from larger ones. Self-Instruct-style pipelines have a model generate its own diverse (instruction, response) pairs from a few seeds, bootstrapping a dataset.

Newer self-improvement methods push further. SPIN (Self-Play Fine-Tuning) has the model learn to distinguish its own outputs from human-written ones, improving without new human labels. SPICE grounds self-play in external documents to keep it factual and avoid amplifying the model's own errors (reporting solid gains on reasoning). The frontier is models that generate, critique, and refine their own training data with minimal human input.

The essential caveats: synthetic data inherits the teacher's biases and errors, and naive self-training risks model collapse (quality degrading as a model trains on its own unfiltered output). So synthetic pipelines lean hard on filtering and verification — keep only outputs that pass checks (a verifier, a judge, execution, human spot-checks). Used with discipline, synthetic data is how you get quantity and quality; used carelessly, it quietly poisons the model.

Why it mattersSynthetic data and distillation make quality data scalable — but they inherit the teacher's errors and risk model collapse, so ruthless filtering and verification are what keep them from poisoning the model.

09Data & SFT

Catastrophic forgetting

Analogy: cramming for a new exam so hard you forget last semester's material — you gained the new skill but overwrote the old ones.

When you fine-tune a model on a narrow task, it can catastrophically forget capabilities it had before — general knowledge, other languages, reasoning, safety behavior. The mechanism: gradient updates on your new data move weights toward your task, and nothing preserves the old behavior, so it erodes. You fine-tune for great customer-support replies and discover the model has gotten worse at everything else, or "unlearned" its safety training.

The defenses are practical. LoRA/PEFT is the biggest one: freezing the base and training a small adapter inherently limits how far behavior drifts, so PEFT forgets far less than full fine-tuning — a major reason it's preferred. Replay / data mixing: blend some general-purpose or original-distribution data into your fine-tuning set so the model keeps rehearsing old skills. Lower learning rates and fewer epochs: gentler updates preserve more (and reduce overfitting, next-door problem). Regularization toward the base model (a KL term, as in preference tuning) also helps.

The key mindset is to evaluate for forgetting, not just for the new task. It's easy to celebrate a jump on your target metric while a general benchmark quietly drops — so measure both. Forgetting is the reason "just fine-tune it harder" often backfires, and it's a recurring theme: preference tuning uses a reference-model leash partly to prevent drift, and PEFT is popular partly because it forgets less. Balancing new skill against retained ability is the core tension of fine-tuning.

Why it mattersFine-tuning can silently overwrite a model's general and safety skills — LoRA, data mixing and gentle updates limit it, but the real defense is evaluating for what was forgotten, not just the new task.

10LoRA & PEFT · the star

LoRA — the low-rank trick

Analogy: instead of rewriting a thick manual, you clip a thin sheet of correction notes onto it. The manual stays; your notes tell the reader what to do differently.

LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) is the idea that made fine-tuning cheap, and its trick is elegant. Instead of updating a giant weight matrix W (say d×d), you freeze W and learn a small additive update ΔW, but you constrain ΔW to be low-rank: ΔW = B·A, where B is d×r and A is r×d, with the rank r tiny (8, 16, 32) compared to d (thousands). At inference the layer computes h = Wx + (α/r)·BAx — the frozen path plus the small trained detour, added together.

Why does this work? Research found that the update a model needs for a new task has low intrinsic rank — it lives in a small subspace, so two skinny matrices can express it well. The savings are dramatic: for a 4096×4096 layer, full fine-tuning trains ~16.8M weights; LoRA with r=8 trains 2×4096×8 ≈ 65K — about 0.4%. Across a model that's ~0.1–1% of parameters trained, and since frozen weights need no optimizer state, memory plummets.

The practical wins compound: you train fast on modest hardware, the base model is untouched (so little forgetting), and the result is a tiny adapter file (megabytes, not gigabytes) you can store, share, and hot-swap. A clever initialization — B starts at zero, so ΔW starts at zero — means training begins exactly at the pretrained model and improves from there. LoRA is the default fine-tuning method for most people, and everything in this track builds on it. Watch the two paths add in the animation.

11LoRA & PEFT

Rank and alpha — the two dials

Analogy: the width of your correction notes (rank) and how boldly they're written (alpha). Wider notes hold more corrections; bolder ink makes them count more.

LoRA has two hyperparameters that matter most. Rank r is the width of the bottleneck — the capacity of the adapter. Small r (4–8) is cheap and enough for style, tone, and format tasks; larger r (16–64+) adds capacity for harder skills or bigger behavior shifts, at more memory and slight overfitting risk. Since trainable params scale as 2·d·r, doubling r doubles the adapter size but it's still a tiny fraction of full fine-tuning — play the animation to see the percentage.

Alpha (α) scales the adapter's contribution: the update is multiplied by α/r. Think of it as the "strength" of the detour relative to the frozen weights. A common convention sets α around r or 2r; effectively α/r sets an effective learning rate for the adapter. Tuning r and α together (along with which layers get adapters and the actual learning rate) is most of LoRA hyperparameter tuning.

Two more choices round it out. Target modules: which weight matrices get adapters — commonly the attention projections (q, k, v, o) and often the MLP layers; adapting more modules adds capacity. Dropout on the adapter helps regularize. The good news is LoRA is forgiving: sensible defaults (r=16, α=32, attention+MLP, low LR) work well for most tasks, and you tune from there guided by evaluation. Understanding what each dial does turns guesswork into deliberate choices.

12LoRA & PEFT

Why LoRA works — and merging adapters

Analogy: your correction notes can either stay clipped on (swappable) or be permanently printed into a new edition of the manual (merged) — same reading, different logistics.

The deeper reason LoRA works is intrinsic dimensionality: adapting a pretrained model to a downstream task requires changing far less information than the model's full parameter count suggests — the needed update genuinely lives in a low-dimensional subspace. LoRA doesn't approximate full fine-tuning by luck; it exploits a real property of how large pretrained models adapt. This is why, on many tasks, LoRA matches full fine-tuning quality despite training a fraction of the weights.

At inference you have a choice. Keep the adapter separate: compute Wx + BAx with the adapter loaded alongside the frozen base — this enables swapping many adapters on one base (next scenario) at a tiny per-layer overhead. Or merge: since ΔW = (α/r)BA is just a matrix the same shape as W, you can add it into W once (W′ = W + ΔW) to get a standalone fine-tuned model with zero inference overhead — identical to a fully fine-tuned model in shape and speed.

Merging is great when you want one dedicated model and maximum inference speed; keeping adapters separate is great when you want to serve many fine-tunes cheaply or swap them dynamically. You can also merge multiple adapters or use techniques to combine them, though quality can degrade if they conflict. The mental model: a LoRA adapter is a portable "diff" against the base — apply it live, bake it in, or keep a library of them. That flexibility is a big part of LoRA's power.

Why it mattersLoRA works because task adaptation is genuinely low-rank — and its adapter is a portable diff you can keep separate to swap many, or merge into the weights for zero-overhead serving.

13LoRA & PEFT

DoRA and the PEFT family

Analogy: LoRA is the best-selling tool, but the toolbox has specialized variants — some adjust the direction of a change, some prepend hidden instructions, some scale activations.

LoRA is one member of the broader PEFT family, and several variants refine it. DoRA (Weight-Decomposed LoRA) splits each weight into a magnitude and a direction and applies LoRA to the direction while learning magnitude separately — this tends to match full fine-tuning more closely, especially at low ranks, for a small extra cost. rsLoRA (rank-stabilized) fixes a scaling issue so higher ranks actually help. LoRA+ uses different learning rates for A and B. These are drop-in improvements you reach for when plain LoRA underperforms.

Other PEFT approaches change where the new parameters live. Prefix / prompt tuning prepends a small set of trainable "virtual token" vectors to the input, steering the frozen model without touching its weights — extremely lightweight but generally less expressive than LoRA. (IA)³ learns tiny per-feature scaling vectors, even smaller than LoRA. Adapter layers (the original PEFT idea) insert small trainable bottleneck modules between transformer layers.

The practical landscape: LoRA and QLoRA are the defaults and solve most cases; DoRA is the go-to upgrade when you need a bit more quality; prompt/prefix tuning and (IA)³ are niche ultra-light options. You don't need to memorize them all — know that PEFT is a family, that LoRA-family methods dominate, and that DoRA exists as the "slightly better LoRA" when evaluation says you need it. The unifying idea across all of them is unchanged: freeze the giant model, train something tiny.

Why it mattersPEFT is a family with one idea — freeze the model, train something tiny — where LoRA/QLoRA are the defaults and DoRA is the quality upgrade to reach for when plain LoRA falls short.

14LoRA & PEFT · the serving win

Multi-LoRA — many adapters, one base

Analogy: one grand piano and a rack of interchangeable sheet music. The expensive instrument stays put; you slot in whichever score the moment calls for.

Because a LoRA adapter is tiny and merely adds to a shared frozen base, you can serve many adapters on one base model simultaneously — a capability with no equivalent for fully fine-tuned models (which are full separate copies). The base weights load into GPU memory once; each request specifies which adapter it wants; the server applies that adapter's small B·A on the fly. Dozens or hundreds of custom fine-tunes share one GPU footprint.

This is a major operational win, and the serving stack supports it: S-LoRA pioneered efficient multi-adapter serving (paging adapters between GPU and host memory, batching requests across different adapters), and vLLM and other engines support multi-LoRA in production. The economics are transformative — instead of provisioning a GPU per fine-tuned model, one fleet serves a whole catalog of per-customer, per-task adapters, with the base amortized across all of them.

It ties directly back to the LLMOps serving volume: multi-LoRA is where fine-tuning meets serving. Design your fine-tuning around it — keep adapters unmerged, version them, and let the gateway route a request to the right adapter — and you can offer per-tenant customization at almost no marginal serving cost. 15LoRA & PEFT

Full vs LoRA vs QLoRA — the decision

Analogy: three ways to tailor a suit: bespoke from scratch (full), expert alterations (LoRA), or expert alterations done in a compact home studio (QLoRA). Most people should book the alterations.

The everyday decision. Full fine-tuning: updates all weights, maximum capacity to change the model, but needs many GPUs and risks forgetting — justified for large behavior shifts, continued pretraining, or when you have the compute and LoRA has been shown to leave quality on the table. LoRA: trains a small adapter on the full-precision base — near-full quality on most tasks, a fraction of the memory, swappable adapters, little forgetting. QLoRA (next track): LoRA on top of a 4-bit quantized base — the cheapest, fitting even a 65B model on a single GPU, at a tiny quality cost.

The default recommendation for most teams and tasks is start with LoRA or QLoRA, and only reach for full fine-tuning if evaluation proves you need it. QLoRA when memory is the binding constraint (one GPU, big model); LoRA when you have the memory and want to avoid even QLoRA's small quantization overhead; full when the task is a large enough shift that low-rank genuinely can't capture it (rare for narrow adaptation, more common for broad continued training).

The economics decide it more often than quality: LoRA/QLoRA are so much cheaper, faster to iterate, and easier to serve (adapters) that they win unless there's a measured reason to spend on full fine-tuning. And the choice isn't permanent — prototype with QLoRA, and if results are promising but not quite there, step up to LoRA or full. Match the method to the constraint (memory, quality, serving), measure, and don't pay for capacity you don't need.

Track 04 — Fine-tuning on a budget

Quantized training

How 4-bit quantization put large-model fine-tuning on a single GPU. QLoRA and its on-the-fly dequantization, the three tricks (NF4, double quantization, paged optimizers) that make it near-lossless, the precision choices across the training stack, and the accuracy cost you must always measure.

16Quantized training · the unlock

QLoRA — fine-tune a 4-bit model

Analogy: renovating a house while it's shrink-wrapped to save space — the structure is compressed and untouched, and you only build the small new additions in full detail.

QLoRA combined two ideas into the single biggest accessibility unlock in fine-tuning: quantize the frozen base model to 4-bit to shrink its memory ~4×, then train LoRA adapters in higher precision (bf16) on top. The base is frozen anyway, so storing it in 4-bit costs almost nothing in quality — and because only the tiny adapters are trained (and only they need gradients and optimizer state), a model that would need a cluster now fits on one GPU. QLoRA famously fine-tuned a 65B model on a single 48GB GPU.

The mechanism: the 4-bit base weights are dequantized on the fly to bf16 for each layer's forward/backward compute, then discarded — so you get 4-bit storage with near-full-precision math, and gradients flow only into the bf16 LoRA adapters. The base never leaves 4-bit in memory; the adapters carry all the learning. Toggle the animation between "what trains" and "memory footprint" to see both halves.

QLoRA's impact was democratization: fine-tuning large models went from "needs a GPU cluster" to "runs on a workstation or a single cloud GPU," putting it in reach of individuals and small teams. It does add a small overhead (on-the-fly dequantization is compute) and a small potential quality cost versus full-precision LoRA, but for most tasks the trade is overwhelmingly worth it. The three tricks that make the 4-bit part work well — NF4, double quantization, and paged optimizers — are the next scenario.

17Quantized training

NF4, double quantization, paged optimizers

Analogy: three space-saving tricks in one carry-on: a smarter way to fold (NF4), compressing the folding instructions too (double quant), and an overflow bag that only fills when needed (paged optimizer).

QLoRA's 4-bit quality rests on three specific innovations. NF4 (4-bit NormalFloat): a data type whose 16 possible values are placed to match the normal distribution that neural-network weights actually follow — so the limited 4-bit codes land where the weights actually are, preserving far more information than naive uniform 4-bit. This is why 4-bit base weights barely hurt quality.

Double quantization: quantization itself needs metadata (scaling constants per block of weights), and at 4-bit that metadata isn't negligible — so QLoRA quantizes the quantization constants too, squeezing out another ~0.4 bits per parameter of overhead. Small, but at billions of parameters it adds up to real GPU memory saved. Paged optimizers: to survive memory spikes (long sequences can momentarily blow the budget), optimizer states are paged between GPU and CPU memory automatically — like OS virtual memory — so a transient spike doesn't crash the run with an out-of-memory error.

Together these turn "4-bit fine-tuning" from a lossy hack into a reliable, near-lossless method. You rarely touch them directly — libraries (bitsandbytes, and the frameworks) implement them — but knowing what they do explains why QLoRA works: NF4 preserves weight information, double quant trims the overhead, and paged optimizers keep the run stable. It's a case study in how careful systems engineering, not just algorithms, made a capability practical.

18Quantized training

Precision in training — FP32, BF16, FP8

Analogy: measuring ingredients: grams for the delicate optimizer chemistry (FP32), a coarser scale for the bulk work (BF16), and an ultra-fast rough scale where you can get away with it (FP8).

Training uses several numeric precisions deliberately. FP32 (32-bit) is the safe, precise default but slow and memory-heavy. BF16 (brain float 16) is the modern workhorse: half the memory and much faster on modern GPUs, with the same exponent range as FP32 (so it doesn't overflow like the older FP16), trading away mantissa precision that training mostly tolerates. Mixed precision is the standard recipe: do the heavy matrix math in BF16 for speed, but keep a master copy of weights and the optimizer states in FP32 for numerical stability during the tiny weight updates.

That master-copy detail is exactly why optimizer memory is so large (Track 08): AdamW in mixed precision holds FP32 master weights plus two FP32 moment estimates — the "extra copies" that dominate the training budget. The precision you compute in and the precision you store the update state in are different choices, made for speed and stability respectively.

The frontier is FP8 (and even FP4) training on Hopper/Blackwell GPUs: doing more of the compute in 8-bit for large throughput gains, with careful scaling to preserve stability — increasingly used for large-scale pretraining and now reaching fine-tuning. The practical takeaway: BF16 mixed precision is the default you'll use; FP32 for stability-critical state; FP8 when you're squeezing maximum throughput on new hardware and can manage its finicky scaling. Precision is a knob you set knowingly, not a detail to ignore.

Why it mattersMixed precision computes in fast BF16 but keeps FP32 master weights and optimizer state for stability — that FP32 state is exactly why training memory dwarfs the model, and FP8 is the next throughput frontier.

19Quantized training

The accuracy cost of quantized training

Analogy: a compressed photo: for most uses it's indistinguishable, but zoom into fine detail and the artifacts show. You compress until someone can tell — then back off.

QLoRA is close to full-precision LoRA on many tasks, but "close" is not "identical," and the gap is task-dependent. 4-bit quantization introduces small errors in the frozen base, and while NF4 minimizes them, they can matter for precision-sensitive tasks — exact math, code, tasks needing the model's full nuanced knowledge, or when you're pushing for the last few points of quality. The honest stance: treat QLoRA's equivalence to full-precision as a hypothesis to test on your task, not a guarantee.

The failure isn't usually dramatic — it's a quiet few-percent quality gap that's easy to miss if you only eyeball a few examples. This is why evaluation (Track 09) is non-negotiable for quantized training: measure your fine-tune on your real metric, and if QLoRA underperforms, try full-precision LoRA (drop the base quantization) or full fine-tuning before concluding the approach doesn't work. Often the fix is more/better data or higher rank, not more precision — but sometimes precision is the culprit.

The broader principle, shared with the LLMOps quantization scenario: quantization is the one optimization you must always re-evaluate, because it trades a resource you can measure (memory) for a quality change you might not notice. Used with evaluation, QLoRA is a superb default; used blindly, it can leave quality on the table invisibly. Compress aggressively, but verify — the whole discipline of good fine-tuning is measuring what your shortcuts cost.

20Preference tuning

Why SFT isn't enough — alignment

Analogy: SFT teaches a student to write an essay; alignment teaches them which of two essays is better — and to prefer the helpful, honest, harmless one every time.

SFT teaches the model to imitate good responses, but it has a ceiling. It can only learn from the demonstrations you give, it treats every example as equally "the right answer," and it has no way to learn preferences — that response A is better than response B when both are plausible. It also struggles to teach what not to do. The result after SFT alone is a capable instruction-follower that may still be unhelpful, verbose, evasive, or unsafe in ways demonstrations didn't cover.

Preference tuning (a.k.a. alignment, RLHF-style training) fixes this by learning from comparisons rather than demonstrations. Instead of "here's the right answer," the signal is "here are two answers, humans preferred this one." This is both easier to collect at scale (judging is easier than writing) and far richer — it teaches the model a gradient of quality, nudging it toward the better response and away from the worse, across dimensions demonstrations can't easily express (tone, helpfulness, safety, honesty).

This is the stage that produced the leap from raw instruct models to genuinely helpful assistants — the "HHH" goals (helpful, harmless, honest) are preference-tuning targets. The classic method is RLHF (reward model + PPO), the modern default is DPO (direct, no RL loop), and the family has grown (KTO, ORPO, SimPO). This track covers each, plus the ever-present danger of reward hacking. Preference tuning is where a model learns not just to answer, but to answer well.

Why it mattersSFT teaches a good answer; preference tuning teaches which of two answers is better — learning from comparisons is what turned capable instruct models into genuinely helpful, aligned assistants.

21Preference tuning · the classic

RLHF — reward model plus PPO

Analogy: training a chef by first hiring a food critic who has tasted thousands of dishes (the reward model), then having the chef cook, get scored, and adjust — with a rule that they can't stray too far from their proven repertoire.

Classic RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback) has two phases. First, train a reward model: collect human preference pairs (for prompt X, response A preferred over B), and train a model to predict a scalar "how good is this response" score that agrees with humans. Second, use PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization) to fine-tune the language model (the "policy") to maximize that reward — it generates responses, the reward model scores them, and PPO updates the policy toward higher-scoring behavior.

The subtlety that makes it work — and makes it heavy — is that PPO juggles four models in memory: the policy being trained, a frozen reference copy (to measure and penalize drift via a KL term), the reward model, and a critic/value model (to estimate a baseline for stable updates). PPO's clipped objective limits how far each update moves, and the KL penalty to the reference keeps the model from drifting into gibberish that games the reward. Step through the four phases in the animation.

RLHF is powerful and produced the first great aligned assistants, but its cost is real: four models resident, a delicate RL training loop that's notoriously finicky to stabilize, and an expensive reward-model-training step. That complexity is exactly what motivated the simpler methods — DPO removes the reward model and RL loop, GRPO removes the critic. Understanding the four-model dance is what makes those simplifications make sense: each later method drops one of these pieces.

22Preference tuning · the modern default

DPO — skip the reward model

Analogy: instead of hiring a critic and then cooking to impress them, you just study thousands of "this dish beat that dish" verdicts and directly adjust your cooking — no critic on payroll, no trial dinners.

DPO (Direct Preference Optimization) was a breakthrough: it gets RLHF's benefit without a separate reward model or an RL loop. The insight is mathematical — the RLHF objective (maximize reward while staying near the reference) has a closed-form optimal solution that relates the ideal policy directly to the preference data. DPO rearranges this so you can train the policy directly on preference pairs with a simple classification-style loss: increase the probability of the chosen response and decrease the probability of the rejected one, relative to the frozen reference.

The practical result is dramatically simpler: only two models in memory (the policy and the frozen reference — no reward model, no critic), a stable supervised-style training loop instead of finicky RL, and no separate reward-model-training phase. You need the same preference-pair data RLHF needs, but you consume it directly. For most teams, DPO delivers RLHF-quality alignment at a fraction of the complexity and compute — which is why it became the default open-source alignment method.

The reference model still matters: it anchors the policy (the loss is about relative probability shifts versus the reference), playing the KL-leash role that keeps DPO from over-drifting. DPO isn't strictly identical to RLHF — there are tasks where online RL (generating fresh samples and scoring them, as PPO/GRPO do) still wins — but as a first choice for alignment, DPO's simplicity-to-quality ratio is hard to beat. 23Preference tuning

The DPO family — KTO, ORPO, SimPO

Analogy: variations on a recipe: one works from thumbs-up/down instead of A-vs-B (KTO), one bakes the whole cake in a single step (ORPO), one throws out an ingredient it decides is unnecessary (SimPO).

DPO spawned a family, each fixing a practical limitation. KTO (Kahneman-Tversky Optimization) drops the need for paired preferences — it learns from unpaired binary feedback ("this response was good" / "this was bad"), which is far easier and cheaper to collect at scale (thumbs up/down from production) than curated A-vs-B pairs. When you have abundant binary signals rather than clean comparisons, KTO fits your data.

ORPO (Odds Ratio Preference Optimization) merges SFT and preference tuning into a single stage with one objective, and notably needs no reference model — removing both a training stage and a model from memory. It's attractive when you want to go from a base model to an aligned one in one pass, avoiding the SFT-then-DPO two-step and its distribution shift. SimPO (Simple Preference Optimization) also drops the reference model, using the average log-probability of a sequence as an implicit, length-normalized reward — simpler still, and it addresses a length-bias issue where models learn to just write longer.

The practical guidance: DPO remains the default; reach for KTO when your data is binary/unpaired, ORPO when you want a single-stage pipeline without a reference model, SimPO when you want reference-free simplicity and length control. They're mostly drop-in swaps in the same frameworks (TRL supports all), so you can try the one that matches your data and constraints. The meta-point: preference optimization is now a toolkit of simple, RL-free methods — pick by your data shape and pipeline, not by prestige.

24Preference tuning · the danger

Reward hacking and the KL leash

Analogy: a student who learns to game the grading rubric — padding essays with keywords the grader loves — scoring higher while actually writing worse. The reward went up; the goal was missed.

The deepest hazard in preference tuning: the reward signal is a proxy for what you want, not the thing itself, and models are ruthless proxy-optimizers. Reward hacking (a.k.a. over-optimization, Goodhart's law) is when the model finds ways to score high on the reward model or metric while getting worse at the actual goal — becoming sycophantic (the reward model likes agreeable answers), verbose (longer looks more thorough), or exploiting quirks the reward model rewards for the wrong reasons. Reward goes up; true quality goes down.

The primary defense is the KL penalty / reference-model leash: penalize the policy for drifting too far from the reference (SFT) model, so it can improve on the reward but can't run off into degenerate territory to game it. The KL coefficient is a dial — too loose and the model hacks the reward (quality collapses); too tight and it can barely improve (under-fitting). Finding the balance is central to stable RLHF, and the same leash lives in DPO (via the reference anchor). Slide the KL penalty in the animation to watch true quality diverge from measured reward.

Other defenses: better and more robust reward models (ensembles, updating them as the policy changes to prevent it exploiting stale weaknesses), early stopping before over-optimization sets in, and — the big structural fix — verifiable rewards (next track) that can't be hacked because they check correctness directly. Reward hacking is why alignment is subtle: you're not just maximizing a number, you're maximizing a number without letting the model exploit the gap between the number and the goal. Respecting that gap is the craft.

25RL post-training · the shift

GRPO — reinforcement learning without a critic

Analogy: grading a student not against an examiner's secret rubric, but against how their classmates did on the same question — the class average IS the baseline, so you need no separate examiner.

GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization), introduced by DeepSeek, is the RL method that reshaped post-training by dropping the critic model entirely. Recall PPO needs a value/critic network to estimate a baseline (how good an average response is) so it knows whether a given response was better or worse than expected. That critic is a whole extra model to train and hold in memory. GRPO's insight: for a given prompt, sample a group of G responses, and use the group's mean reward as the baseline. No critic needed.

Each response's advantage is computed group-relatively: (its reward − the group mean) ÷ the group's standard deviation. Responses that beat the group average get reinforced; those below it get suppressed — a simple, self-normalizing signal. The KL penalty to a reference model still leashes drift (often placed directly in the loss). The result matches PPO's learning while cutting a model from memory, making RL post-training far more accessible.

GRPO is the engine behind DeepSeek-R1 and the wave of open reasoning models, precisely because it made RL cheap enough to run at scale on reasoning tasks. It pairs naturally with verifiable rewards (next scenario): sample G solutions to a math problem, check each, and the ones that got it right are pushed up relative to the group. Understanding "the group mean replaces the critic" is understanding why modern RL post-training exploded. Sample a new group in the animation to watch above- and below-average responses sorted.

26RL post-training · the unlock

RLVR — verifiable rewards for reasoning

Analogy: the difference between an essay graded by a subjective panel and a math problem graded by an answer key. For anything checkable, you don't need judges — and you can't bribe an answer key.

RLVR (Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards) is the idea that, for domains where correctness is checkable — math (does the answer match?), code (do the unit tests pass?), formal proofs, structured tasks — you replace the learned, hackable reward model with an automated verifier that returns a clean binary reward. No human preferences, no reward model, no reward hacking: the verifier is ground truth. Combine RLVR with GRPO and you have the recipe behind the reasoning-model revolution.

The stunning result DeepSeek-R1 demonstrated: training a base model with pure RLVR (correct-answer rewards on math/code, via GRPO) causes reasoning to emerge on its own — the model learns to produce long chains of thought, check its own work, backtrack, and try alternative strategies, without ever being shown labeled reasoning traces. The reward only says "right or wrong"; the how (step-by-step reasoning) is discovered by the model because reasoning is what earns the reward. This was a genuine surprise and a paradigm shift.

RLVR's two big advantages over preference-based RL: rewards are cheap and scalable (a verifier runs for free over millions of problems, no human labeling) and un-hackable (you can't sycophant your way past a unit test). Its limit is scope — it needs verifiable tasks, so it shines on math, code, and logic and is harder to apply to open-ended writing or subjective quality (where you still need preferences/judges). 27RL post-training

DAPO and the GRPO successors

Analogy: GRPO worked, then the community found its rough edges — and a wave of patches (DAPO, Dr.GRPO, GSPO) each fixed one, the way a new engine gets refined lap after lap.

GRPO opened the floodgates, and 2026 brought a rapid series of refinements addressing its instabilities, especially for very long chain-of-thought training. DAPO is the notable open recipe, bundling four fixes: clip-higher (asymmetric clipping that prevents "entropy collapse," where the model becomes over-confident and stops exploring), dynamic sampling (filter out prompts where all sampled responses are all-right or all-wrong, since they give zero learning signal), token-level policy-gradient loss (so long responses don't get their gradient diluted), and overlong reward shaping (penalize runaway-length outputs).

The payoff is concrete: DAPO trained Qwen2.5-32B to strong AIME-2024 math scores while using roughly half the training steps of the earlier R1-Zero approach — efficiency and stability gains that matter at RL's cost. Other variants (Dr.GRPO fixing length/difficulty biases in the advantage normalization, GSPO operating at sequence level, and more) each target a specific pathology of naive GRPO.

You don't need to track every acronym, but the pattern is important: RL post-training is a fast-moving, empirical area where the base algorithm (policy-gradient with a group baseline) is stable but the details — how you clip, sample, normalize, and shape rewards — are actively being tuned for stability and efficiency on long-reasoning tasks. The frameworks (veRL, TRL, OpenRLHF) implement these recipes, so you consume them as options. The takeaway: GRPO was the breakthrough; DAPO and kin are how it's being made reliable.

28RL post-training

The reasoning-model recipe

Analogy: training a champion: first teach the fundamentals cleanly (a cold-start), then let them grind thousands of real problems with an answer key (RL), then package their hard-won skill into a smaller, faster protege (distill).

The modern reasoning-model recipe assembles this whole guide's pieces into a pipeline, roughly as DeepSeek-R1 showed. (1) Cold-start SFT: fine-tune the base model on a small set of high-quality reasoning examples to establish good format and a readable chain-of-thought (pure RL from scratch works but produces messy, mixed-language reasoning; a cold-start cleans it up). (2) RLVR with GRPO/DAPO: run large-scale RL on verifiable math/code/logic problems, where reasoning ability is discovered and sharpened through trial-and-error against verifiers.

(3) Rejection sampling + SFT: use the RL-improved model to generate many solutions, keep the correct/best ones (rejection sampling), and fine-tune on them to consolidate the gains and broaden coverage. (4) A final alignment pass (preference tuning) for helpfulness and safety on general tasks. And crucially, (5) distillation: the big reasoning model's traces are used to fine-tune smaller models, transferring much of the reasoning ability cheaply — which is why small open "R1-distill" models punch far above their size.

The profound lesson from this recipe is that reasoning is trainable, not just a byproduct of scale — and that a combination of SFT for format, RL for capability, and distillation for efficiency is a repeatable path to it. Every stage draws on earlier tracks (SFT, LoRA optional, GRPO, RLVR, distillation, data curation). It's the current frontier of post-training, and it turned "reasoning models" from a frontier-lab secret into an open, reproducible pipeline in months.

29RL post-training · the frontier

Agentic and tool-use RL

Analogy: teaching not just to answer, but to act — a trainee who learns, over thousands of simulated shifts, when to look something up, call a tool, or stop and ask.

The newest frontier extends RL from single answers to multi-turn agentic behavior: training models to use tools, search, run code, and take actions across many steps to accomplish a task — and rewarding the outcome (did the task succeed?) rather than any single response. This is how you teach an agent to reason about when to call which tool, recover from errors, and chain steps, rather than just imitating tool-use demonstrations.

It's hard because the environment is now part of training: the model generates an action, the environment (a code sandbox, a browser, a search API, a simulator) responds, and this loops for many turns before a reward arrives — a long-horizon credit-assignment problem. New open frameworks and environments (NeMo Gym, RLFactory, verifiers/agentic-RL toolkits, and others) provide the interactive environments and generate the huge numbers of rollouts this needs, and safety-focused work (e.g. training agents to refuse harmful tasks) is emerging alongside.

This closes the loop with the agentic and LLMOps volumes: RLVR-style verifiable rewards extend naturally to agents (did the code run? did the task complete?), and GRPO-style group sampling scales the rollouts. Agentic RL is early and compute-hungry, but it's where a lot of frontier effort is going, because the value of models that reliably act (not just answer) is enormous. It's the current edge of post-training — and a sign that "fine-tuning" increasingly means "teaching a model to do a job," not just to talk.

30Distributed training

One GPU isn't enough — the memory wall

Analogy: one desk can't hold a library. You either give each helper the same books and split the reading (data parallel), or split the library itself across desks (model parallel).

Beyond PEFT on small models, real training hits a memory wall: the training state (weights + gradients + optimizer, Track 08) exceeds one GPU's memory. The first and simplest scaling is data parallelism (DP): put a full copy of the model on each GPU, give each a different slice of the batch, and all-reduce the gradients across GPUs each step so they stay in sync. DP multiplies throughput linearly-ish and is the backbone of most training — but it doesn't help if the model doesn't fit on one GPU, since every GPU still holds the whole thing.

When the model itself is too big, you must shard it, and there are two families. Shard the redundant state (ZeRO/FSDP, next scenario): DP where the model, gradients, and optimizer are split across GPUs and reassembled on demand — keeps DP's simplicity while removing its redundancy. Shard the model structurally (tensor and pipeline parallelism, Track 07 scenario after): split individual layers or the layer stack across GPUs — necessary for the largest models.

The art of large-scale training is combining these to fit the model and maximize the fraction of time GPUs spend computing (not communicating or idling). Communication is the enemy: every sharding scheme trades memory for extra GPU-to-GPU traffic, so you place the chatty parts on fast links (NVLink within a node) and the quieter parts across slower ones (InfiniBand between nodes). Understanding what each parallelism shards and what it costs in communication is the key to the next few scenarios.

ZeRO and FSDP — shard the training state

Analogy: four movers who each carry a quarter of the furniture, and pass a piece to whoever needs it right now — nobody carries the whole house, but the whole house is always available.

ZeRO (Zero Redundancy Optimizer, from DeepSpeed) and its PyTorch equivalent FSDP (Fully Sharded Data Parallel) are the workhorses of large-scale training. The insight: in plain data parallelism, every GPU redundantly stores the same full copy of weights, gradients, and optimizer states — enormous waste. ZeRO shards these across GPUs so each holds only 1/N, then gathers the pieces just-in-time when a layer needs them.

It comes in three stages of increasing savings and communication. ZeRO-1: shard the optimizer states only (the biggest chunk) — ~4× memory reduction on many GPUs, minimal extra comms. ZeRO-2: also shard the gradients — ~8×, moderate comms (reduce-scatter on the backward pass). ZeRO-3 / FSDP: also shard the parameters themselves — each GPU holds 1/N of everything, and layers are all-gathered on demand for the forward and backward pass, then released. ZeRO-3 gives the largest savings at the most communication. Slide the stage in the animation to watch each row of state get sharded.

The beauty is that this is still data parallelism logically — each GPU processes different data — so it's simpler to reason about than model parallelism, and it composes with everything else. CPU/NVMe offloading extends it further (park optimizer states in host RAM). For a huge fraction of real fine-tuning and training, ZeRO-3/FSDP is the answer to "the model doesn't fit": shard the redundant state, gather on demand, and a model that needed a cluster now spreads cleanly across your GPUs.

Tensor, pipeline and sequence parallelism

Analogy: an assembly line for building an engine: many workers on one component at once (tensor), stations each adding a section (pipeline), and splitting a very long part across benches (sequence).

For the largest models, sharding the redundant state isn't enough — you split the model's computation across GPUs. Tensor parallelism (TP): split each layer's weight matrices across GPUs so they compute one layer together, exchanging partial results via all-reduce every layer. It's communication-heavy, so TP is kept within a node on fast NVLink (typical degree 4–8). Pipeline parallelism (PP): cut the layer stack into consecutive stages, one group of layers per GPU; micro-batches flow through like an assembly line. PP communicates less (only activations at stage boundaries), so it spans across nodes.

PP's cost is pipeline bubbles — idle time while stages wait for the first micro-batch to propagate; interleaved schedules (Megatron's 1F1B) shrink the bubble. Sequence / context parallelism (CP): split the sequence dimension across GPUs so each handles part of a long sequence — essential for long-context training, where attention activations dominate memory. Each parallelism shards a different axis: TP splits within layers, PP splits across layers, CP splits within the sequence, DP splits across the batch.

These are provided by Megatron-LM/Megatron-Core (the reference for TP/PP/CP) and increasingly in PyTorch/DeepSpeed. The recipe fleets converge on: TP inside a node (NVLink handles its chatter), PP across nodes (InfiniBand carries the lighter stage-boundary traffic), DP for throughput, and ZeRO layered on top — combined to fit the model and keep GPUs busy. The training-memory budget

Analogy: packing for an expedition: the tent (weights) is the obvious item, but the food, fuel, and safety gear (optimizer state) weigh several times more — and that's what actually fills the truck.

Here are the numbers that govern every scaling decision, for a model with P parameters in mixed-precision AdamW training. Weights (bf16): 2 bytes/param. Gradients (bf16): 2 bytes/param. Optimizer states (AdamW, fp32): a master weight copy (4) + first moment (4) + second moment (4) = 12 bytes/param. That's ~16 bytes/param before activations — and the optimizer alone is 6× the bf16 weights. For a 70B model: ~140GB weights + ~140GB gradients + ~840GB optimizer ≈ 1.1 TB of state, versus 80GB on a single H100.

On top sit activations — the intermediate values from the forward pass that backprop needs — which scale with batch size, sequence length, and model size, and can rival or exceed the weights for long sequences (that's what activation checkpointing and sequence parallelism attack). The headline insight is counterintuitive: the model weights are the small part; the optimizer state and activations dominate the training-memory budget. This is why "the model is 70B, it should fit in 140GB" is wrong by nearly an order of magnitude.

These numbers explain everything in this track. ZeRO shards exactly the first three (weights, gradients, optimizer) — so ZeRO-3 across 16 GPUs turns 1.1TB into ~70GB/GPU of state. LoRA/QLoRA sidestep most of it by freezing weights (no gradient or optimizer state for them). Activation checkpointing (next track) attacks the activation term. Do the memory math first — count bytes per parameter, multiply, compare to your GPUs — and the right combination of techniques becomes obvious. Slide model size and sharding in the animation.

35Memory & systems

Where the memory actually goes

Analogy: a moving truck where the sofa (the model) takes up less room than the mountain of packing material and supplies (the optimizer) wrapped around it.

It bears repeating as its own idea, because it's the most counterintuitive fact in training: the model weights are usually the smallest part of the memory bill. The four consumers, in typical order of size: optimizer states (AdamW: 3 fp32 values per param = 12 bytes, the biggest), activations (forward-pass intermediates saved for backprop, batch- and sequence-dependent), gradients (2 bytes/param), and finally the weights (2 bytes/param). The optimizer and activations dominate; the weights bring up the rear.

Why AdamW is so hungry: it keeps a running first moment (momentum) and second moment (adaptive per-parameter scaling) for every weight, plus a high-precision master copy for stable updates — three extra full-size arrays. This is a deliberate trade: those extra states are why Adam trains language models so well (adaptive, stable), and the memory cost is the price. Alternatives like 8-bit Adam (quantize the states), Adafactor (factorize the second moment), or newer memory-light optimizers cut this at some quality/stability risk.

Knowing the ranking tells you which lever to pull. Optimizer too big? Shard it (ZeRO-1), offload it to CPU, or freeze most weights (LoRA — frozen weights have no optimizer state, which is the deepest reason LoRA saves so much). Activations too big? Checkpoint them (next scenario) or use sequence parallelism. Gradients too big? ZeRO-2. The whole discipline of fitting a training run is a targeted attack on whichever of these four is blowing your budget — and it's almost never the weights.

Why it mattersThe weights are the smallest part of the training bill — optimizer states and activations dominate, so fitting a run means attacking those, which is exactly why freezing weights (LoRA) saves so much.

Activation checkpointing

Analogy: solving a long maze: instead of remembering every step, you drop a breadcrumb every tenth turn. To backtrack, you re-walk from the last breadcrumb — a little extra walking to carry far less in your head.

The forward pass produces activations at every layer, and the backward pass needs them to compute gradients — so by default you store all of them, which for deep models and long sequences is a huge, often dominant, chunk of memory. Activation checkpointing (gradient checkpointing) trades compute for memory: instead of saving every layer's activations, save only a few checkpoints, discard the rest, and recompute the discarded ones on the fly during the backward pass by re-running that segment of the forward.

The trade is quantified: roughly ~30% extra compute (you redo part of the forward pass) to cut activation memory by a large factor (often to O(√N) of layers, or user-chosen). For many runs this is the difference between fitting and an out-of-memory crash, or between a batch size of 1 and a useful batch size. It's a standard, one-flag technique in every framework, and it composes with everything else.

An important nuance: checkpointing attacks only the activation term — it does nothing for optimizer states, gradients, or weights (those you shard or offload instead). So it's one specific tool for one specific consumer. The mental model from the previous scenario pays off here: identify that activations are your bottleneck (long sequences, big batches), then reach for checkpointing (and sequence parallelism); if the optimizer is the bottleneck, checkpointing won't help and you shard instead. Gradient accumulation and effective batch size

Analogy: carrying a heavy load up the stairs in several small trips, then counting it as one big delivery — you get the effect of a large batch without a truck big enough to hold it.

Large batch sizes stabilize and speed training, but a big batch needs proportionally more memory (more activations at once) — and often you simply can't fit the batch size you want. Gradient accumulation is the fix: run several micro-batches forward and backward without updating the weights, summing their gradients, and only after N micro-batches do a single optimizer step. The model sees N× the data per update, so the effective batch size is (micro-batch × N × number of GPUs) — a large batch simulated on small memory.

This decouples the batch size you want (for training dynamics) from the batch size that fits (for memory). Want an effective batch of 256 but only 16 fits per GPU on 4 GPUs? Accumulate over 4 micro-batches (16 × 4 × 4 = 256). The cost is time — more forward/backward passes per update — but no extra memory, and the training behaves (almost) as if you had the big batch.

It's a staple of real training configs, and it interacts with other knobs: the learning rate is usually tuned to the effective batch size, and gradient accumulation composes with data parallelism (which contributes its own factor to the effective batch). Alongside checkpointing (memory for compute) and sharding (memory across GPUs), gradient accumulation (batch across steps) completes the toolkit for making a desired training configuration fit on the hardware you actually have. These three — checkpoint, shard, accumulate — are the everyday levers.

Throughput and the cost of a fine-tune

Analogy: running a kitchen: it's not just whether you can cook the dish, but how many you plate per hour and what the gas bill comes to — the economics decide what's worth doing.

Once a run fits, the questions become how fast and how much. Throughput is measured in tokens/second and summarized by MFU (Model FLOPs Utilization — the fraction of the GPUs' theoretical peak you actually achieve); good large-scale runs reach 40–50% MFU, and low MFU means you're bottlenecked on communication, data loading, or bubbles rather than compute. Improving MFU (better parallelism, overlap, bigger batches) directly cuts time and cost.

The cost math is concrete and often surprisingly cheap for fine-tuning. A QLoRA fine-tune of a 7–13B model on a few thousand examples can run in an hour or two on a single GPU for a few dollars. Even a full fine-tune of a 70B model on ~1B tokens is on the order of ~30 hours on 8 H100s — a few hundred dollars on-demand, less on spot. Pretraining is the expensive world (thousands of GPUs, weeks, millions of dollars); fine-tuning is usually accessible, which is the whole point of PEFT.

Practical cost levers: spot/preemptible GPUs (cheap but interruptible — checkpoint often so you can resume), right-sizing the model and method (QLoRA a 13B often beats full fine-tuning a 70B on cost-per-quality), efficient frameworks (Unsloth's kernel optimizations can 2× throughput and halve memory), and stopping early once evaluation plateaus. The discipline mirrors the LLMOps FinOps mindset: measure tokens/sec and dollars, know your MFU, and spend on quality that evaluation shows you're actually buying — not on compute for its own sake.

Evaluating a fine-tune

Analogy: a fitness test after training: did they get stronger at the target lift (learned), can they still run (didn't forget), and are they actually fit or just memorizing the test (overfitting)?

Fine-tuning without evaluation is flying blind, and you must check three things, not one. (1) Did it learn? Measure on your target task with a held-out test set and your real metric (accuracy, format compliance, a task-specific score, LLM-as-judge for quality). (2) Did it forget? Run general benchmarks and adjacent capabilities to catch catastrophic forgetting (Track 02) — it's easy to celebrate a target-task gain while general ability quietly drops. (3) Is it overfitting? Watch the gap between training and validation loss; a model that memorized your training set will ace it and fail on new inputs.

The tooling reuses the evaluation stack from the RAG and LLMOps volumes: lm-evaluation-harness for standard benchmarks, task-specific eval sets you build (a golden set of held-out examples), LLM-as-judge for fuzzy quality, and side-by-side comparison against the base model and previous checkpoints. Evaluate every checkpoint, not just the final one — the best model is often not the last (training can overfit or degrade past a point), so you pick the checkpoint that maximizes validation quality.

Common failure signals to watch: format collapse (the model over-learns one output shape and loses flexibility), overfitting (great on train, poor on val), reward over-optimization (for RL, the metric rises but real quality falls — Track 05), and forgetting. The discipline is the same as everywhere in this series: define what "good" means before training, measure it continuously, compare against baselines, and let evaluation — not hope or a single cherry-picked example — decide whether the fine-tune ships. Most fine-tuning failures are evaluation failures in disguise.

Analogy: a lab notebook: every experiment's recipe, ingredients, and result written down — so a promising run can be reproduced, and a regression can be traced to what changed.

The fine-tuning failure modes

Analogy: a checklist of the usual suspects — when a fine-tune goes wrong, it's almost always one of a handful of named culprits, each with a known fix.

Fine-tunes fail in recognizable ways, and naming them speeds diagnosis. Overfitting: memorizes the training set, fails on new inputs — fix with more/diverse data, fewer epochs, lower LR, regularization. Catastrophic forgetting: loses general or safety skills — fix with LoRA, data mixing, gentler updates (Track 02). Format collapse / mode collapse: over-learns one output pattern and loses flexibility or diversity — fix with more varied data and less aggressive training. Underfitting: barely changed — raise rank/LR/epochs, check loss masking and data quality.

Data problems are the most common root cause: mislabeled examples, inconsistent formats, a broken chat template, wrong loss masking, leakage between train and test, or simply too little signal — garbage in, garbage model. Reward hacking / over-optimization (for preference and RL): the metric rises while true quality falls (Track 05). Instability (for RL especially): loss diverges or collapses — fix with lower LR, better KL control, the DAPO-style stabilizers.

The meta-lesson mirrors RAG's seven failure modes: when a fine-tune disappoints, don't randomly tweak — walk the checklist. Is the data clean and correctly formatted? Is it overfitting (train/val gap) or underfitting (both high)? Did it forget (general benchmarks)? Is the reward being gamed (RL)? Each symptom points to a specific fix from the earlier tracks. And overwhelmingly, the first thing to check is the data — most fine-tuning failures trace back to data quality, format, or masking long before they're a method problem.

The alignment recipe

Analogy: finishing school after the fundamentals: first the model learns to answer (SFT), then learns to answer the way people prefer (DPO), with a final exam at every step.

The standard recipe for a helpful, aligned model layers two stages. Stage 1 — SFT: fine-tune the base on high-quality instruction-response data so it follows instructions and adopts your format (LoRA or full, per your budget). Stage 2 — preference tuning: run DPO (the default) on preference pairs to align it to human taste — more helpful, less verbose, safer — anchored to the SFT model as reference. For many production assistants, SFT + DPO is the entire post-training pipeline, and it's very achievable (both are stable, both run with LoRA, both are in TRL).

Variations by need: use KTO if your preference data is binary thumbs rather than pairs, ORPO to fuse the two stages into one pass, or add a lightweight GRPO/RLVR stage if you need verifiable-task performance (math, code, reasoning) on top. Throughout, the reference-model leash guards against reward hacking and drift, and evaluation runs at each stage — check the SFT model before aligning, and check the aligned model for both improved preference and preserved capability.

The key structural insight is order and reference: preference tuning builds on an SFT'd model (aligning a base model directly is unstable), and each preference method anchors to a reference to stay honest. This recipe — SFT then DPO, evaluated throughout — is the reproducible path from a raw base model to a genuinely useful assistant, and it's within reach of a small team on modest hardware. It's the "make it helpful" counterpart to the single-GPU "teach it a task" recipe.

The frontier recipe

Analogy: the same craft as the home workshop, industrialized into a factory: thousands of coordinated stations, verifiable quality control, and a process that produces reasoning at the edge of what's possible.

The frontier recipe assembles the whole guide at scale: multi-node RLVR reasoning training. (1) Cold-start SFT on clean chain-of-thought data for readable format. (2) RLVR at scale — sample groups of solutions to verifiable math/code problems, score with automated verifiers, and update with GRPO/DAPO (advantage = group-relative, KL to reference). (3) Rejection-sample the best solutions and SFT to consolidate. (4) A final alignment pass for general helpfulness and safety. (5) Distill the traces into smaller models. The systems layer underneath: FSDP/ZeRO-3 × tensor parallelism (in-node) × pipeline parallelism (cross-node) over InfiniBand, on hundreds to thousands of GPUs.

This is where every track converges: SFT (Track 02), LoRA optional, the preference and RL methods (Tracks 05–06), the distributed-training machinery (Tracks 07–08), and the frameworks and evaluation (Track 09). And it's the same scaling vocabulary and the same 16-to-16,000-GPU story as the LLMOps serving volume — frontier pretraining (Llama 3.1 405B on ~16,000 H100s) and frontier post-training both run on this machinery.

The reassurance that threads this entire series: the method does not change with scale — only the systems machinery does. The single-GPU QLoRA run and the 16,000-GPU frontier run share the same loop (forward, loss, backward, step), the same techniques (SFT, preference, RL), and the same disciplines (data quality, evaluation, tracking). Scale replaces one GPU with a coordinated fleet and adds ZeRO and parallelism to fit and go fast — but you learned all of it at small scale. Learn it on one GPU; operate it on many.
