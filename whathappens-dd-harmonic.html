Harmonic Drive: 100:1 and Zero Backlash From a Gear That Flexes

Every precise robot joint hides the same strange part — a thin steel cup with teeth, deliberately squashed into an ellipse and made to walk, one or two teeth per turn, around a rigid ring. From that flexing comes a 100:1 reduction in a single stage, with essentially zero backlash, in a package a conventional gear train can’t touch. It looks as though it shouldn’t work; it is in fact the cleanest way to get huge, precise reduction into a joint.

≈ 18 min read robotics · gearing · precision actuation companion to The DC-Motor and Control Systems

Hand a robot designer a fast, light electric motor and ask them to turn it into a joint — slow, strong, and precise enough to place a tool to a hair — and they reach, almost every time, for a part that looks as though it was designed as a dare. A thin steel cup with teeth cut around its rim, deliberately squashed out of round into an ellipse, and made to flex its way around a rigid ring that has a couple more teeth than it does. Spin the ellipse and the cup walks — creeping backward a tooth or two for every fast turn of the input — and out of that walking comes a 100:1 reduction in a single stage, with essentially no backlash, in a disc you could hold in your palm. A normal gear train cannot come close: big reduction means a stack of meshes, and every mesh must carry clearance or it jams, so the lost motion piles up exactly where a joint can least afford it. The escape is one reframe — stop treating the gear as rigid, make it flexible, and give it the wrong number of teeth — and from that single move the whole subject falls out: why the ratio is just a tooth-count difference, why so many teeth share the load that backlash vanishes, and why the same flexing that buys you all of this also makes the gearbox a soft spring with a fatigue clock. Then the piece turns to the half the textbooks skip: how you actually spec a joint, how it bites when it misbehaves, what to put on the purchase order, and a 100:1 cobot elbow sized end to end.

1The constraint: robot joints want the impossible gearbox

Start with exactly what a joint asks of its gearbox, because it asks for four things at once and ordinary gears can give you at most three. It wants a large reduction — typically 50:1 to 160:1 — because a small, fast, efficient motor has to become a slow, strong joint. It wants near-zero backlash, because any lost motion between teeth becomes slop at the end of the arm: the joint can no longer tell exactly where it is, and a position loop built on top of that slop jitters, buzzes, or hunts. It wants to be compact and light, because every gram at a joint near the shoulder is paid for again and again by every link it has to swing. And it wants to be coaxial and stiff — the motor in line with the output, nothing cantilevered out to one side.

A conventional spur-gear train walks straight into a wall. A big reduction out of spur stages means many stages — bulky, heavy, and lossy — and every meshing pair of teeth must have backlash, a deliberate clearance, or the teeth bind and wear as they roll through mesh and the housing grows with heat. You cannot simply machine the backlash out; teeth need room to move. So the one thing a joint most wants — huge reduction with no lost motion in a small, coaxial package — is precisely the combination a normal gear train is worst at. The question this piece answers is how you escape that wall, and the escape turns out to be a single strange idea.

2The reframe: make one gear flexible and give it the wrong tooth count

The escape is to stop treating the gear as rigid. Take a thin, flexible steel cup with external teeth — the flexspline — and surround it with a rigid internal ring gear — the circular spline — that has just a few more teeth, almost always exactly two more. Because the flexspline is flexible, you can deform it into an ellipse with a smooth elliptical bearing — the wave generator — pressed onto the motor shaft. The ellipse pushes the flexspline’s teeth into full engagement with the ring at the two ends of the major axis and pulls them completely clear at the minor axis. The teeth mesh only in those two moving zones; everywhere else they float free.

Now the trick. Spin the wave generator one full turn. The zone of engagement travels around the ring like a wave — nothing is sliding in the ordinary gear sense, the bulge is what moves. But the flexspline has two fewer teeth than the ring, so when the wave has gone all the way around and the same stretch of flexspline is back in mesh, the flexspline has been forced to slip back by exactly two teeth relative to the ring. One fast turn of the input becomes a tiny, two-tooth backward creep of the output. The reduction is not built from a train of meshes handing motion down a line; it is built from a tooth-count mismatch that the continuous flexing silently takes up, a little each turn, forever.

Spin the ellipse fast — the flexible gear walks back a couple of teeth per turn

Spin the wave generator and watch the three parts work together: the ellipse presses the flexspline into the ring at its two ends, the engagement zones chase the major axis around, and — this is the whole thing — a gold reference tooth on the flexspline creeps slowly backward, against the spin. Set the flexspline tooth count and the tooth difference and the creep rate changes with them: the reference tooth walks back by the tooth difference per input turn, so it drifts at exactly 1/ratio of the input speed. Fast in, a hair out, and backward — the reduction made visible.

3The core: the ratio is a tooth-count difference

Count the walking carefully and the ratio falls right out, with no gear train to track. Per full turn of the wave generator, the flexspline rotates relative to the fixed circular spline by the tooth difference, in the opposite direction. Divide the input turn by that creep and the reduction is simply:

ratio = NfNc − Nf = Nf2Nf flexspline teeth, Nc ring teeth; the usual difference is two

where Nf is the flexspline tooth count and Nc the circular spline’s. With the standard difference of two teeth (Nc − Nf = 2) the ratio is just half the flexspline tooth count. So a flexspline with 200 teeth inside a 202-tooth ring gives a clean 100:1 in a single stage — and the output turns backward relative to the input. The number of teeth directly sets the ratio; there is no stack of stages, just how many teeth the wave has to swallow per revolution. (Hold a different element fixed — drive the circular spline, fix the flexspline, or take the output off a different member — and the same three parts give different ratios and signs; the fixed-circular-spline, output-on-flexspline case is the common one.)

The whole drive in one line
ratio = NfNc − Nf = Nf2

Deform a flexible gear into an ellipse inside a rigid ring that has a few more teeth, and spin the ellipse. The flexible gear walks backward by the tooth difference each input turn, so the reduction is Nf/(Nc − Nf) = Nf/2 for the usual two-tooth difference — 100:1 from a 200/202 pair, in one compact, coaxial stage, output running opposite the input. No train, and no clearance to stack up.

Tooth count sets the ratio directly — no gear train

Set the flexspline tooth count Nf and the tooth difference and read the three numbers that follow straight from them: the ratio Nf/(Nc − Nf), the output speed (tiny, and backward), and the fraction of teeth engaged at once. Push the difference to one and the ratio leaps to Nf itself; open it up and the ratio collapses toward ordinary values. Alongside sits a single spur stage for scale — one harmonic stage at 100:1 dwarfs what a spur or planetary stage reaches before you have stacked three of them. The reduction is a tooth-count difference you dial in, nothing more.

4Limit checks — read the ratio at its ends, and what the flexing costs

The formula earns its keep at the extremes, and reading it there is the fastest way to see both where the magic comes from and what it will cost you.

Limit check — read the ratio at its ends Tooth difference = 1 (Nc − Nf = 1): the ratio becomes Nf itself — the maximum for a given size — but a one-tooth difference is fragile, hard to make run smoothly, and throws the ellipse off balance, so it is rare. Difference = 2 is the near-universal standard: still a high ratio (Nf/2), robust, and symmetric — the two engagement zones sit opposite each other and balance the radial loads. Difference large / few flexspline teeth: the ratio drops back toward conventional values, and you have thrown away the harmonic drive’s whole reason to exist. Many teeth engaged at once: because the ellipse presses a long arc of teeth into mesh at each end, ~10–30 % of all the teeth are engaged simultaneously — against one or two in a spur mesh — and this one fact is where the headline benefits come from: enormous torque density (the load is shared across many teeth), near-zero backlash (the teeth are held preloaded in mesh with no clearance to take up), and averaging (position accuracy beats any single tooth’s error, because many teeth vote at once). Flexspline flex → 0 (make the cup rigid): the mechanism simply stops working — the whole principle is the elastic deformation. The flexspline must flex through a full ellipse twice per input turn, forever, which is also exactly its life limit.

That last line is the hinge of the whole part, because the same flexing that makes the drive work also writes its character. Because many preloaded teeth share the load you get zero backlash and very high torque density — the twin signatures that put harmonic drives in every precision robot wrist and joint. But the flexspline is a thin elastic cup, so the drive is torsionally compliant: apply torque and the output twists a little before it truly moves. The gearbox is also a soft spring sitting in series with your joint. That compliance — plus a small hysteresis and a tiny, repeatable “kinematic error” that cycles twice per input turn — sets a ceiling on control bandwidth: you cannot servo a joint stiffer than its own gearbox, so the harmonic drive’s springiness decides how hard you can tune the position loop before it rings. And the relentless double-flex is a fatigue process — the flexspline is the wear-and-life-limiting part, rated for a finite number of cycles. Zero backlash is bought with compliance and a fatigue clock; that trade is the entire personality of the part, and everything in Part II is about living with it.

5Where it lives: speccing a joint, and how it bites

Harmonic drives live wherever precise, compact, zero-backlash reduction matters and cost is secondary: the joints of industrial arms and collaborative robots, the actuators of humanoid and legged robots, antenna and camera gimbals, satellite and telescope pointing mechanisms, CNC rotary and indexing tables, and semiconductor wafer handlers. You usually select one as a unit — increasingly an integrated motor + harmonic drive + encoder “actuator module.” The design sequence is fixed, not a flash of insight: fix the output torque (continuous and peak) and the ratio your motor-to-joint speeds demand; pick a frame size rated for that torque and for the moment / overhung load the joint actually sees; confirm the torsional stiffness is high enough for the control bandwidth you want; and budget the flexspline life against your duty cycle — in cycles, not hours. When a harmonic joint misbehaves it is almost always the compliance or the life talking, in a short and legible vocabulary:

Joint rings or buzzes when you tighten the position loop
You have hit the torsional compliance ceiling: the gearbox spring plus the arm inertia set a resonance you cannot servo past. Add a load-side sensor and a notch filter, or accept lower bandwidth — a faster motor will not help.
End-of-arm position is slightly off under load and springs back when unloaded
Wind-up: the flexspline twists elastically under torque. This is not backlash — it is recoverable deflection — so compensate it with a load-side encoder or a stiffness model, not by hunting for slop that isn’t there.
A small, repeatable position error that cycles twice per input revolution
Kinematic transmission error, inherent to the two-lobe wave. Map it and compensate in software if you need the last arc-seconds; otherwise live with it.
Premature roughness or failure after long service
Flexspline fatigue or lubrication breakdown: you have exceeded the rated cycles, lived too near the peak-torque limit, or starved the grease. It is a life-limited part — respect the cycle rating.
Grinding or ratcheting under shock
Dedoidal failure — tooth jumping — from a momentary overload past peak torque. Size peak torque for the worst shock the joint will ever take, not the average.

6How the flexing shapes the whole actuator

Nearly everything about a harmonic-driven joint traces back to the flexing cup, and the properties are not independent dials. Zero backlash is why you can close a precise position loop on the joint at all — but the torsional stiffness of that same cup caps the loop’s bandwidth, so stiffness is the number that quietly governs how responsive the joint is ever allowed to be. Torque density — torque per unit mass and volume, straight from the many engaged teeth — is why the arm can be light and the joint compact, which is a direct win on reach and payload. The single-stage coaxial form keeps the actuator short and the motor in line, instead of stacking reduction stages down the arm. And the fatigue life sets the maintenance interval and the real duty-cycle limit. So choosing a harmonic drive is choosing a point on a coupled surface of {ratio, torque, stiffness, backlash, life, mass}, where the flexspline’s elasticity ties several of those axes together — you cannot independently maximize stiffness and ratio and life. Move one and you have moved the others, which is why selection is a negotiation, not a lookup.

7Where it’s used, and what to spec

The same part runs industrial and collaborative robot joints, humanoid and legged-robot actuators, satellite and telescope pointing mechanisms, gimbals, CNC rotary and indexing tables, and medical and semiconductor automation — anywhere precise, compact, zero-backlash reduction is worth paying for. When you select one, these are the numbers that actually decide the outcome:

Reduction ratio
Set by tooth count (Nf/2 for the standard two-tooth difference). Pick it so the motor’s efficient speed lands at the joint’s needed speed.
Rated (continuous) & peak/momentary torque
Size continuous torque to the duty and peak torque to the worst shock. Peak is a flexspline-survival limit, not a duty you may sit at.
Torsional stiffness
The number that sets your achievable control bandwidth. Higher means stiffer, more responsive joints — but usually a larger, heavier unit.
Backlash / hysteresis / lost motion
Near-zero is the entire point. Check the spec, and check that it holds over the rated life, not just when new.
Rated life (cycles) & allowable moment / overhung load
The flexspline’s fatigue rating and the output bearing’s load capacity — and a joint sees big moments, not just pure torque.
Frame size / mass / form
Component set (lightest, but you own the bearings and alignment), housed unit (adds the cross-roller bearing), or integrated actuator (drop-in motor + drive + encoders + brake).
Efficiency & lubrication
Harmonic efficiency sags at low load and low temperature; the maker’s grease and the thermal environment matter more than the headline number.

8Field notes

What the datasheet won’t tell you
  • It is zero-backlash, not zero-compliance. People conflate the two and are shocked when a loaded joint deflects. There is no lost motion, but the flexspline is a spring — model the wind-up or add a load-side encoder if you need accuracy under torque.
  • The gearbox sets your control bandwidth, not the motor. You cannot servo the joint stiffer than its own torsional resonance, so a “faster” motor buys you nothing once you are up against the gearbox spring. Pick the stiffness for the bandwidth you need.
  • It is a life-limited part — count cycles, not hours. The flexspline fatigues with every revolution’s double-flex, so a lightly-loaded joint that moves constantly can wear out before a heavily-loaded one that rarely moves. Hours on a nameplate tell you little.
  • Efficiency is lousy at light load and cold. Harmonic drives can fall to 30–50 % efficiency at low torque or low temperature — starting friction is high — so never assume the headline efficiency at your actual operating point. Budget motor heat accordingly.
  • Peak torque is a survival rating, not a duty. You can touch peak for a moment (a collision, a shock); live near it and the flexspline fatigues or a tooth jumps (dedoidal failure). Size continuous torque for the job and peak for the accident.
  • Mind the kinematic error and the grease. There is a tiny, repeatable error twice per input turn and a real sensitivity to lubrication and temperature; precision-pointing applications map and compensate that error and control the thermal environment, rather than wishing it away.
  • Alignment and the wave generator are where it dies young. A component set assembled with poor concentricity or the wrong flexspline preload runs rough and fails early — one reason integrated actuator modules are popular: the maker owns the hard assembly, and you inherit it done right.

9Build it: a cobot elbow joint, 100:1

Enough principle — here is a real joint sized end to end, so you can copy the shape and reuse it. The job: a collaborative-robot elbow that holds ≈ 40 N·m at the joint, moves at up to ≈ 60 °/s, and must repeat its position to arc-minutes. Walk the choices, and every one of them reads straight off the part you already understand.

Worked example — a 100:1 cobot elbow
Ratio from the speeds
A BLDC servo runs comfortably at ≈ 3000 rpm; the joint needs ≈ 60 °/s = 10 rpm. A naïve 3000/10 ≈ 300 is too much for one harmonic stage at this torque, so target 100:1 (a 200/202 flexspline/circular-spline pair) and let the motor run a little slower, or trim the motor speed to suit. The output turns backward — account for the sign in firmware.
Torque sizing
The joint needs ≈ 40 N·m continuous; at 100:1 the motor supplies only ≈ 0.4 N·m before efficiency. Choose a frame size whose rated continuous torque exceeds 40 N·m with margin, and whose peak rating covers the worst collision shock — a cobot will be hit — often 2–3× continuous.
Stiffness vs bandwidth
Look up the unit’s torsional stiffness; with the arm inertia it fixes the joint’s resonant frequency. For a 10–20 Hz position bandwidth, the gearbox stiffness must put that resonance comfortably above it. If it cannot, add a load-side encoder and close an outer loop on the true joint angle.
Dual encoders
Put one encoder on the motor (for commutation and velocity) and one on the output/load side (for true joint angle, to see past the wind-up and kinematic error). This is standard on precise cobot joints, not a luxury.
Life
Estimate the revolutions over the robot’s service life from the duty cycle and confirm it is within the flexspline’s rated cycles at your average torque — derated for peak events. Count cycles, not hours.
Lubrication & thermal
Spec the maker’s grease and expect lower efficiency when cold; budget the motor heat accordingly, because the drive’s losses land in the same small housing as the windings.

That is the method made concrete: a torque, a speed, an accuracy target and a motor speed hand you the ratio, the tooth pair, the frame size, the control architecture, and — loudly — the fact that your real risks are the collision peak and the gearbox spring, not the steady 40 N·m. The plug-in set is small and you already have all of it. The reduction is the tooth-count difference, and the output runs opposite the input:

ratio = NfNc − Nf = Nf2tooth count sets the ratio directly

Speed divides by the ratio and torque multiplies by it, less the efficiency, which is poor at light load:

ωout = ωinratiothe output creeps; the input races
τout = ratio · τin · ηη = efficiency, often only 0.3–0.5 when cold or lightly loaded

Roughly 10–30 % of the flexspline’s teeth are engaged at once, which is what buys the torque density and the zero backlash. And the compliance limit — the ceiling on how hard you may tune the loop — is a resonance of the gearbox spring against the load inertia:

fres ≈ 12π√KtorsionalJloadthe stiffness-vs-inertia resonance that caps the bandwidth

Finally, the total output revolutions over the service life must stay inside the rated cycle life, derated for peak torque — the fatigue clock, written as a budget rather than a hope.

Bring it up in this order. The sequence matters; skipping a step just relocates the pain — usually to the moment the loop rings or the flexspline wears out early:

  1. Confirm ratio and direction. Command one motor revolution, measure the output motion → verify it is 1/ratio and in the expected (reversed) direction before you trust any kinematics.
  2. Measure backlash and wind-up separately. Lock the input, apply a known output torque both ways, and measure the output deflection → that is compliance (recoverable). Backlash proper should be near zero; distinguish the two now, before they confuse a field diagnosis later.
  3. Find the torsional resonance. With the real arm attached, do a frequency sweep — chirp the torque, watch the load-side encoder — and the resonant peak is your control-bandwidth ceiling.
  4. Set up dual-encoder control. Commutate and take velocity from the motor encoder; close the position loop on the load-side encoder to reject wind-up and kinematic error.
  5. Map the kinematic error (optional, for arc-second work): command slow, constant output motion, log the load-side error against input angle, and store the twice-per-turn correction.
  6. Validate the peak/shock case and log the duty. Exercise a worst-case stop or collision within the peak rating, confirm nothing jumps a tooth, and start a cycle counter for life tracking.

The forks that actually change the design. Most parameters you can trim later; these you decide up front:

Harmonic vs cycloidal vs planetary
Harmonic for the highest ratio, zero backlash, and lowest mass — robot wrists and distal joints. Cycloidal (RV) for higher torque, higher stiffness, and shock tolerance at proximal joints (heavier, a little backlash). Planetary when you need high efficiency and a moderate ratio cheaply and can tolerate some backlash.
Component set vs housed unit vs integrated actuator
Component set is lightest and cheapest but you own the bearings, alignment, and flexspline preload (hard); a housed unit adds the cross-roller bearing; an integrated actuator (motor + drive + encoders + brake) is drop-in and de-risks the assembly.
Single vs dual encoder
Motor-only is fine for speed, commutation, and rough position; add a load-side encoder the moment wind-up or kinematic error would blow your accuracy budget — which, on a precise cobot, it will.
Ratio: higher vs lower
A higher ratio (more flexspline teeth) means more reduction and torque density but lower output speed and a touch more compliance per unit; match it to the motor’s efficient speed and the joint’s speed need rather than maximizing it blindly.
The last mile — what you still do on the bench This page gets you the right gearbox type, a sound ratio, a frame size that covers continuous and peak torque, and a control architecture built around dual encoders — about 90 % of the job. The last 10 % is the mechanical reality of a flexing part and a stiff control loop. Measure the real torsional stiffness and resonance on your assembled joint with the real arm inertia; the catalog stiffness plus your structure’s own compliance set the true bandwidth ceiling, and it is always lower than you hoped. Budget flexspline life against the real duty cycle in cycles, derated for peak and shock events — a cobot that is constantly nudged clocks cycles faster than you think. Handle the wind-up and kinematic error with a load-side encoder (and a stored error map for arc-second work); do not try to out-stiffen them in software past the resonance. And respect lubrication and thermal — use the maker’s grease, expect low cold efficiency, and keep the gearbox in its temperature band for precision work. Tools to stand on: the maker’s selection software and catalog curves (Harmonic Drive LLC, Nabtesco for cycloidal), an integrated actuator module to de-risk assembly, dual encoders with a load-side angle sensor, and a frequency-response / chirp test to find the real resonance. The flexing-tooth principle in Part I never changes; the last mile is learning to live with the spring and the fatigue clock that come attached to the zero backlash.

10Check yourself

Question 1

A harmonic drive has a flexspline with 200 teeth and a circular spline with 202. What is the reduction ratio, which way does the output turn relative to the input, and what would a flexspline with 160 teeth (still two fewer than its ring) give?

The ratio is Nf/(Nc − Nf) = 200/2 = 100:1, with the output turning opposite to the input. A 160/162 pair gives 160/2 = 80:1, again reversed. For the standard two-tooth difference the ratio is simply half the flexspline tooth count — no gear train to work through, just how many teeth the wave walks back per turn. Change the tooth count and you change the ratio directly.

Question 2

Your cobot joint shows essentially zero backlash on the bench, yet when it holds a payload the tool tip sags a few arc-minutes and springs back the instant you remove the load. A colleague says the gearbox is worn. Why is he probably wrong, and what single sensor addition fixes the accuracy?

That is not backlash — backlash is lost motion independent of load, and it would not spring back. It is torsional wind-up: the flexspline is an elastic spring and twists under torque, recovering fully when unloaded. The gearbox is not worn at all; it is doing exactly what a flexing cup does. The fix is a load-side encoder — close the position loop on the true joint angle so the controller sees and compensates the deflection directly, instead of trusting the motor-side angle through a spring.

Question 3

You fit a “faster” motor to make your robot joint more responsive, but the position loop still rings at the same frequency no matter how you tune it. What is actually limiting the bandwidth, and what would raise the ceiling?

The torsional compliance of the harmonic drive — the flexspline spring — together with the arm inertia sets a mechanical resonance, fres ≈ (1/2π)√(K/J), and that resonance caps the achievable bandwidth. A faster motor cannot servo past the gearbox’s own spring, which is why the ring frequency does not move. To raise the ceiling you need a stiffer gearbox (higher K), a lower load inertia (J), or a load-side sensor with resonance-aware control — not more motor.
