Field-Oriented Control: How to Make a Spinning Motor Stand Still

A brushless motor only makes smooth torque if its magnetic field stays a precise 90° ahead of a rotor that won’t stop moving. The fix isn’t a faster controller — it’s a change of coordinates that makes the whole spinning problem sit still.

≈ 18 min read motors · power electronics · motion control companion to Control Systems and Drones

Every electric vehicle, every drone, every robot joint and half the appliances in your house turn a three-phase brushless motor with a technique called field-oriented control. It is one of those ideas that reads as intimidating — Clarke transforms, Park transforms, d–q frames, space-vector modulation — and feels like a wall of machinery you just have to memorise. It isn’t. All of it falls out of a single awkward fact about how a motor makes torque, plus one move so natural you would probably have reached for it yourself once you felt the problem. This piece builds the whole thing from that fact, and then turns to the half that textbooks skip: how you actually bring one up on the bench, how it shapes the machine around it, and the things that only bite you on the third prototype.

1The constraint: torque wants the field 90° ahead, and the rotor keeps moving

A motor makes torque by pulling one magnetic field against another. The rotor carries permanent magnets — a fixed magnetic arrow. The stator (the windings you control) makes a second field whose direction and strength you set by choosing the three phase currents. The torque between them is largest when the two fields are perpendicular: a stator field pointing straight at the rotor’s pole just pulls radially and spins nothing, while a field 90° (electrical) ahead pulls entirely sideways, all torque.

T = Tmax · cos δδ = error from the ideal 90°

So the job is simply stated: keep the stator field a quarter-turn ahead of the rotor, always. The catch is in that last word. The rotor is spinning — at a few thousand RPM its field sweeps through a full electrical revolution hundreds of times a second — so “90° ahead” is a target that races around the circle. In a brushed motor a mechanical commutator does this switching automatically as the shaft turns; that is the one thing the brushes are for. Take the brushes away (for the efficiency, the lifetime, the lack of sparking) and the responsibility lands on you: you must drive the three phase currents so their combined field tracks a moving target precisely.

Torque needs the stator field 90° ahead — lose the angle, lose the torque

The rotor’s magnet (one arrow) spins; the controller holds the stator field (the other arrow) ahead of it. Dial the advance error δ away from the ideal 90° and watch the torque gauge follow cos δ — at 90° of error the field points straight at the pole and the torque collapses to zero. This is why knowing the rotor’s angle is everything.

2The reframe: stop chasing the rotor — ride with it

Here is the move. You are struggling because every quantity in the problem is a fast sinusoid: each phase current swings iA = I cos θe, iB and iC lag by 120°, and the field they make is an arrow spinning around the circle at the electrical frequency. Trying to regulate a target that is itself whirling is what makes it hard — any simple controller is always chasing a value that has already moved on.

So change your point of view. First collapse the three redundant phase currents (they always sum to zero) into a single current vector on two fixed axes — that is the Clarke transform, pure geometry, three projections onto two:

iα = iA − ½iB − ½iC,    iβ = √32(iB − iC)

Now you have one vector spinning in the (α, β) plane. Then — the key step — rotate your coordinate frame by the rotor angle θe so your axes spin with the rotor. That is the Park transform:

id = iα cos θe + iβ sin θe,    iq = −iα sin θe + iβ cos θe

In this rotating frame the whirling stops. A current vector that was racing around the circle now sits still, described by two steady numbers: id, pointing along the rotor’s magnet (the “direct” axis), and iq, pointing 90° ahead of it (the “quadrature” axis). The impossible job of regulating fast AC has become the easy job of holding two DC values.

Three phase currents (AC) become two numbers (DC) in the rotor frame

On the left, the three phase currents sweep as sinusoids; speed the motor up and they oscillate faster. On the right, the same currents after Clarke + Park: two flat lines. No matter how fast the motor spins, id and iq hold steady — because you are now watching from the rotor’s own frame. That is the entire trick.

3The core result: torque is just iq

Recall the 90°-ahead rule. In the rotating frame, “90° ahead of the rotor’s magnet” is exactly the q-axis. So the torque-producing part of your current is simply iq, and the part pointing uselessly at the magnet is id. For a surface-magnet motor the torque is linear and clean:

The result the whole method exists to reach
T = 32 P λm iq

Commanding torque is commanding iq (P = pole pairs, λm = magnet flux). You run two simple PI loops on steady DC errors — one driving id to zero (don’t waste current pushing on the magnet), one driving iq to the torque you want — then rotate the result back out (inverse Park, inverse Clarke) and realise it with the switches. Three sinusoids go to the motor; two calm DC loops do the thinking.

Everything that looked like machinery is now plumbing: the transforms are coordinate changes, space-vector modulation is just how you ask three half-bridges to synthesise the voltage vector you computed. The intellectual content is one sentence — watch the currents from the rotor’s frame and torque becomes a single DC knob.

Limit check — the rotor angle is load-bearing Every transform above used θe, the rotor’s electrical angle. Feed in an angle that is wrong by δ and your “q-axis” current lands δ off from true 90°, so torque drops as cos δ — the same curve from §1, now as a control error rather than a physical one. At 90° of angle error you command full current and get zero torque (and a lot of heat). This is why half of FOC bring-up is really about measuring the rotor angle accurately, and why a good position sensor — or a good estimator — is the real heart of the system.

4Why not just switch the phases in steps? And the speed ceiling

The older, simpler answer is six-step (trapezoidal) commutation: chop the electrical revolution into six sectors and energise a fixed pair of phases in each. It needs only crude rotor sensing (three Hall switches) and no transforms, and it is why cheap drone ESCs and fans have run it for decades. But because the field jumps between six fixed directions instead of rotating smoothly, the angle to the rotor wanders ±30° within each sector, and the torque ripples with it — a roughly 14% peak-to-peak throb at six times the electrical frequency. FOC keeps the field continuously at 90°, so the torque is flat.

Six-step vs FOC — the torque ripple you can hear

Instantaneous torque over one electrical revolution: six-step lumps along with ~14% ripple at 6× the electrical frequency (you hear it as whine and feel it as cogging), while FOC holds a flat line at the same average. The ripple is the price of letting the field jump instead of rotate.

One consequence is worth carrying forward, because it reappears as a design limit everywhere. As the motor speeds up, its back-EMF — the voltage the spinning magnet induces — grows until it approaches your DC-bus voltage, and the inverter can no longer push more current: that is the motor’s base speed. To go faster you deliberately inject a negative id, using the axis you were holding at zero to partially cancel the magnet’s flux. This field weakening trades torque for speed and gives you the constant-power region above base speed — the reason an EV keeps accelerating past the speed where its motor “should” have run out of voltage. The d-axis you thought was wasted is your high-speed gear.

5Where it lives in the design, and how it fails on the bench

FOC is the innermost loop of a motion system. From the inside out you have the current (torque) loop running fastest — typically 10–20 kHz, once per PWM cycle — then a velocity loop around it, then a position loop around that, each roughly five to ten times slower than the one it wraps. You design and tune them in that order: a wobbly current loop makes every outer loop hopeless, so you get FOC solid first. A useful back-of-envelope: the current loop’s natural bandwidth is set by the motor’s electrical time constant L/R, and the PI gains follow directly from L and R — which is why the first thing a modern drive does at commissioning is measure (or auto-identify) phase resistance and inductance.

Commissioning almost always runs in this sequence: identify R and L → find the encoder/rotor alignment offset (where is electrical zero?) → tune the current loop → close velocity → close position. And the failures cluster into a short, recognisable list:

Draws current, gets hot, barely turns
Rotor-angle alignment is wrong, or two phases / the encoder direction are swapped — you’re commanding current off the q-axis, so cos δ is killing your torque.
Works one way, bucks the other
Encoder offset has the wrong sign, or pole-pair count is off — the electrical angle tracks correctly in one direction only.
Audible whine, once-per-rev wobble
Current-sensor offset/gain mismatch between phases (calibrate at zero current), or the current loop tuned too aggressively and singing on PWM noise.
Loses control at high speed
You hit the back-EMF / bus-voltage ceiling and field weakening isn’t enabled or is mistuned.
Rough torque at 6× electrical
Inverter dead-time distortion uncompensated, or you’re actually still in six-step.

6How it shapes the machine around it

The quality of the current loop propagates into properties you’d never think to trace back to a coordinate transform. Smooth torque means a quiet, low-vibration machine — which matters enormously on a drone, where motor vibration is the dominant noise injected into the IMU, and on a camera gimbal or an EV cabin where people feel every ripple. Efficiency rises because current is always doing useful (q-axis) work rather than heating the windings uselessly, and since copper loss scales with i2, the current limit you can sustain sets the motor’s thermal envelope — which in turn sizes the magnets, the inverter FETs, and the cooling. The torque bandwidth — how fast the loop can change torque — is exactly how tight a robot joint can be and how aggressively a drone can snap an attitude correction. And field weakening sets the top speed, which decides your gear ratio. A great many of a machine’s headline numbers — range, noise, agility, top speed, continuous torque — trace back through the current loop to this one idea.

7Where it’s used, and what to keep in mind

Anywhere a brushless or synchronous motor needs smooth, efficient, controllable torque: EV traction inverters, drone and eVTOL ESCs, robot joints and collaborative-robot actuators, CNC and industrial servos, e-bikes, hard-drive and fan spindles, and the compressors in heat pumps, fridges and washing machines. When you choose or design one, the parameters that actually decide the outcome:

Rotor-position source
Encoder/resolver (accurate, costly, wiring) vs Hall sensors (cheap, coarse) vs sensorless back-EMF/observer (no sensor, but blind near zero speed). The single biggest architecture decision.
PWM frequency
Higher cuts current ripple and audible noise but raises switching loss — a trade against your FETs and heatsink.
Dead-time
Must be long enough to prevent shoot-through, short enough not to distort the voltage — and you almost always need dead-time compensation.
Current sensing
Shunt placement (inline vs low-side) and sample timing relative to the PWM edge decide how clean your id,iq are.
DC-bus voltage vs back-EMF
Sets base speed and how much field weakening you’ll need for your top-speed target.
SPMSM vs IPMSM
Interior-magnet motors add reluctance torque, so id isn’t simply held at zero — you track a maximum-torque-per-amp (MTPA) curve.

8Field notes

What the datasheet won’t tell you
  • The rotor-alignment offset is the number-one bring-up headache. Get its value or sign slightly wrong and the motor almost works — runs, warms, turns weakly — which is far more confusing than a dead failure. Budget real bench time for finding electrical zero.
  • Sensorless FOC is blind at a standstill. Back-EMF is what it listens to, and a stopped motor makes none, so you need an open-loop forced start or high-frequency injection to get moving. Plenty of shipping products hide a “kick to start” behind a clean spec sheet.
  • Dead-time compensation is the quiet secret behind smoothness. The textbook derivation ignores it; the product lives or dies by it. Uncompensated dead-time is the usual culprit when FOC still sounds rough.
  • Always use the electrical angle — pole-pairs × mechanical angle. Forgetting to multiply by pole pairs is a rite-of-passage first bug: the motor cogs and stutters because your frame rotates at the wrong rate.
  • A once-per-revolution wobble is almost never mechanical — it’s current-sensor offset or scale mismatch between phases. Calibrate the sensors at zero current before you chase the mechanics.
  • The efficiency and range wins everyone quotes live in MTPA and field weakening, and both are model-sensitive: magnetic saturation makes λm and the inductances drift with current, so a model tuned at low current lies at high current.
Build itthe numbers and the procedure — so you can apply it, not just follow it

9Build it: one motor, end to end

Enough principle — here is the actual chain of numbers, with a concrete motor, so you can copy it once and reuse the shape. Take a typical robotics/drone brushless motor: 7 pole-pairs, phase resistance R = 0.08 Ω, phase inductance L = 25 µH, Kv = 270 RPM/V, run from a 24 V bus at 30 kHz PWM. Four numbers decide the design, and each is a one-line formula you plug into — the method already did the hard part.

Worked example — the four numbers that size an FOC drive
Base / no-load speed
n ≈ Kv · Vbus = 270 × 24 ≈ 6,500 RPM — the speed where back-EMF meets the bus (loaded, a little less). Need more? That gap is what field weakening buys.
Current-loop bandwidth
Set it to ≈ 1/10 of the PWM rate: ωc = 2π·(fpwm/10) = 2π·3000 ≈ 18,800 rad/s (≈ 3 kHz) — crisp torque without amplifying PWM noise.
PI gains (plug straight in)
Kp = ωc·L = 18,800 × 25µH ≈ 0.47 V/A;  Ki = ωc·R = 18,800 × 0.08 ≈ 1,500 V/(A·s). The PI zero at R/L cancels the motor's electrical pole, leaving a clean first-order loop.
Current ripple (a reality check)
ΔIpp ≈ Vbus / (4·L·fpwm) = 24 / (4·25µH·30k) ≈ 8 A peak-to-peak. On a low-inductance motor that's large — which is why small motors force high PWM frequencies (or added inductance). This one line is why a drone ESC switches at 30–50 kHz, not 10.

That is the whole point of the method made concrete: three datasheet numbers (R, L, Kv) plus your bus and PWM choice hand you the gains to type in, the speed you'll reach, and a warning that your ripple is high — no transforms by hand, no controls theory at the keyboard.

Bring it up in this order. Commissioning almost always runs the same sequence; skipping a step just relocates the pain:

  1. Identify R and L — measure, or let the drive auto-ID. Every gain below depends on them.
  2. Fix phase order & sensor direction — a swapped phase or a backwards encoder makes everything downstream chase the wrong way.
  3. Find electrical zero (alignment) — align the rotor to a known angle and store the offset. This is the step that most often goes wrong; spend real bench time here.
  4. Set the current loop from Kp=ωcL, Ki=ωcR, then verify a clean torque step — fast, no overshoot.
  5. Close velocity, then position — each roughly 5–10× slower than the loop it wraps.
  6. Enable field weakening only if you need speed past base — and only with a motor model you trust.

The forks that actually change the design. Most parameters you can trim later; these you decide up front:

Encoder vs sensorless
Need torque from zero speed (a robot joint, traction, anything that holds position)? You need a real position sensor. Only ever spinning fast (a fan, a pump, a prop in flight)? Sensorless drops the sensor and its wiring — you accept a managed open-loop start.
FOC vs six-step
Smoothness, low speed, efficiency or quiet matter → FOC. Dirt-cheap, always-fast, ripple-tolerant (a cooling fan) → six-step is simpler and fine.
PWM frequency
Set by inductance: low-L motors (drones) force 30–50 kHz to tame ripple; high-L industrial motors run happily at 8–16 kHz with less switching loss.
The last mile — what you still do on hardware Two things this page cannot do for you, and honestly no article can. Electrical-zero alignment on your actual motor and sensor is a physical calibration — you run the drive's alignment routine once and store the number. And the final gain trim: the formulas above land you in the right ballpark on the first try, but you’ll nudge them against a real step response. When you get to that bench, don’t hand-roll the loop — stand on a proven implementation and tune its knobs: SimpleFOC (Arduino/ESP, ideal for learning and small robots), ODrive or VESC (robotics and e-mobility, open and well-documented), or your MCU vendor's library (TI InstaSPIN / MotorControl SDK, ST Motor Control SDK). Your job is to know what their knobs mean — which, after this, you do.

10Check yourself

Question 1

Your new motor draws its rated current and gets warm, but produces almost no torque and barely spins. Nothing is mechanically jammed. What is the single most likely cause, and why does “draws current but no torque” point straight at it?

The rotor-angle reference (encoder alignment offset / electrical zero) is wrong. Torque is T = Tmax cos δ where δ is the error between your commanded q-axis and true 90°; a large δ means the current is pushing almost straight at the magnet — full copper loss (hence the heat and current) with almost no sideways pull (hence no torque). A mechanical jam would instead trip overcurrent or stall hard.
Question 2

Why does transforming into the rotating d–q frame make the controller’s job easy, when the three phase currents are fast sinusoids?

A PI controller drives a steady error to zero; it lags and never catches a continuously moving target. In the frame that rotates with the rotor, a constant torque demand corresponds to constant id,iq — the AC vanishes, the setpoints stop moving, and two ordinary PI loops on DC errors suffice. The transform doesn’t change the physics; it changes the vantage point so the problem stops moving.
Question 3

Your motor runs out of speed well below where you need it, even at full current. You can’t raise the battery voltage. What can you do in the control, and what does it cost you?

Apply field weakening: inject a negative id to partially cancel the rotor’s flux, lowering the back-EMF so the inverter can keep pushing current past base speed. The cost is torque — you’re spending current on the d-axis instead of the q-axis, so you enter the constant-power region where torque falls as speed rises. It also stresses the magnets and demands a good motor model, since over-weakening risks loss of control.
