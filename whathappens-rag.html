Track 01 — What RAG actually is

Foundations

The mental models before the machinery: what retrieval-augmented generation is, the pipeline every RAG system shares, when to reach for RAG versus fine-tuning versus long context, and why retrieval is the right design even when context windows are enormous.

01Foundations

What RAG actually is (and the problem it solves)

Analogy: an open-book exam. The model is a brilliant student with a frozen memory; RAG hands it the exact page it needs, right before it answers.

A language model's knowledge is parametric — baked into its weights at training time and frozen there. That means it cannot know your private documents, anything newer than its cutoff, or the specific fact buried in your 10,000-page corpus; and when it doesn't know, it tends to hallucinate a plausible answer. Retrieval-Augmented Generation fixes this by adding a step before generation: fetch the relevant passages from an external knowledge source and place them in the prompt, so the model answers from evidence you supplied rather than from memory.

The payoff is four things at once: freshness (update the knowledge base, not the model), private knowledge (your docs the model never trained on), grounding (answers cite real passages, cutting hallucination), and attribution (you can show where an answer came from). It is by far the most common way to make a general model useful on specific, changing, proprietary data — without touching the weights.

The catch, and the reason this whole guide exists: RAG is only as good as what it retrieves. Put the wrong passage in the prompt and the model confidently answers from garbage; miss the right passage and it has nothing to ground on. So the real engineering is not "call an LLM" — it is building a retrieval system that reliably finds the right evidence, which is a search problem with decades of depth behind it.

Why it mattersRAG turns a frozen, general model into one that answers from your current, private evidence — but it moves the hard problem to retrieval, which is what the next nine tracks are about.

02Foundations · the map

The RAG pipeline, end to end

Analogy: a research assistant's workflow: understand the question, pull the right files from the archive, skim to the best passages, lay them on the desk, then write the answer from them.

Every RAG system, from a weekend demo to a web-scale platform, is the same pipeline in two halves. Ingestion (offline): load documents, chunk them into passages, embed each chunk into a vector, and index those vectors in a vector database. Query (online): embed the user's query, run an approximate-nearest-neighbor search to get candidate chunks, optionally rerank them with a sharper model, augment the prompt with the best few, and generate the answer.

Each stage is a lever with its own failure mode, and this guide is organized around them: embeddings (does the vector capture meaning?), the ANN index (does search find the true neighbors fast?), chunking (is the fact intact in one chunk?), retrieval and hybrid search (dense + keyword), reranking and query transforms (advanced retrieval), and evaluation (did any of it work?). Master the stages and you can debug which one broke when an answer is wrong.

The mental model to carry: the LLM at the end never memorized your corpus — retrieval's entire job is to put the right passages in front of it at answer time. 03Foundations

RAG vs fine-tuning vs long context

Analogy: three ways to help a chef cook your dish: hand them the recipe card as they cook (RAG), send them to culinary school for your cuisine (fine-tuning), or read them the entire cookbook aloud every time (long context).

There are three ways to give a model knowledge or behavior it lacks, and they solve different problems. RAG injects knowledge at inference time from an external store — best for facts that are large, changing, private, or need citations. Fine-tuning changes the weights — best for teaching behavior, format, tone, or a skill (how to respond), not for memorizing a big evolving fact base. Long context stuffs everything into a huge prompt — simplest, but costs scale with tokens and quality degrades as the context grows.

The clean rule of thumb: RAG for knowledge, fine-tuning for behavior, long context for small or one-off inputs. Need the model to answer from a million documents that change weekly? RAG. Need it to always reply in your JSON schema or legal style? Fine-tune. Need it to reason over one 50-page contract right now? Long context (or RAG over that one doc). They combine: a fine-tuned model that also uses RAG is common and strong.

Why not "just use a huge context window"? Because context is not free memory: every token costs money and latency on every request, models retrieve worse from the middle of a long context (the "lost in the middle" effect), and you still need to find the right documents to put in it — which is retrieval again. Long context complements RAG; it doesn't replace it for large, dynamic corpora.

Why it mattersPicking the wrong tool wastes months — fine-tuning to inject facts, or RAG to change behavior, both fight the technique. Knowledge → RAG; behavior → fine-tune. (See the companion fine-tuning volume.)

04Foundations

Why retrieval beats stuffing everything into the prompt

Analogy: you don't photocopy the whole library for every question — you use the catalog to pull the three right books.

If context windows are huge, why not just paste the entire corpus every time? Four reasons make retrieval the right architecture. Cost and latency: you pay per token on every request, so pasting a million tokens of context for a one-line answer is enormous waste, repeated forever. Attention dilution: models genuinely reason worse over very long inputs and often miss facts sitting in the middle — a focused five-passage prompt beats a 500-passage one.

Freshness and scale: your knowledge base is often far bigger than any context window and changes constantly; an index you update is tractable, re-pasting everything is not. Access control: retrieval is where you enforce who can see what — you filter the search to the documents this user is allowed to read, which is impossible if you've dumped everything into one prompt.

So retrieval is not a workaround for small context windows — it is the correct design even when windows are large. It turns "search a huge, permissioned, changing corpus" into "read a handful of relevant, authorized passages," which is cheaper, faster, safer, and higher-quality. The rest of this guide is how to make that handful the right handful.

Why it mattersRetrieval is the correct architecture for large, changing, permissioned knowledge even with million-token context windows — it is cheaper, sharper, and the only place to enforce who-can-see-what.

Track 02 — Meaning as geometry

Embeddings & vectors

Retrieval begins by turning text into vectors where meaning becomes distance. This track covers what an embedding is, how to choose the model, how "nearness" is measured, and the systematic ways embeddings fail — the blind spots that motivate half of everything downstream.

05Embeddings

Text becomes a vector

Analogy: a map of meaning. Every word or passage gets GPS coordinates, and things that mean similar things sit close together — "dog" near "puppy," far from "invoice."

An embedding model converts a piece of text into a fixed-length list of numbers — a vector, typically a few hundred to a couple thousand dimensions. The magic is that the model is trained so that semantic similarity becomes geometric closeness: texts with similar meaning land near each other in this high-dimensional space, even if they share no words. "How do I reset my password" sits near "steps to recover your credentials," because meaning, not spelling, drives position.

This is what makes semantic search possible. To find relevant passages you embed the query into the same space and look for the nearest vectors — "nearest" measured by a distance like cosine similarity. Unlike keyword search, this finds conceptually related text that uses entirely different words, which is exactly what you want when a user's phrasing differs from the document's. The embedding is the bridge between human questions and stored knowledge.

Two things to internalize. First, the query and the documents must be embedded by the same model into the same space, or distances are meaningless — which is why changing your embedding model forces you to re-embed the entire corpus. Second, the vector is a lossy summary: it captures the gist wonderfully but can blur precise details (exact IDs, numbers, rare terms), which is why pure semantic search sometimes needs keyword search alongside it (Track 06). 06Embeddings

Choosing an embedding model

Analogy: picking a translator: dimensions, languages, and specialty all matter — a great generalist may still fumble your legal jargon.

Embedding models differ on axes that directly shape retrieval quality. Dimensions (e.g. 384, 768, 1024, 1536, 3072) trade recall for memory and speed — higher can capture more nuance but costs more storage and compute per vector; some models (Matryoshka embeddings) let you truncate dimensions to trade quality for size. Max input length caps how big a chunk you can embed. Domain and language matter enormously — a model strong on general web text may be mediocre on code, law, medicine, or non-English.

The open-source workhorses are strong and free: the BGE, E5, GTE, Nomic, and Jina families, plus multilingual variants; the MTEB leaderboard is the standard place to compare them on retrieval, clustering and reranking benchmarks. Many teams start with a well-regarded open model and only move to a paid API embedding if evaluation shows it's worth it — because self-hosting embeddings keeps your data private and costs predictable.

The decisive move is to evaluate on your own data, not the leaderboard. A benchmark-topping model can underperform a smaller one on your domain; build a small labeled set of (query, relevant-doc) pairs and measure retrieval recall. And remember the coupling: whichever model you pick, every document is embedded with it, so switching later means a full re-index — choose deliberately, and version the model with the index.

Why it mattersThe embedding model sets the ceiling on retrieval quality — and because switching it means re-embedding everything, it is a decision to make on your own data, and to version alongside your index.

07Embeddings

Distance metrics — how "nearness" is measured

Analogy: measuring how alike two arrows are: by the angle between them (cosine) or by how far apart their tips land (Euclidean). For meaning, the angle usually wins.

Once text is a vector, "similar" becomes a math operation, and which operation matters. Cosine similarity measures the angle between two vectors, ignoring their length — it asks "do these point the same direction?" This is the default for text embeddings, because meaning is encoded in direction and you don't want a longer document to look more or less similar just because its vector has bigger magnitude. Dot product is cosine times the magnitudes; on normalized vectors (length 1) dot product and cosine are identical, which is why many systems normalize and use dot product for speed.

Euclidean (L2) distance measures the straight-line gap between the vector tips — sometimes used, but for text it usually behaves like cosine once vectors are normalized. The practical rule: use the metric your embedding model was trained with (most text models expect cosine or normalized dot product), and be consistent everywhere — the index, the query, and any reranking must all agree, or scores are nonsense.

A subtle but real gotcha: some vector databases default to one metric, and if it mismatches your model's training you get quietly worse retrieval with no error. Normalization also matters — if your model outputs unnormalized vectors and you use dot product, longer texts can dominate. Pick the metric the model documents, normalize consistently, and you remove a whole class of silent quality bugs.

Why it mattersThe distance metric must match what the embedding model was trained for and be consistent across index and query — a mismatch silently degrades retrieval with no error to warn you.

08Embeddings

Where embeddings quietly fail

Analogy: a photo that captures the vibe of a scene but blurs the license plate — great for "find similar streets," useless for "find this exact car."

Embeddings are powerful but have systematic blind spots worth knowing before they bite you. Exact tokens: a vector blurs precise identifiers — part numbers, error codes, names, dates — so semantic search can miss "error E-4021" while nailing "connection failures." Asymmetry: a short question and a long answer document look different geometrically; some models offer separate query and document encodings or instruction prefixes ("query:" / "passage:") — use them, or retrieval suffers.

Domain gap: a model trained on general text underperforms on specialized jargon — the embedding for a medical or legal term may sit near the wrong neighbors. Multilinguality: a monolingual model can't match a French query to an English doc; you need a multilingual model for cross-language retrieval. Granularity: embedding a whole 5-page document into one vector averages away everything — which is why chunking (Track 05) exists.

And the operational trap: staleness on model change. Because embeddings only compare within one model's space, upgrading the embedding model invalidates your entire index — old vectors and new vectors are not comparable. You must re-embed the whole corpus and swap atomically. Knowing these failure modes is what separates "I called an embedding API" from "I built retrieval that works," and it's exactly why production RAG pairs dense embeddings with keyword search.

Why it mattersEmbeddings capture gist and blur specifics — knowing exactly where they fail (exact terms, jargon, asymmetry, staleness) is why serious RAG adds keyword search and re-indexes deliberately.

Track 03 — Inside the vector index

ANN index internals

The engine room most RAG tutorials never open. How approximate-nearest-neighbor search actually finds vectors fast — the HNSW graph, the IVF cluster-and-probe, product-quantization compression, and disk-based DiskANN — and how their parameters trade recall against speed, memory and scale.

09ANN indexes

Exact search doesn't scale — the ANN bargain

Analogy: finding the closest coffee shop by driving to every shop in the country versus asking a few locals near you. The second is 99% as good and a million times faster.

To find the nearest vectors to a query, the honest method is brute force: compute the distance to every stored vector and keep the smallest. That is exact — and it is O(N·d): for N vectors of dimension d, every query touches all of them. At a few thousand vectors, fine. At millions or billions (a real corpus), it is hopeless — each query would read gigabytes and take seconds.

The escape is Approximate Nearest Neighbor (ANN) search: give up the guarantee of finding the exact top-k in exchange for finding almost the top-k, thousands of times faster. The quality of an ANN index is measured by recall — the fraction of the true nearest neighbors it actually returns — and a good index hits ~95–99% recall while examining a tiny slice of the data. That trade is the entire reason vector databases exist.

Every ANN algorithm is a different way to avoid looking at most of the data while still landing near the true answer: build a graph you can navigate (HNSW), cluster the space and only search nearby clusters (IVF), or compress vectors so you can scan many cheaply (PQ) — often combined. The knobs on each let you dial the recall ↔ speed ↔ memory trade-off. The next three scenarios open each one up; play this one to feel the bargain first.

10ANN indexes · the star

HNSW — the navigable small-world graph

Analogy: a subway map with express and local lines. You ride the sparse express line to get near your destination fast, then switch to local lines for the last few stops.

HNSW (Hierarchical Navigable Small World) is the most popular in-memory ANN index, and it is a multi-layer graph. Every vector is a node connected to some of its nearest neighbors. The bottom layer (Layer 0) contains all vectors densely connected; each layer above contains a random, sparse subset with longer-range links — like the express lines of a subway, or the fast lanes of a skip list. This hierarchy is what makes search both fast and accurate.

Search is greedy descent: start at an entry point in the top, sparse layer and repeatedly hop to whichever neighbor is closest to the query; when you can't get closer, drop down a layer and continue with its denser links; repeat until Layer 0. The top layers cover huge distances in a few hops (coarse approach); the bottom layer refines to the true neighborhood. You reach the right region in roughly log(N) hops instead of scanning N vectors.

Three parameters govern the trade-off. M = connections per node (higher = denser graph, better recall, more memory). ef_construction = how hard it searches for good neighbors when building (higher = better graph, slower build). ef_search = the candidate-list size at query time (higher = better recall, slower query) — the main runtime dial. HNSW gives the best recall-for-speed of the common indexes; its cost is memory (the whole graph lives in RAM) and slower builds, which is why billion-scale systems reach for IVF-PQ or DiskANN instead.

11ANN indexes

IVF — cluster the space, probe a few cells

Analogy: a library organized into sections. You don't search every shelf — you go to the two or three sections most likely to hold your book, and search only those.

IVF (Inverted File index) takes the opposite approach to HNSW: instead of a graph, it partitions the space into clusters. At build time, k-means groups all vectors into nlist cells, each with a centroid; every vector is filed under its nearest centroid (the "inverted list"). This is cheap to build and cheap on memory — it's just a clustering plus lists.

At query time, IVF compares the query to the nlist centroids, picks the nprobe nearest cells, and searches only the vectors in those cells, ignoring the rest. nprobe is the master dial: nprobe=1 is fastest but may miss neighbors that sit just across a cell boundary; higher nprobe searches more cells for higher recall at more cost. Choosing nlist (often ~√N) balances cell size against the number of centroids to compare.

IVF's superpower is scale and memory: it is built for hundreds of millions to billions of vectors, and it pairs naturally with product quantization (next) to shrink the vectors inside each cell — the famous IVF-PQ combination that fits billion-scale corpora in RAM. Its weakness is the boundary problem: a true neighbor in an unprobed cell is simply missed, so IVF typically needs higher nprobe (more work) to match HNSW's recall. 12ANN indexes

Product quantization — compress the vectors

Analogy: describing a paint color not by its exact RGB but by "nearest match in a 256-swatch card." You lose a little precision, but the color now fits in one byte.

Vectors are big: a 1024-dim float32 vector is 4 KB, so a billion of them is 4 TB — far too much RAM. Product Quantization (PQ) compresses them dramatically with a clever trick. Split each vector into m equal sub-vectors. For each sub-vector position, run k-means over the dataset to learn a small codebook (say 256 representative sub-vectors). Now store each sub-vector as just the index of its nearest codebook entry — one byte instead of many floats.

A 128-dimensional float vector (512 bytes) split into m=8 sub-vectors becomes 8 bytes — a ~64× compression. The reason it still works for search: distances can be approximated from the codes using precomputed lookup tables (asymmetric distance computation), so you never decompress. Scanning millions of 8-byte codes with table lookups is far faster and lighter than scanning full float vectors.

The cost is a little accuracy — you're comparing quantized approximations, so recall drops slightly, tuned by m and codebook size (nbits). PQ is rarely used alone; it lives inside IVF cells (IVF-PQ) or alongside a graph, giving the memory of quantization with the pruning of clustering. This is the machinery behind billion-scale vector search on commodity RAM. Slide m in the animation to watch the compression.

13ANN indexes

DiskANN — billion-scale on an SSD

Analogy: a warehouse too big to keep in your office. You keep the index (the aisle map) on your desk and fetch the actual boxes from the warehouse only when you need them.

HNSW is fast but demands that the whole graph and all vectors live in RAM — expensive and capped at a few hundred million vectors per machine. DiskANN (and its streaming variants) breaks that ceiling by keeping the bulk of the index on SSD/NVMe and only a compressed summary in RAM. It builds a single navigable graph (Vamana) designed for the access pattern of disk: search touches only the handful of nodes on the path, fetching their full vectors from SSD on demand.

The design trick is to keep PQ-compressed vectors in memory for fast approximate navigation, and read the full-precision vectors from disk only for the final re-ranking of the few candidates the graph search surfaces. This yields near-HNSW recall at a fraction of the RAM — a billion vectors served from one node with modest memory, where an all-in-RAM index would need a cluster.

The trade is latency: an SSD read is far slower than a RAM hop, so DiskANN queries are slower than HNSW's (still milliseconds), and it leans on fast NVMe. It is the go-to when your corpus is genuinely huge and RAM cost dominates — the answer to "billions of vectors without a giant memory bill." Choosing between HNSW, IVF-PQ and DiskANN is the next scenario.

Why it mattersDiskANN is how a single machine serves billions of vectors without a giant RAM bill — keeping compressed vectors in memory and full ones on SSD, trading a little latency for enormous scale.

14ANN indexes

Choosing an index — the recall/speed/memory triangle

Analogy: choosing a vehicle: a sports car (HNSW — fastest, thirstiest), a cargo ship (IVF-PQ — vast capacity, steady), or a long-haul truck with a trailer (DiskANN — huge loads, a bit slower).

There is no universally best index — you pick by where you sit on the recall ↔ speed ↔ memory ↔ scale triangle. HNSW: best recall-for-latency, but all in RAM — ideal up to tens or low hundreds of millions of vectors where memory cost is acceptable and you want the lowest latency. It is the default in most vector databases for good reason.

IVF-PQ: much lower memory (quantized vectors) and built for billions, at the cost of tuning (nlist/nprobe/m) and somewhat lower recall per unit work — the choice when the corpus is huge and RAM is the constraint. DiskANN: billion-scale on one node with modest RAM by paging from SSD, trading latency — the choice when even IVF-PQ in RAM is too big or too costly. Flat / brute force is still right for small collections (<~50k) where exactness beats index-building complexity.

The practical method: estimate your vector count and memory budget, set a target recall and latency, then choose — and measure recall on your own data by comparing against a brute-force ground truth on a sample. Most managed vector DBs expose these indexes with sensible defaults; the value of understanding the internals (previous scenarios) is that when recall or latency disappoints, you know which knob (ef_search, nprobe, m, or a different index) to turn.

Why it mattersThe index choice is a budget decision on the recall/speed/memory/scale triangle — and knowing the internals means that when it underperforms, you know exactly which parameter to turn.

Vector databases

15Vector DBs

What a vector database actually does

Analogy: not just a shelf of vectors, but the whole library system: the shelves, the catalog, the checkout desk, the security gate, and the staff who keep it all current.

A vector database is more than "a place to put embeddings." It bundles several jobs: store the vectors durably alongside their metadata and original text (or a pointer to it); build and maintain an ANN index (HNSW/IVF-PQ) over them; execute similarity search with metadata filtering; support CRUD (insert, update, delete vectors as documents change); and handle the operational concerns — persistence, replication, sharding, backups, and concurrency — that turn a clever algorithm into a service.

The distinctive hard part is combining vector search with the rest: filtering ("nearest vectors where tenant=acme and year=2026"), hybrid keyword+vector search, and consistency as data changes. A plain ANN library like FAISS gives you the index but none of the database around it; a vector database wraps the index in storage, filtering, updates, and APIs. Which you need depends on scale and how much you want to operate yourself.

The landscape splits into two camps: add vectors to a database you already run (pgvector in Postgres, plus vector support in Elasticsearch/OpenSearch, Redis, MongoDB) or dedicated vector databases built for it (Qdrant, Milvus, Weaviate, Chroma, LanceDB). The next scenarios cover both, the filtering problem that unites them, and how they scale — because choosing here is really choosing how retrieval behaves under load and change.

16Vector DBs

pgvector — vectors in the database you already run

Analogy: adding a new aisle to your existing supermarket instead of building a second store across town — same building, same staff, one checkout.

pgvector is a PostgreSQL extension that adds a vector column type and ANN indexes (HNSW and IVFFlat) to plain Postgres. Its appeal is enormous when your data already lives in Postgres: you store embeddings in the same table as your rows, and a single SQL query can do vector similarity, ordinary WHERE filtering, joins, and transactions together — no second system to run, sync, back up, or keep consistent.

That co-location is the killer feature. Filtering by metadata is just SQL (WHERE tenant = $1 AND created_at > $2 ORDER BY embedding <=> $query LIMIT 10), access control is your existing row security, and the vectors stay transactionally consistent with the source rows — when a document is deleted, its vector goes with it in the same transaction. For many teams, "just use pgvector" is the right first (and sometimes last) answer.

The limits appear at very large scale: a dedicated vector engine can index billions of vectors, offer richer quantization and distributed sharding, and tune ANN more aggressively than pgvector does inside a general-purpose database. So the rule of thumb: start with pgvector if you're already on Postgres and are below ~tens of millions of vectors; graduate to a dedicated store when scale, recall tuning, or vector-specific features demand it. Many production systems never need to graduate.

Why it matterspgvector lets you do vector search inside the database you already operate, with SQL filtering and transactional consistency — often the simplest correct choice up to tens of millions of vectors.

17Vector DBs · the hard part

Metadata filtering and the pre/post-filter problem

Analogy: finding the closest restaurant that is also open and vegetarian. Do you find the 10 closest and hope some qualify, or find all the qualifying ones and then sort by distance? The order changes the answer.

Real queries are rarely "nearest vectors" — they are "nearest vectors that also match a filter": this tenant, this date range, this document type, this permission. Combining an ANN index with metadata predicates is genuinely hard, and there are two naive strategies, each with a failure mode. Post-filtering: run ANN for the top-k, then drop results that fail the filter — simple, but if the filter is selective (few docs match), you might retrieve 10 and keep 1, or keep none.

Pre-filtering: restrict to matching documents first, then find the nearest among them — always returns a full set, but the ANN index can't prune as aggressively (the graph or cells were built over everything), so it can be much slower, sometimes degrading toward brute force over the filtered subset. Neither is free, and the right choice depends on how selective the filter is.

This is exactly where vector databases earn their keep: mature ones implement filtered ANN — pushing the predicate into the graph traversal or cell scan (filtered HNSW, per-cell filtering), so search stays fast even with selective filters. When you evaluate a vector DB, its filtered-search quality and speed matter more than raw unfiltered benchmarks, because real workloads always filter. 19Vector DBs

Scaling a vector store

Analogy: growing from a single filing cabinet to a warehouse: you split records across rooms (shards), keep copies for safety and speed (replicas), and compress folders to fit more (quantization).

As vectors grow from thousands to billions, a vector store scales along familiar database axes plus vector-specific ones. Sharding: partition vectors across nodes so each holds a slice; a query fans out to all shards and merges the top-k. Replication: keep copies of each shard for availability and read throughput. Quantization (PQ, scalar, binary): shrink vectors so more fit in RAM per node — often the highest-leverage scaling move, since memory is usually the binding constraint.

The core tension is memory vs recall vs latency. In-memory HNSW is fastest but caps how many vectors per node; quantization and disk-based indexes (DiskANN) trade a little recall/latency for far more capacity per dollar. You also decide freshness vs build cost: rebuilding an index is expensive, so systems support incremental inserts and background re-indexing (Track 05), and segment-based designs (like Lucene/Milvus) that add new data without rebuilding everything.

Operationally, watch the same signals as any database plus recall: query latency (p95/p99), recall against a ground-truth sample, memory per node, and index build time. The scaling recipe is usually: quantize to fit more per node, shard when one node is full, replicate for availability and QPS, and keep an eye on recall as you compress — because it's easy to scale capacity while silently degrading answer quality. That silent-recall-loss risk is why evaluation (Track 09) is not optional.

Track 05 — From documents to chunks

Chunking & ingestion

Before anything can be retrieved it has to be split, parsed, enriched and kept current. This track covers chunking strategy and the size/overlap trade, the messy reality of parsing real PDFs and tables, metadata and contextual enrichment, and the forever-pipeline of keeping the index fresh.

20Chunking

Splitting documents into chunks

Analogy: cutting a long document into index cards. Too big and each card is a vague blur; too small and a single idea gets split across two cards, so neither makes sense alone.

You rarely embed a whole document — you embed chunks, because one vector per 20-page document averages away all specifics, and because you want to retrieve the passage that answers the question, not the whole file. Chunking is the deceptively critical step where retrieval quality is quietly won or lost. The strategies form a ladder: fixed-size (every N tokens — simple, dumb), recursive (split on paragraph, then sentence boundaries to respect structure), and semantic (split where the topic shifts, using embedding similarity between sentences).

Two dials dominate: chunk size and overlap. Too large, and the embedding is diluted (many topics in one vector) and you waste prompt tokens; too small, and a single fact gets split across a boundary so no chunk fully answers the question. Overlap (repeating a bit of the previous chunk's tail) heals boundary seams so a fact straddling a cut still appears whole in one chunk. There is no universal size — it depends on your documents and your embedding model's ideal input length.

The professional stance: chunking is an empirical choice you evaluate, not a default you accept. Structure-aware splitting (by heading, by Markdown section, by code function) usually beats blind fixed windows, and keeping semantically coherent units together is what makes retrieval land on answerable passages. Parsing real documents — the messy-data reality

Analogy: RAG demos use clean text files; real corpora are scanned PDFs, spreadsheets, and slide decks — the difference between a recipe and a working kitchen.

The dirty secret of production RAG is that most effort goes into parsing, not vectors. Real knowledge lives in PDFs (often scanned images needing OCR), Word docs, HTML, spreadsheets, slides, and email — formats where the reading order, tables, columns, headers/footers, and figures are hard to extract as clean linear text. Garbled parsing (a two-column PDF read straight across, a table flattened into gibberish) poisons everything downstream: bad text makes bad chunks makes bad embeddings makes bad retrieval.

The toolkit has matured: layout-aware parsers (Unstructured, Docling, LlamaParse and similar) detect structure, preserve tables as Markdown/HTML, run OCR on scans, and emit clean, structured chunks with element types (title, table, list). Tables especially deserve care — you often serialize a table to Markdown or summarize it, because a raw grid of numbers embeds poorly. For scanned or complex documents, vision-language models increasingly parse the page directly.

The lesson is to treat ingestion as a first-class engineering problem, not a one-liner. Budget for it, evaluate parse quality on your actual documents (open the extracted text and read it), and handle the long tail of formats. A brilliant retrieval stack on badly parsed text underperforms a simple stack on clean text — garbage in, garbage retrieved. This is where many RAG projects silently fail.

Metadata and contextual enrichment

Analogy: labeling each index card with its book, chapter, and date — and adding a one-line note at the top so it makes sense even when pulled out of the drawer alone.

A chunk should carry more than its text. Attaching metadata — source document, section/title, page, timestamp, author, document type, and crucially permissions/tenant — is what enables filtered retrieval (Track 04), freshness, access control, and citations back to the source. Metadata is cheap to store and pays off at query time; design your schema up front (what will you filter and cite by?) rather than discovering you need it after indexing a million chunks.

A powerful recent technique is contextual retrieval (popularized by Anthropic's "contextual embeddings/BM25"): before embedding a chunk, prepend a short, LLM-generated sentence that situates it in the whole document ("This chunk is from the Q3 2026 earnings report, discussing cloud revenue"). This fixes a core problem — a chunk like "revenue grew 40%" is ambiguous alone, but with its context it embeds and retrieves far more accurately, cutting retrieval failures substantially.

The broader pattern is enrichment: generating titles, summaries, hypothetical questions, or keywords per chunk to give retrieval more surfaces to match on. It costs an LLM call per chunk at ingestion (offline, one-time) to buy better retrieval on every query (online, forever) — usually a great trade. The principle: the chunk you index need not be exactly the raw text; enrich it so it is self-contained and findable.

23Chunking

Keeping the index fresh

Analogy: a newspaper morgue that must file today's edition, correct yesterday's errata, and pull retracted stories — every day, without shutting the reading room.

A knowledge base is not static: documents are added, edited, and deleted, and RAG must reflect that or it confidently serves stale answers. Incremental indexing handles adds and updates without rebuilding everything — you embed and insert new/changed chunks and delete removed ones, ideally keyed by a stable document ID so an edit replaces exactly the old chunks. Getting deletes right matters: an orphaned vector for a deleted document is a source of wrong, un-attributable answers.

Two freshness traps. Re-embedding on model change: upgrading the embedding model invalidates the whole index (Track 02), so you re-embed the corpus and swap atomically — a planned, occasional, expensive event. Change detection: you need a pipeline that notices source changes (webhooks, change-data-capture, scheduled crawls, content hashes) and updates only what changed, rather than re-ingesting everything nightly. For fast-moving data, some systems add a "freshness" signal or time-decay so newer documents rank higher.

The mindset shift is from "index once" to "run an ingestion pipeline forever." Treat ingestion like ETL: idempotent updates keyed by document ID, deletes propagated, versioning so you can roll back, and monitoring for drift between the source of truth and the index. A RAG system's trustworthiness depends as much on this unglamorous freshness plumbing as on any clever retrieval trick.

Track 06 — Finding the right passages

Retrieval & hybrid search

The online heart of RAG: retrieving the top-k, why you need both semantic (dense) and keyword (sparse/BM25) search, fusing them with reciprocal rank fusion, learned-sparse SPLADE, and the last-mile craft of packing chunks into the prompt around the lost-in-the-middle effect.

24Retrieval

A query retrieves the top-k

Analogy: asking a librarian for "the few most relevant books." How many is "few," and how sure are you each one is actually relevant? Those two dials shape every answer.

The online path is short: embed the query with the same model used for documents, run ANN search in the vector store, and return the top-k most similar chunks. But "top-k" hides two consequential choices. k (how many chunks to retrieve) trades recall against precision and cost: a bigger k is more likely to include the right passage (higher recall) but also drags in irrelevant ones that distract the model and burn prompt tokens; a smaller k is cleaner but risks missing the answer.

The second choice is a similarity threshold: do you return the top-k regardless of score, or only chunks above some similarity? A threshold lets you return nothing when nothing is relevant — which is exactly what you want, because feeding the model weak matches invites hallucination ("here are some vaguely related passages, now answer confidently"). Being willing to retrieve zero and say "I don't know" is a feature, not a bug.

In practice, teams over-retrieve then filter: fetch a generous top-k (say 20–50) with recall as the goal, then use a reranker (Track 07) to sharpen down to the best 3–5 for the prompt. This two-stage shape — cheap high-recall retrieval, then expensive high-precision reranking — is the backbone of good RAG, and it's why the next track exists. First, though, retrieval quality depends on what you search with: dense vectors, keywords, or both.

25Retrieval · the key insight

Dense vs sparse — and why you want both

Analogy: two detectives: one recognizes faces by overall impression (dense/semantic), the other by exact fingerprints (sparse/keyword). Each solves cases the other can't.

There are two fundamentally different ways to search text, and they have complementary blind spots. Dense retrieval (embeddings) matches meaning — it finds "credential recovery" for "reset my password" even with no shared words. Sparse retrieval (keyword, classically BM25) matches exact tokens with clever term-frequency weighting — it nails rare identifiers, product codes, names, and exact phrases that embeddings blur together.

Where one is blind, the other sees. Dense misses "error E-4021" (rare tokens smear together in vector space) but sparse matches it exactly. Sparse misses paraphrases with no shared words (and typos, and synonyms) but dense handles them via meaning. Dense excels at natural-language questions; sparse excels at precise terminology, codes, and out-of-vocabulary terms your embedding model never learned. Neither alone is enough for real, mixed queries.

This is why serious production RAG runs both and combines them — hybrid search, next scenario. The insight to carry: semantic search is not a strict upgrade over keyword search; it is a different tool with different failures. 26Retrieval

Hybrid search and reciprocal rank fusion

Analogy: two judges scoring the same contestants. Rather than trusting one, you reward the contestants both judges rank highly — robust, and no need to calibrate their different scoring scales.

Hybrid search runs dense and sparse retrieval in parallel and merges their results, capturing both meaning and exact-match. The merge is the tricky part, because dense similarity scores and BM25 scores live on completely different, incomparable scales — you can't just add them. The elegant, dominant solution is Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF): ignore the raw scores entirely and fuse by rank. Each document gets a score of ∑ 1/(k + rank) across the lists it appears in (k is a small constant, often 60); sum across lists; sort.

RRF's beauty is that it needs no score normalization or tuning: a document ranked highly by both retrievers rises to the top, while one ranked #1 by only a single retriever is treated with healthy skepticism. It is robust, parameter-light, and works across any number of retrievers (you can fuse dense + BM25 + a third signal). Weighted variants let you favor one retriever, but plain RRF is a strong default that "just works."

The result is retrieval that is more than the sum of its parts: measurable recall gains over either method alone, especially on mixed workloads (natural questions plus exact terms). Most vector databases and frameworks now offer hybrid search with RRF built in. The takeaway: don't choose dense or sparse — fuse them, and let RRF handle the score-scale mismatch. SPLADE and learned sparse retrieval

Analogy: a keyword search that also knows synonyms — it indexes not just the words that are there, but the words that should be there too.

Classic BM25 matches only the exact terms present in the text, so it misses synonyms and related terms. Learned sparse retrieval — the best-known is SPLADE — keeps the efficiency and exact-match strength of a sparse, inverted-index representation but uses a transformer to expand each document (and query) into a weighted set of vocabulary terms, including relevant words that don't literally appear. A document about "cars" might get weighted terms for "vehicle," "automobile," "engine" added, so a keyword-style match now catches semantic variants.

The appeal is having your cake and eating it: sparse representations run on the mature, fast, exact inverted-index machinery (and stay interpretable — you can see which terms matched), while gaining some of dense retrieval's synonym-awareness. SPLADE and neural sparse models often land between BM25 and dense on quality, and some hybrid stacks fuse all three (BM25 + SPLADE + dense) for maximum recall.

The trade-offs: learned sparse needs a model to expand text (more compute at index and query time than BM25), and the expanded postings lists can be larger. It is not always necessary — plain hybrid (BM25 + dense) is the common baseline — but learned sparse is a strong option when exact-match interpretability plus synonym-awareness both matter, e.g. technical or enterprise search. Know it exists so you can reach for it when BM25 is too literal and dense is too blurry.

Top-k, thresholds and context packing

Analogy: packing a suitcase with a strict weight limit — and knowing that things buried in the middle get forgotten, so you put the essentials where you'll actually see them.

Once you have ranked chunks, you must decide what actually goes in the prompt — context packing — and it has real subtleties. First, the token budget: retrieved context competes with the system prompt, the query, and the model's output for the context window and for cost, so you pack the best few, not everything you retrieved. More is not better past a point.

Second, the "lost in the middle" effect: models attend best to the beginning and end of their context and reliably worse to the middle. So the order of packed chunks matters — a common trick is to place the most relevant chunks at the very start and very end, not buried in the center. Dumping 50 chunks in ranked order can actually hurt, because the model's attention skips the middle where a key passage might sit.

Third, deduplication and diversity: near-duplicate chunks waste budget and bias the answer; some systems apply MMR (maximal marginal relevance) to pick chunks that are relevant and diverse. And the threshold decision returns here: if nothing clears the bar, pack nothing and let the model say it doesn't know. Context packing is the quiet last mile where good retrieval is either delivered to the model well or squandered.

Track 07 — Making retrieval reliable

Advanced retrieval

The techniques that separate a demo from a dependable system: reranking with cross-encoders (usually the biggest win), ColBERT late interaction, query transformation (HyDE, multi-query), decomposition and routing for hard questions, and small-to-big retrieval that decouples what you search from what you read.

29Advanced retrieval · the biggest win

Reranking — a second, sharper pass

Analogy: a hiring funnel: a keyword screen surfaces 100 resumes fast, then a human reads each one closely to pick the top 5. The cheap filter finds candidates; the careful read gets the order right.

First-stage retrieval (ANN over embeddings) is fast because it compares independent vectors — the query is embedded once, documents are embedded once, and you compare. That's a bi-encoder, and its speed comes at a cost: query and document never "see" each other, so subtle relevance is blurred. A reranker is a cross-encoder: it feeds the query and each candidate document together through a transformer, so attention can directly compare them — producing a far sharper relevance score.

The catch is cost: a cross-encoder must run once per (query, document) pair, so you can't run it over the whole corpus. The standard pattern solves this in two stages: cheap bi-encoder retrieval fetches a generous candidate set (top-50 to top-100), then the expensive cross-encoder reranks just those down to the best 3–5 that go in the prompt. You get bi-encoder speed at corpus scale and cross-encoder precision at the top.

Reranking is often the single highest-ROI upgrade to a naive RAG system, because it fixes the most common failure — the right passage was retrieved but ranked #14, below noise. Strong open rerankers (bge-reranker, and cross-encoder families) and hosted ones (Cohere Rerank, Jina) are easy to drop in. The rule: retrieve for recall, rerank for precision. ColBERT and late interaction

Analogy: instead of summarizing two books into one sentence each and comparing the sentences, you compare them paragraph-by-paragraph — more work, but you catch matches a summary would miss.

Standard dense retrieval squashes a whole chunk into one vector, losing token-level detail. ColBERT takes a middle path called late interaction: it keeps a separate vector per token (a multi-vector representation) for both query and document. At scoring time it uses MaxSim — for each query token, find its best-matching document token, then sum those maxima. This lets fine-grained term matches contribute that a single pooled vector would blur away, giving retrieval quality closer to a cross-encoder.

Crucially, the expensive query-document interaction is late — document token vectors are precomputed and indexed offline, and only the cheap MaxSim runs at query time. So ColBERT gets much of a cross-encoder's precision at closer to bi-encoder speed — a genuine sweet spot. It can serve as a strong first-stage retriever or a reranker.

The cost is storage and complexity: many vectors per document instead of one means a bigger index and specialized machinery (PLAID and similar optimize this). ColBERT (v2) is a notable open model, and late-interaction ideas are spreading (including to multimodal, e.g. ColPali for documents-as-images, Track 08). Know it as the "token-level, high-precision" retrieval option when single-vector dense isn't sharp enough and full cross-encoder reranking is too slow at your recall depth.

Query transformation — HyDE, multi-query, expansion

Analogy: a good librarian doesn't search your exact words — they rephrase your fuzzy question into the terms the archive actually uses, and try a few angles.

Users ask short, ambiguous, or oddly-phrased questions that don't retrieve well as-is. Query transformation rewrites the query before retrieval to bridge the gap. Multi-query: have an LLM generate several rephrasings of the question, retrieve for each, and union the results — catching documents that only match one phrasing. Query expansion: add synonyms and related terms so retrieval casts a wider net.

HyDE (Hypothetical Document Embeddings) is the clever one: instead of embedding the question, ask the LLM to write a hypothetical answer to it, then embed and search with that. The insight is that a fake answer — even if factually wrong — lives near real answers in embedding space (answers resemble answers), far closer than a terse question does. So searching with a hallucinated answer often retrieves the true documents better than searching with the query itself.

These techniques trade extra LLM calls and latency for higher recall on hard queries, so you apply them selectively (they're overkill for easy lookups). They pair naturally with reranking — cast a wide net with transformed queries, then rerank the union down to the best few. Query decomposition and routing

Analogy: a good research assistant splits "compare our 2025 and 2026 revenue and explain the change" into three tasks, and sends each to the right filing cabinet.

Some questions cannot be answered by one retrieval, no matter how good. Multi-hop questions ("which of our products launched after the CEO who wrote the 2024 memo joined?") need intermediate facts. Comparative and aggregative questions ("compare X and Y", "summarize all complaints about Z") need multiple, separate retrievals combined. Query decomposition breaks a complex query into sub-questions, retrieves for each, and composes the answer — turning one impossible search into several tractable ones.

Query routing is the complementary skill: send the query to the right source. A production system often has multiple indexes (docs, code, tickets, a SQL database, a web search) and a router — usually an LLM classifier — picks which to query, or whether to use RAG at all (a chit-chat message needs no retrieval). Routing also chooses strategy: a factual lookup vs a broad "themes" question (which might go to GraphRAG, Track 08) call for different retrieval.

A related trick is step-back prompting: before retrieving for a very specific question, ask a more general version first ("what are the principles of X?") to pull in foundational context, then retrieve the specifics. Together, decomposition and routing move RAG from "one query, one search" toward a small reasoning process over retrieval — which is the on-ramp to agentic RAG (Track 08). The cost is latency and orchestration complexity, so reserve them for genuinely hard queries.

33Advanced retrieval

Contextual and parent-document retrieval

Analogy: you search by the sharp, specific sentence — but hand the model the whole surrounding paragraph, so it has context, not just the hit.

There is a tension in chunk size: small chunks retrieve better (a focused sentence embeds precisely and matches the query sharply), but large chunks generate better (the model needs surrounding context to actually answer). Small-to-big / parent-document retrieval resolves it: index and search over small chunks for precision, but when one matches, return its larger parent chunk (or a window around it) to the LLM. You search narrow and read wide.

Variants abound. Sentence-window retrieval indexes individual sentences but returns the sentences on either side. Parent-document retrieval indexes child chunks that point to a parent document/section returned on a hit. Auto-merging retrieves leaf chunks but, if several children of the same parent are hit, returns the merged parent. All share the same idea: decouple the retrieval unit from the generation unit.

This pairs with contextual enrichment (Track 05): you can retrieve on a context-augmented small chunk and still hand over the full parent. The broader principle across advanced retrieval is that the thing you search need not be the thing you read — you can transform the query, expand it, rerank results, and decouple retrieval granularity from generation granularity, all to get the right, well-contextualized evidence in front of the model. These are the levers that turn a mediocre RAG system into a reliable one.

GraphRAG & agentic

Where RAG grows past "one query, top-k chunks": GraphRAG for connected, big-picture questions a knowledge graph can answer; agentic RAG where the model grades its own evidence and retries; multimodal RAG over images and document pages; and memory, which is simply retrieval pointed at the user.

34GraphRAG & agentic

GraphRAG — retrieval over a knowledge graph

Analogy: plain RAG hands you a few relevant pages; GraphRAG first draws the map of who-relates-to-what, so it can answer "what's the big picture?" — a question no single page contains.

Vector RAG retrieves independent chunks by similarity, which is great for "find the passage about X" but fails at questions that require connecting many pieces: "what are the main themes across these 500 documents?" or "how is person A connected to project B?" No single chunk holds the answer, and top-k similarity can't assemble it. GraphRAG (notably Microsoft's) addresses this by building a knowledge graph first: an LLM extracts entities and relationships from the corpus into nodes and edges.

Then it runs community detection to cluster the graph into groups of related entities, and pre-generates a summary for each community. Now two query modes exist. Local (entity-centric) questions hop across the graph from an entity through its relations — strong for multi-hop ("who did X work with on Y?"). Global (corpus-wide) questions like "what are the overarching themes?" are answered by combining the community summaries — something flat vector RAG structurally cannot do.

The trade is cost and complexity: building the graph means many LLM extraction calls over the whole corpus (expensive, offline), and maintaining it as data changes is nontrivial. Recent benchmarks find GraphRAG shines on global/aggregative and multi-hop questions but is overkill for simple lookups — so many systems route (Track 07) between vector RAG and GraphRAG by question type. Know it as the tool for connected, big-picture questions. Toggle local vs global in the animation.

35GraphRAG & agentic

Agentic RAG — the model drives retrieval

Analogy: the difference between a vending machine (one request, one grab) and a detective who follows leads, checks whether the evidence holds up, and goes back for more if it doesn't.

Naive RAG retrieves once and hopes the top-k contained the answer. Agentic RAG makes retrieval a loop the model controls: the LLM decides whether to retrieve, what to search for, evaluates what came back, and retries if the evidence is weak — before answering. This turns a fixed pipeline into a small reasoning process, and it fixes the biggest naive-RAG failure: confidently answering from a bad or empty retrieval.

Named patterns formalize this. Self-RAG trains the model to emit reflection tokens deciding when to retrieve and whether passages are relevant and supported. Corrective RAG (CRAG) grades retrieved documents and, if they're poor, falls back to a web search or query rewrite. Iterative / multi-step RAG lets the agent retrieve, reason, and retrieve again for multi-hop questions. In an agent framework, "retrieve" is simply a tool the model calls as many times as needed.

The payoff is reliability — the system refuses to answer on weak evidence and can recover from a bad first search — at the cost of latency and tokens (multiple LLM and retrieval round-trips) and orchestration complexity. So you reserve the full loop for high-stakes or hard queries and keep a fast path for easy ones. Multimodal RAG

Analogy: your knowledge isn't only text — it's charts, screenshots, scanned forms and diagrams. Multimodal RAG lets you search and answer over the pictures too.

A great deal of real knowledge is not plain text: charts, tables, diagrams, screenshots, product photos, scanned forms, slides. Text-only RAG either ignores these or relies on a lossy text extraction. Multimodal RAG retrieves over images (and other modalities) directly. The classic approach embeds images and text into a shared multimodal space (CLIP-style models) so a text query can retrieve relevant images, or generates text descriptions/summaries of images to embed and search.

A powerful newer approach for documents is treating the page as an image: models like ColPali use late interaction (Track 07) over image patches to retrieve document pages visually, sidestepping brittle PDF parsing entirely — the layout, tables and figures are preserved because you never flattened them to text. Generation then uses a vision-language model that can "read" the retrieved page image and answer, including from its charts and tables.

The trade-offs are model choice (a multimodal embedder and a vision-capable generator), bigger storage, and evaluation that must cover visual grounding. But for domains where the answer lives in a figure or a scanned table — finance, insurance, engineering, medicine — multimodal RAG is transformative, retrieving what text extraction would have destroyed. It's the frontier where RAG meets vision, and it's moving fast.

Why it mattersMuch real knowledge lives in figures and scans that text extraction destroys — multimodal RAG (and page-as-image retrieval like ColPali) searches and answers over the visuals directly.

Memory and personalization as retrieval

Analogy: an assistant who remembers your past conversations and preferences — not by re-reading everything, but by looking up the few relevant memories when they matter.

RAG's pattern — "store knowledge externally, retrieve the relevant bit at inference" — is exactly what an AI system needs for long-term memory and personalization. Instead of documents, you store memories: past conversation turns, user preferences, facts learned about the user, prior decisions. At each turn you retrieve the memories relevant to the current message and inject them, so the assistant appears to "remember" without stuffing the entire history into every prompt.

This is retrieval with a twist: memories are per-user (strict tenant isolation — never leak one user's memory to another), written continuously (the system decides what's worth remembering, summarizes, deduplicates, and updates or forgets stale facts), and often typed (episodic events vs semantic facts vs preferences). Open frameworks (Mem0, Letta/MemGPT-style, Zep) formalize this memory layer on top of a vector store, handling extraction, consolidation and retrieval of memories.

The design challenges are real: deciding what to remember (not everything), keeping memory consistent (contradictions, updates, forgetting), and privacy (memory is sensitive personal data — retention, deletion, isolation all matter). But the architecture is pure RAG. Recognizing that memory is retrieval means every technique in this guide — embeddings, filtering, reranking, freshness, access control — applies directly to building agents that remember. It's the same machine, pointed at the user instead of a document corpus.

Track 09 — Did it actually work?

Evaluation & failure modes

RAG fails silently, so measurement is the job. The two-place model (retrieval vs generation), the IR metrics for retrieval (recall@k, MRR, NDCG) and the groundedness metrics for generation (Ragas, the RAG triad), the seven concrete failure modes with their fixes, and the security fronts — access control and injection — most guides skip.

38Evaluation · the core insight

RAG can be wrong in two places

Analogy: a relay race with two runners. If the first drops the baton (retrieval) or the second runs the wrong way (generation), you lose — and "we lost" doesn't tell you which runner to coach.

The single most important idea in RAG evaluation: a RAG answer passes through two independent stages, each of which can fail on its own. Retrieval can fail — the evidence needed simply wasn't in the top-k. Generation can fail — the evidence was retrieved, but the model ignored it, misread it, or added unsupported claims. A correct answer requires both to succeed, and a wrong answer could be either's fault.

This is why a single "accuracy" number is nearly useless for debugging RAG — it hides which half broke. You must measure them separately. Retrieval quality: did the right chunks get retrieved and ranked well (recall@k, precision, MRR/NDCG)? Generation quality: given the retrieved context, is the answer faithful (grounded in it, not hallucinated) and relevant (actually addresses the question)? Only with both do you know whether to fix chunking/retrieval or prompting/generation.

The two-place model also names the four outcomes: good retrieval + faithful generation = correct; bad retrieval + any generation = answer built on nothing (best case, an honest "I don't know"); good retrieval + unfaithful generation = the maddening case where the answer was right there and the model still got it wrong. Internalize this split and RAG debugging becomes systematic instead of guesswork. Toggle the two stages in the animation to see all four outcomes.

Evaluating retrieval

Analogy: grading a search engine: of the pages that truly matter, how many did it find (recall), and did it put them near the top (ranking)?

Retrieval evaluation borrows decades of information-retrieval metrics, and you need a small golden set to use them: queries paired with their known-relevant chunks. Recall@k: of all the relevant chunks, what fraction appear in the top-k? This is usually the headline — if the answer isn't retrieved, nothing downstream can fix it. Precision@k: of the top-k returned, what fraction are relevant (noise dilutes the prompt). Together they capture "did we find it, without too much junk."

Ranking-aware metrics matter because order feeds reranking and packing. MRR (Mean Reciprocal Rank) rewards putting the first relevant result high. NDCG (Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain) rewards relevant results near the top and handles graded relevance (some chunks more relevant than others). A system with great recall@50 but terrible MRR is telling you to add a reranker; great recall@5 means retrieval is already sharp.

The practical challenge is building the golden set without hand-labeling forever: you can bootstrap with an LLM proposing relevant chunks (then spot-checking), mine real user queries, and grow it over time from production failures (Track 09's continuous-improvement loop). The payoff is that retrieval becomes a dial you can tune with numbers — try a new embedding model, chunk size, or index, and see recall@k move — instead of eyeballing a few examples and hoping.

Why it mattersRetrieval metrics (recall@k, MRR, NDCG) on a golden set turn retrieval into something you tune with numbers — and recall@k is the ceiling, since nothing downstream can recover an un-retrieved answer.

Evaluating generation — faithfulness and relevance

Analogy: grading an essay written with open books: did the student actually use the sources (faithful), and did they answer the question asked (relevant), rather than write something fluent but unsupported?

Given the retrieved context, generation evaluation asks two things. Faithfulness / groundedness: is every claim in the answer supported by the retrieved context, or did the model add unsupported (hallucinated) statements? This is the crucial RAG metric — the whole point of RAG is grounded answers, so an unfaithful answer is a failure even if it happens to be true. Answer relevance: does the response actually address the user's question, or does it drift?

The open-source framework here is Ragas (with peers DeepEval, promptfoo, TruLens, Phoenix), which computes exactly these: faithfulness, answer relevance, and context precision/recall — often using an LLM-as-judge to check whether each answer claim is entailed by the context. The "RAG triad" is a useful frame: context relevance (is the retrieved context on-topic?), groundedness (is the answer supported by it?), and answer relevance (does it address the query?) — three checks that localize failures.

LLM-as-judge scales this but has known biases (Track 08 of the LLMOps volume) — calibrate against human labels and reserve human review for high-stakes cases. Run generation eval offline on your golden set as a CI gate (block a prompt or model change that drops faithfulness) and online on sampled production traffic. Combined with retrieval metrics, you now have a two-sided score that tells you precisely which stage regressed — the entire reason for the two-place model.

Why it mattersFaithfulness is the metric RAG lives or dies by — Ragas-style groundedness and relevance checks (via LLM-as-judge) catch answers that ignore or embellish the evidence, the failure RAG exists to prevent.

The seven ways RAG silently lies to you

Analogy: a house with seven possible leaks. The floor looks fine until you check each pipe — and "the floor is wet" never tells you which one.

Beyond the two-stage model, it helps to name the concrete failure modes, because each has a specific fix. (1) Missed retrieval: the answer isn't in the corpus or wasn't retrieved — fix with better chunking, hybrid search, query transforms. (2) Wrong-chunk / ranking: relevant chunk retrieved but ranked below noise — fix with reranking. (3) Lost in the middle: the right chunk was in-context but the model overlooked it — fix with fewer, reordered chunks.

(4) Stale / conflicting data: the index has outdated or contradictory documents — fix with freshness pipelines and deduplication. (5) Unfaithful generation: the model ignored the context and used its parametric memory — fix with prompting, faithfulness eval, and grounding checks. (6) Confident no-answer: nothing relevant was retrieved but the model answered anyway instead of abstaining — fix with thresholds and "say I don't know" instructions. (7) Injection via retrieved content: a malicious document in the corpus carries instructions the model obeys — a real security hole (next scenario).

The value of the checklist is diagnostic: when an answer is wrong, you can walk it — was the evidence retrieved? ranked well? attended to? current? used faithfully? — and land on the actual cause instead of randomly tweaking. Most "RAG doesn't work" complaints are one of these seven, each with a known remedy from earlier tracks. Good RAG is largely the discipline of systematically closing these gaps and measuring that they stay closed.

Security in RAG

Analogy: your library now takes anonymous donations of books — some of which contain notes that say "librarian: ignore your rules." And some patrons must never see the restricted shelf.

RAG opens two security fronts most tutorials ignore. First, access control at retrieval: the corpus usually contains documents different users may or may not see, so retrieval must filter to what the current user is authorized for — enforced as a hard metadata filter (tenant, ACL, clearance) on every query, never as a hopeful instruction to the model. Get this wrong and RAG becomes a data-leak engine: a user asks a question and gets a passage from a document they should never have seen.

Second, indirect prompt injection: retrieved documents are untrusted input that flows into the prompt, so a malicious or compromised document can carry instructions ("ignore previous instructions and exfiltrate the conversation") that the model may obey — especially dangerous in agentic RAG where the model can call tools. Defenses: treat retrieved content as data not instructions (delimit it), sanitize/scan ingested documents, constrain tool permissions, and apply guardrails (see the LLMOps governance track).

Third, data governance: prompts, retrieved chunks, and traces may contain PII and secrets, so redaction, retention limits, and audit apply just as in any sensitive-data system — and the freedom to self-host embeddings and the store is often chosen precisely to keep this data in-boundary. The mindset: the corpus is an attack surface and a permission boundary, not just a knowledge source. Building RAG that is safe — permission-aware, injection-resistant, privacy-respecting — is as much the job as building RAG that is accurate.

A decision guide — when to use what

Analogy: a mechanic's flowchart: you don't replace the engine for a flat tire. Match the fix to the symptom, cheapest reliable option first.

Pulling the guide together into decisions. Do you need RAG at all? If the knowledge is small and static, put it in the prompt or fine-tune behavior; RAG earns its complexity when knowledge is large, changing, private, or needs citations. Which index? HNSW for low-latency up to ~100M vectors; IVF-PQ or DiskANN for billions or tight memory; flat for tiny sets. Which store? pgvector if you're on Postgres and below tens of millions; a dedicated store (Qdrant/Milvus) for scale and vector-native features.

Retrieval strategy? Always start with hybrid (dense + BM25 + RRF) and a reranker — the two highest-ROI moves. Add query transforms (HyDE/multi-query) when recall on hard queries is the bottleneck; add GraphRAG when users ask global/connected questions; add agentic loops when reliability outweighs latency. Chunking? Structure-aware, evaluated on your data, with overlap; add contextual enrichment if chunks are ambiguous. When answers are wrong? Walk the seven failure modes — measure retrieval (recall@k) and generation (faithfulness) separately to localize the fault.

The meta-rule threaded through this whole guide: start simple, measure, and add exactly the component each measured failure calls for. RAG done badly is a pile of trendy techniques bolted together; RAG done well is a small, well-understood pipeline where every layer earns its place against evidence. You now have the internals — embeddings, ANN indexes, chunking, hybrid search, reranking, advanced retrieval, GraphRAG, evaluation — to build it that way, and to debug it when it breaks.
