The Hohmann Transfer: Why You Speed Up to Reach a Slower, Higher Orbit

You’re in one circular orbit and you want a higher one. Every instinct from flying — point where you want to go and thrust — is exactly wrong in orbit. The cheapest way up is two carefully timed prograde burns joined by a coasting half-ellipse, and the punchline is a paradox: you fire your engine to add speed, yet you arrive in an orbit that is slower than the one you left. Learn this one ellipse and you can budget almost any orbit change.

≈ 17 min read orbital mechanics · astrodynamics · mission design companion to The Rocket Equation and Control Systems

You are in a circular orbit — low Earth orbit, say — and you need to be in a higher circular one, like the geostationary belt, and the instinct you bring from every aircraft you have ever flown is precisely the wrong one. In the air you pitch up, thrust toward where you want to go, and climb; try that in orbit and thrusting straight at your destination mostly just tips your circle into an ellipse that swings right back to where you started, buying no permanent altitude at all. The bind is real: an orbit is not a place you steer toward but a shape you are locked into, and on top of that every scrap of velocity you can spend is exponentially expensive in propellant (the rocket equation’s logarithm, Deep Dive #04), so you cannot afford to thrash. The escape is a single reframe — stop aiming at the destination and instead change the energy of the orbit you are on, twice — and from that one move the whole subject falls out: a transfer ellipse that just kisses both circles, a prograde burn at the bottom to get onto it and a second prograde burn at the top to circularize, every speed on it read from one equation (vis-viva), a transfer time that is simply half the ellipse’s period, and a sting in the tail that trips everyone — you fire the engine to add speed at both burns, yet you end up in an orbit moving slower than the one you left, because the price of altitude is being slow. Then the piece turns to the half the textbooks skip: how you plan a real maneuver and build its Δv budget, how it goes wrong (phasing, plane changes, finite burns), what to spec, and a full LEO→GEO raise worked end to end.

1The constraint: you can’t just fly up

Start with exactly what you are being asked to do. You are in a circular orbit — low Earth orbit — and you need to be in a higher circular one, the geostationary belt. Every instinct from flying says: pitch up, thrust toward the destination, climb. In orbit that instinct fails completely. Thrust radially outward and you mostly just tilt your circle into an ellipse that comes right back around to where you started; you have bought yourself a brief excursion in altitude and no permanent gain at all. There is no “up” to hold onto, only the orbit you are on.

And there is a second, harsher constraint layered on top: the fuel. Deep Dive #04 showed that Δv — the total change in velocity you can buy — is exponentially expensive in propellant (the rocket equation’s logarithm), so you cannot afford to flail around spending velocity on maneuvers that do not stick. The real question is therefore sharp, and the whole piece answers it: what is the cheapest way, in Δv, to move between two circular orbits? Not a way — the way the physics itself certifies as minimal.

2The reframe: change the orbit’s energy, then fix it up

The key realization is that in orbit you do not steer toward a place — you change the shape and energy of the orbit you are on, and let the orbit carry you there. Two facts drive everything. The first is that an orbit’s size is set by its energy: add energy by burning prograde (along your direction of motion) and the orbit grows, its far side swinging higher; remove energy by burning retrograde and it shrinks. The second is that on a circular orbit, speed falls as you go higher — the circular speed is set entirely by the radius:

vcirc = √(μ/r)μ is the gravitational parameter, r the orbit radius — bigger r, smaller v

High orbits are slow orbits. Put those two facts together and the strategy writes itself. To get from a low circle to a high circle, do this: at your current altitude, burn prograde to add energy — this raises the opposite side of your orbit, turning the circle into an ellipse whose far point (apogee) reaches the target altitude. Then coast — for free, no fuel — all the way up that ellipse to apogee. Now you are at the right altitude but moving too slowly to hold a circle there (you are sitting at the slow, far end of an ellipse), so you burn prograde again to add exactly the speed that circularizes you. Two prograde burns, one coasting ellipse. You never once “aimed up”; you re-shaped the orbit twice and let gravity do the climbing.

3The core: the transfer ellipse and its two burns

Now make it exact. Connect the inner circle (radius r1) and the outer circle (radius r2) with a single transfer ellipse that just kisses both: its perigee touches the inner orbit and its apogee touches the outer. An ellipse’s size is fixed by its semi-major axis, and for a tangent ellipse that reaches from r1 to r2 the semi-major axis is simply the average of the two radii:

a = r1 + r22the ellipse spans from the inner circle to the outer, so its mean radius is theirs

The one equation that gives the speed anywhere on any orbit — circle, ellipse, the lot — is vis-viva, and it is the single relation worth carrying out of this piece:

v = √( μ·(2/r − 1/a) )speed at radius r on an orbit of semi-major axis a

Apply vis-viva at the two tangent points and compare it to the circular speed each place needs. At perigee (r = r1) the ellipse carries you at vp = √(μ(2/r1 − 1/a)), while the circle there needs only v1 = √(μ/r1), and since vp > v1 you must add speed to get onto the ellipse:

Δv1 = vp − v1prograde boost at perigee, to climb onto the transfer ellipse

At apogee (r = r2) the ellipse has slowed you to va = √(μ(2/r2 − 1/a)), but a circle there needs v2 = √(μ/r2), and now va < v2, so you add speed again to circularize:

Δv2 = v2 − vaprograde boost at apogee, to turn the ellipse into the higher circle
Δv = Δv1 + Δv2the whole price of the transfer, in velocity

And the trip itself takes exactly half the ellipse’s orbital period — you ride the ellipse from perigee to apogee and no further:

t = π·√( a3/μ )half the period of the transfer ellipse

This two-tangent-burn ellipse is the Hohmann transfer, and it is the minimum-Δv way to move between two coplanar circular orbits — for radius ratios up to about 11.9, past which something cleverer wins (§4). It is worth setting the whole payoff apart, because everything downstream is a consequence of it.

The whole transfer, in one ellipse and two burns
a = (r1 + r2)/2
v = √( μ·(2/r − 1/a) )

Join the two circles with an ellipse tangent to both (semi-major axis the average of the radii). Burn prograde at perigee to get on it (Δv1 = vp − v1), coast free to apogee, burn prograde again to circularize (Δv2 = v2 − va). Every speed comes from the one equation, vis-viva; the trip takes t = π√(a3/μ). It is the cheapest two-impulse transfer between circular orbits.

Two prograde burns, one coasting ellipse

Drag the two orbit radii and watch the transfer ellipse re-form between them, its perigee glued to the inner circle and its apogee to the outer. The two Δv readouts update live — a prograde kick at the bottom to climb onto the ellipse, a second prograde kick at the top to circularize — along with the transfer time t = π√(a3/μ) stretching out as the ellipse grows. Keep an eye on the speeds: the higher circle always reads the lower circular speed, even though you added velocity at both burns to get there. That contradiction-in-plain-sight is the whole paradox of §4, and the next sim pins it to a curve.

4Limit checks — read the transfer at its ends

The transfer earns its keep at the extremes, and reading it there is the fastest way to see both where the cheapness comes from and where the famous paradox hides.

Limit check — read the transfer at its ends No change (r2 = r1): the ellipse collapses onto the circle, a = r1 and vp = va = v1 = v2, so Δv = 0 — nothing to do, exactly as it must be. Going down (r2 < r1): the identical geometry runs in reverse and both burns become retrograde — you slow down to drop into a lower, faster orbit. De-orbiting is a Hohmann run backwards. The paradox, sharpened: you add speed at both burns (both Δv’s are prograde), yet the destination circular speed is lower than where you began (v2 < v1). You spend velocity to end up slower — because the burns pour energy into altitude, and the price of altitude is being slow. Energy up, speed down. Huge ratios (r2/r1 beyond ≈ 11.94): the Hohmann stops being optimal — a bi-elliptic transfer (fling apogee far past r2, nudge at that distant point, then drop back in) can cost less total Δv, trading a much longer trip for cheaper burns. For most real transfers the ratio is small and Hohmann wins. Transfer time scales as a3/2: a LEO→GEO hop is about 5 hours; an Earth→Mars Hohmann is about 8–9 months. The coast is free in fuel but expensive in time.
Higher orbit = slower — yet both burns add speed

Here is the paradox made visible. The curve is circular speed vcirc = √(μ/r) falling off as 1/√r: slide out to a bigger radius and the circle you would sit on is unarguably slower. Now watch the two burns drawn as upward jumps — at perigee you leap up from v1 to the ellipse speed vp, and at apogee you leap up again from va to v2. Both arrows point up; you added speed twice. Yet the point you land on, v2, sits below where you started, v1, because it lives far out on the falling curve. Energy up, speed down — the whole thing in one picture.

Three consequences the clean ellipse forces on any real mission, none of which appear in the two-burn arithmetic. First, phasing and the launch window: the transfer only works if the target is at the right place when you arrive at apogee, so you must depart at the one phase angle that puts the destination there half an orbit later. That single geometric fact is why interplanetary launches have windows that open roughly every 26 months for Mars, and why a GEO insertion is timed rather than fired on a whim — the ellipse is cheap, but you may wait a long time to start it. Second, plane changes are brutally expensive, and you do them where you are slowest: tilting the orbit costs Δv roughly proportional to your current speed, so you fold it into the apogee burn (the slow, high end), never the fast perigee end. A pure plane change is one of the most expensive maneuvers in all of spaceflight.

Third, real burns are not impulses. The vis-viva math assumes instantaneous velocity changes, but an engine pushes for minutes, so you pay small gravity and steering losses on top of the ideal numbers — and low-thrust electric propulsion cannot do a Hohmann at all. With only milli-newtons of thrust it spirals outward continuously over months instead of kicking twice: a genuinely different trajectory, and for high specific impulse often far cheaper in propellant. The two-impulse ellipse is the idealization every real transfer is measured against, not the one every vehicle actually flies.

5Where it lives: planning a maneuver, and how it goes wrong

The Hohmann transfer is the first thing you compute when planning almost any orbit change — satellite deployment and station-keeping, GEO insertion, raising or lowering a constellation, rendezvous and docking approach, and the first cut of any lunar or interplanetary trajectory. The sequence is fixed, not a flash of insight: establish r1 and r2; compute a, the apsis speeds, and the two Δv’s; add that into your Δv budget (which feeds straight into the rocket equation and the propellant mass, Deep Dive #04); compute the transfer time and the required phase angle / launch window; and add margins for plane change, finite-burn losses, and navigation. When it goes wrong, the failure modes are specific and each is really one of the core facts talking back:

Arrive at the right altitude, but the target isn’t there
A phasing error — the departure phase angle was wrong, or the window was missed. The ellipse is correct; the timing is not. You either wait for the geometry to come round again or pay for a costly phasing maneuver.
Δv budget blows up on an inclined target
An unbudgeted plane change. The fix is placement, not brute force: combine it with the apogee burn, where you are slowest and a degree of tilt is cheapest, rather than doing it as a separate maneuver down low.
The transfer “costs more than Hohmann”
Either your burns were not purely tangential / prograde (any off-axis component is wasted Δv), or the radius ratio is huge and a bi-elliptic would have been cheaper. Check both before you accept the number.
Didn’t circularize
You nailed the apogee altitude but skipped or under-sized Δv2, so you are still on the ellipse and fall right back down to perigee. Both burns are mandatory — reaching the altitude is only half the job.
Low-thrust stage can’t execute it
An electric / ion stage cannot deliver an impulsive apogee kick; it must be planned as a slow spiral, a different trajectory entirely. Hohmann-planning a low-thrust vehicle is a category error, not a tuning problem.

6How the transfer shapes the mission

The Hohmann numbers ripple outward through the whole spacecraft and mission design; they are never just a line in a maneuver table. The Δv budget the transfer produces sets, through the rocket equation, the propellant fraction and therefore much of the vehicle’s mass and cost — a bigger orbit raise is exponentially more propellant, so “how high” is really “how much fuel.” The transfer time (scaling as a3/2) sets mission duration, crew consumables, and radiation exposure, and it forces the launch-window cadence that dominates interplanetary scheduling. The decision of where to spend plane-change Δv — at the slow apogee — shapes the entire burn plan. And the choice of propulsion type forks the whole architecture: high-thrust chemical does crisp Hohmann impulses (fast, higher Δv), while high-Isp electric does long spirals (slow, less propellant) — a trade between time and mass decided largely by this one maneuver. In short, “how do we get to that orbit” is answered first by a Hohmann calculation, and everything downstream sizes to its output.

7Where it’s used, and what to spec

It governs satellite orbit-raising and GEO insertion, constellation deployment and disposal, rendezvous and docking approach ellipses, lunar and interplanetary first-cut trajectories, and de-orbit planning. When you plan one, these are the numbers that actually decide the outcome:

Δv1, Δv2 and total Δv
The maneuver’s price, and the single most important output — fed straight into the rocket equation to size propellant. Everything else is secondary to this number.
Transfer time t = π√(a3/μ)
Mission duration and the coast you must survive — power, thermal, consumables, radiation. Free in fuel, paid in time.
Phase angle / launch window
When you must depart so the target is there on arrival. Sets scheduling and cadence, and can dominate an entire program’s calendar.
Plane-change Δv (if any)
Do it at apogee, where you are slowest; budget it together with the circularization burn rather than as a separate line.
Propulsion: thrust & Isp
Chemical (impulsive Hohmann) vs electric (spiral). This decides whether a Hohmann is even the right trajectory, not just how you fly it.
Margins
Gravity and steering losses for finite burns, plus navigation and dispersion allowances. The ideal Δv is a floor, not the number you load into the tanks.
Bi-elliptic check
For ratios ≳ 12, compare against a bi-elliptic transfer before committing — it can be cheaper in Δv at the cost of a much longer flight.

8Field notes

What the textbook won’t tell you
  • Burn prograde to go up — and accept that you’ll be slower on top. The paradox trips people forever: between circular orbits, energy and speed move in opposite directions. Internalize “higher = slower” and the two prograde burns stop feeling wrong.
  • Both burns must be tangential (prograde / retrograde). Any radial or out-of-plane component is Δv you paid for that did not help the transfer. The efficiency of the Hohmann is that both burns are purely along-track.
  • Do plane changes where you’re slow. Inclination change costs roughly v·Δθ, so a degree costs far less at a 1.6 km/s apogee than at a 7.7 km/s perigee. Fold the plane change into Δv2.
  • The free coast isn’t free in time. Months-long interplanetary coasts drive consumables, power, radiation, and reliability. Fuel-optimal is rarely time-optimal — the cheapest ellipse can be the slowest ride.
  • Windows, not whims. You launch when the geometry lines up; miss it and you wait a full synodic period. This one fact shapes entire program schedules, Mars missions most famously.
  • Electric propulsion changes the game, not just the numbers. With milli-newton thrust you cannot do an impulsive Hohmann; you spiral. It is slower but, thanks to high Isp, often far cheaper in propellant — a different trajectory, not a tweaked one.
  • Vis-viva is the one equation to carry. v = √(μ(2/r − 1/a)) gives every speed on every conic. Memorize it and you can sanity-check any transfer on a napkin.
  • The ideal Δv is a floor. Real missions add 5–15 % for losses, dispersions, and station-keeping. Quoting the textbook Hohmann number as the budget is how you run out of fuel short of the orbit.

9Build it: LEO → GEO, end to end

Enough principle — here is a real raise walked end to end, so you can copy the shape and reuse it. The goal: lift a satellite from a 400 km circular LEO to the geostationary circle, and come away knowing the Δv, the time, and the window. Use μEarth = 398,600 km³/s². Every line below is one number read straight off the equations you already own.

Worked example — raising a satellite from 400 km LEO to GEO
The two radii
LEO: r1 = 6378 + 400 = 6778 km. GEO: r2 = 42,164 km (where the period is one sidereal day). Ratio ≈ 6.2 — well under 11.9, so Hohmann is optimal.
The ellipse
a = (r1 + r2)/2 = (6778 + 42,164)/2 = 24,471 km.
Circular speeds
v1 = √(μ/r1) = √(398600/6778) ≈ 7.67 km/s; v2 = √(μ/r2) = √(398600/42164) ≈ 3.07 km/s — GEO is much slower, the paradox already visible in the raw numbers.
Ellipse apsis speeds (vis-viva)
vp = √(μ(2/r1 − 1/a)) ≈ 10.07 km/s; va = √(μ(2/r2 − 1/a)) ≈ 1.62 km/s.
The two burns
Δv1 = vp − v1 ≈ 10.07 − 7.67 = 2.40 km/s (prograde at LEO); Δv2 = v2 − va ≈ 3.07 − 1.62 = 1.45 km/s (prograde at GEO). Total ≈ 3.85 km/s.
Transfer time
t = π√(a3/μ) = π√(24,4713/398,600) ≈ 5.3 hours — one half-ellipse.
Reality adders
A real GTO→GEO insertion from a non-equatorial launch also pays a plane change (fold it into Δv2), plus finite-burn and dispersion margins — the loaded budget sits a few hundred m/s above the ideal 3.85 km/s. That total, via the rocket equation (Deep Dive #04), sizes the apogee-kick propellant.

That is the method made concrete: two altitudes and a gravitational parameter hand you the ellipse, the four speeds, the two burns, the trip time, and — loudly — the fact that the ideal 3.85 km/s is a floor, not the number you fuel to. The plug-in set is small, and the entire transfer lives in a handful of lines you already have:

vcirc = √(μ/r)circular speed at radius r
a = (r1 + r2)/2semi-major axis of the transfer ellipse
v = √( μ(2/r − 1/a) )vis-viva — speed anywhere on the orbit
Δv1 = √(μ(2/r1 − 1/a)) − √(μ/r1)burn 1, at the inner radius
Δv2 = √(μ/r2) − √(μ(2/r2 − 1/a))burn 2, at the outer radius
t = π√(a3/μ)transfer time (half the ellipse period)
r2/r1 ≳ 11.94beyond this ratio a bi-elliptic transfer can cost less total Δv

Plan it in this order. The sequence matters; skipping a step just relocates the pain — usually to the moment the target isn’t where you arrive, or the tank runs dry short of the circle:

  1. Write down r1, r2 and μ. Convert altitudes to radii (add the central body’s radius), form the ratio, and note if it exceeds ~12 (then also price a bi-elliptic).
  2. Compute a, then the four speeds (v1, v2, vp, va) from vis-viva. Sanity-check: vp > v1, va < v2, and the higher orbit is slower.
  3. Get the two Δv’s and the total, and feed the total into the rocket equation (Deep Dive #04) to size propellant.
  4. Compute the transfer time t = π√(a3/μ) and the phase angle for departure; from that, the launch window and its cadence.
  5. Add a plane change at apogee if the target is inclined, and add loss and dispersion margins (5–15 %).
  6. Check the propulsion type: chemical → execute as two impulsive burns; electric / low-thrust → replan as a spiral, not a Hohmann.

The forks that actually change the plan. Most parameters you can trim later; these you decide up front:

Hohmann vs bi-elliptic
Hohmann for ratios up to ~12 (fewer burns, faster, usually cheaper); bi-elliptic for very large raises, where the far-apogee trick saves Δv at the cost of a much longer trip.
Chemical (impulsive) vs electric (spiral)
Chemical for speed and crewed / time-critical transfers (higher Δv, more propellant); electric for cargo and fuel-limited missions (slow spiral, high Isp, far less propellant) — but it is a different trajectory, not a Hohmann.
When to spend the plane change
Combine it with the apogee burn (slow → cheap) rather than a separate maneuver; split-S or super-synchronous tricks can cut it further for GTO→GEO.
Direct insertion vs parking orbit + transfer
Launch straight onto the transfer ellipse when the window allows, or park in LEO and phase — trading flexibility against a little extra Δv.

10The honest last mile

A deep dive gets you the exact ideal Δv, the transfer time, and the window geometry — a correct first-cut trajectory and budget, about 90 % of the planning. The last 10 % is where real missions live.

The last mile — what you still do with real tools This page gets you the mechanism cold, the exact two-impulse Δv, the transfer time and the phase geometry — a defensible first cut. The last 10 % is flying that ellipse through a real solar system with a real engine on a real clock. Model finite burns and losses — impulsive vis-viva is a floor; real engines burn for minutes and pay gravity and steering losses, so run the burn in a propagator and carry the margin. Nail the phasing and windows — the ellipse is easy; arriving when the target is there, with navigation dispersions, is the hard part, and it wants a real ephemeris, not a circle on paper. Budget plane change, station-keeping, and disposal — the raise is rarely the whole Δv story; inclination, drag make-up, and end-of-life disposal all draw on the same tank. And decide the trajectory class early — chemical-impulsive versus electric-spiral changes everything downstream, so do not Hohmann-plan a low-thrust vehicle. Tools to stand on: a two-body / vis-viva calculator for the first cut, then a real propagator and mission-design tool — GMAT (free, NASA), STK, or poliastro / pykep in Python — with actual ephemerides for phasing, finite burns, and n-body effects. The vis-viva ellipse in Part I never changes; the last mile is everything real that rides on top of it.

Reason these out from vis-viva and the circular-speed relation, rather than recalling them — if the paradox still feels like a trick, these are where it stops being one:

Check your understanding

To raise your orbit you fire the engine and speed up — yet once you’ve finished the transfer and circularized, your spacecraft is moving slower than before you started. Explain, using energy and the circular-speed relation, why adding velocity leaves you slower.

Both burns add orbital energy, which is what raises the orbit; but circular speed v = √(μ/r) falls as r grows, so the higher destination circle is intrinsically slower. The prograde burns pour velocity into altitude (potential energy), and the price of a higher orbit is a lower speed. Energy and speed move in opposite directions between circular orbits — you spent Δv to buy altitude, and altitude is paid for in slowness.

Check your understanding

A colleague plans a transfer with a single big prograde burn at LEO that raises apogee to GEO, and says the satellite will then “be at GEO.” Why will it not stay there, and what exactly is missing?

After the first burn the craft is on the transfer ellipse — it reaches GEO altitude only at apogee, and there it is moving at just ≈ 1.62 km/s, far below the ≈ 3.07 km/s needed to hold a circle at that radius. So it does not stay; it falls right back down to the LEO perigee. The missing piece is the second prograde burn, Δv2 ≈ 1.45 km/s at apogee, to circularize. Reaching the altitude is only half the job — both burns are mandatory.

Check your understanding

You need to both raise a satellite to GEO and change its orbital plane by a few degrees. Why is it far cheaper to do the plane change at the GEO end of the transfer than at the LEO end?

A plane change costs Δv ≈ v·Δθ for small angles — proportional to how fast you are going when you do it. At LEO you are moving at ≈ 7.7 km/s; at the GEO end you are moving at only ≈ 1.6–3 km/s, roughly two to five times slower, so the same angle costs several times less Δv there. That is why you fold the plane change into the slow apogee (circularization) burn rather than paying for it down at fast perigee.
