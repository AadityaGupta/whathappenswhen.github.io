The Delta-Sigma ADC: 20 Bits From a 1-Bit Comparator

You cannot build a 20-bit converter by trimming 20-bit-accurate components — nobody can match a million levels. So delta-sigma stops trying: it uses the crudest possible quantizer, a 1-bit comparator, runs it absurdly fast, and wraps it in a feedback loop that shoves its own error out of the band you care about. Trade precision in amplitude for precision in time and averaging, and read 20 clean bits off a stream of ones and zeros.

≈ 18 min read delta-sigma · oversampling · precision ADCs companion to The Op-Amp & Negative Feedback and Sensors & Signal Chains

An analog-to-digital converter has one job: pin an analog voltage to one of 2N levels. The direct ways build those levels in hardware — a flash converter with a ladder of matched resistors, a SAR converter with a binary-weighted capacitor array — and they are superb to 8, 10, maybe 12 bits. But each extra bit halves the voltage you must resolve and doubles the matching your components need, and somewhere around 16 bits you are asking silicon to match to a handful of parts per million, across temperature and aging and the luck of the fab. That path dead-ends on component matching: you simply cannot trim a million levels into a chip. Delta-sigma is the refusal that gets you there anyway. Stop trying to be precise in amplitude; use the crudest quantizer there is — a 1-bit comparator, which can only ever ask “above or below the middle?” — because a two-level converter has nothing to mismatch and is therefore perfectly linear. Then buy the lost resolution back two ways: run the comparator far faster than the signal needs, and wrap it in a feedback loop with an integrator that pushes its own quantization noise out of your signal band, where a digital filter deletes it. Average the fast one-bit stream down and 20 honest bits fall out — set by clock rate and loop order, not by trimming anything. From that one move the whole subject follows: oversampling, noise shaping, the bitstream whose density of ones is the value, and the decimation filter that is the converter’s other half. Then the piece turns to the part the textbooks skip: how you choose and use a real part, why it disappoints, what to spec, and a 24-bit weigh-scale front end sized end to end.

1The constraint: you can’t match a million levels

Start with exactly what the problem demands. A converter must map a continuous voltage onto one of 2N discrete levels, and the obvious architectures build those levels as physical hardware. A flash ADC needs 2N − 1 comparators and a resistor ladder with 2N matched taps; a SAR ADC needs a capacitor array binary-weighted to N-bit accuracy. Both work beautifully to 8, 10, perhaps 12 bits — the levels are far enough apart that ordinary parts hold them.

The trouble is the exponent. Each extra bit halves the voltage you have to resolve and, with it, doubles the matching precision your components must hold. At 16 bits you are asking resistors or capacitors to agree to roughly 15 parts per million; at 20 bits, to under one part per million — and not just on the day they are made, but across temperature, across years of aging, across the spread of a production run. That is simply not buildable with trimmed components at any sane cost. The brute-force road to high resolution dead-ends on the same wall every time: component matching. You cannot trim a million levels into silicon. The question the rest of this piece answers is how to get a clean 20-bit number out of parts that can’t hold more than about 12 bits’ worth of matching.

2The reframe: trade amplitude precision for time and averaging

Here is the move, and like the best moves it is a refusal: stop trying to be precise in amplitude at all. Reach instead for the crudest quantizer there is — a 1-bit comparator, which answers exactly one question, “is the signal above or below the midpoint?” At first this looks like surrender. But a 1-bit converter has only two output levels, and two levels have nothing to mismatch: two points define a perfectly straight line, so a 1-bit DAC is inherently, flawlessly linear. That is the quiet secret that makes the whole scheme give honest high-resolution results — there is no curvature or trim error hiding in the conversion, because there are not enough levels to bend.

The catch is obvious: one bit is terrible resolution. We buy it back along two axes that are cheap and buildable. First, oversample — run the comparator far faster than the signal strictly needs (its Nyquist rate), so you take thousands of crude looks where you needed one. Second, feed the error back — wrap the comparator in a loop with an integrator so the loop continuously corrects its own quantization error, and arrange that correction so the error energy is shoved out of the frequency band you care about. Then average the fast 1-bit stream down into a slow, high-resolution number. We have traded precision-in-amplitude, which is impossible, for precision-in-time-and-averaging, which is cheap. The hard part moved from matching analog components to counting fast — and counting fast is exactly what digital silicon does for free.

3The core: oversampling, then noise shaping

Build the argument in two moves, because they stack — the first makes the second possible, and together they turn a single bit into twenty.

Move 1 — oversampling spreads the noise thin. Quantization adds a fixed amount of error power, and for a busy signal that power is spread roughly evenly across frequency, from DC up to half the sampling rate. The total doesn’t change when you sample faster — but the band it is spread over does. Sample faster and the same fixed noise power is smeared across a wider band, so the slice of it sitting inside your narrow signal band shrinks. Define the oversampling ratio as the sampling rate over the Nyquist rate of your band:

OSR = fs2·fBhow many times faster than Nyquist you run the comparator

Because the in-band noise power falls in proportion to OSR, every doubling of OSR buys about 3 dB — half a bit:

ΔSNR ≈ 3 dB (½ bit) per doubling of OSRoversampling alone — honest, but painfully slow

Useful, but weak: getting 16 bits from a 1-bit converter by oversampling alone would demand an absurd OSR, as the last self-check makes concrete. We need a lever, not a crank.

Move 2 — noise shaping bends the noise out of the band. Put an integrator before the comparator, take the 1-bit output, and subtract it from the input before the integrator. That subtract is the delta (Δ); the integrate is the sigma (Σ). In one line of pseudocode the whole modulator is just: form the error e = u − y between input and fed-back output, accumulate it in the integrator state v, and let the comparator make the one-bit decision on that accumulator:

e = u − y,    v ← v + e,    y = sign(v)subtract (Δ), integrate (Σ), then a 1-bit decision — the entire modulator

Now trace where the signal and the noise each go. The loop passes the signal straight through — its signal transfer function is about 1 in band — while the quantization noise, which is injected right at the comparator, sees the integrator’s gain working against it. The result is that the noise transfer function is a high-pass: the loop literally pushes its own quantization noise up to high frequencies, out of the signal band, piling it where a digital filter will later throw it away. A first-order loop (one integrator) shapes the in-band noise so it falls about 9 dB per octave of OSR — 1.5 bits per doubling; a second-order loop (two integrators) gives about 15 dB/octave — 2.5 bits per doubling. In one formula, for a loop of order L:

ΔSNR ≈ (6L + 3) dB per doubling of OSRL=1 → 9 dB (1.5 bit);  L=2 → 15 dB (2.5 bit)

Those bits are abstract until you convert signal-to-noise ratio into resolution, and the bridge is the standard one — every extra 6.02 dB of SNR is one more effective bit:

ENOB ≈ SNRdB − 1.766.02effective number of bits, read off the SNR

So a 1-bit comparator clocked fast, inside a 2nd-order loop, really can deliver 16 to 20-plus bits — because the feedback high-pass is a far stronger lever than merely spreading noise thin.

The whole payoff in one line
resolution = oversampling (spread it thin)  +  noise shaping (bend it out of band)

Resolution no longer comes from matched components; it comes from oversampling — spreading the fixed quantization noise thin — plus noise shaping — a feedback loop with integrators that high-pass-filters its own noise out of the signal band. A perfectly-linear 1-bit quantizer, clocked fast, becomes a 20-bit converter, and the bits are set by clock rate and loop order, not by trimming anything.

Spread the noise thin, then bend it out of the band

Drag the oversampling ratio and the loop order and watch the two levers act. With no shaping the quantization noise is a flat carpet from DC to fs/2; pull the OSR up and the signal band (marked at the left) keeps only a thinner and thinner slice of that same total — the ½-bit-per-octave crank. Now turn the order up from 0 to 1 to 2 and the carpet lifts off the floor and tips uphill: the noise is high-pass-shaped, pushed out of the band where the decimation filter will delete it, and the in-band area collapses far faster — 1.5 bits per octave at first order, 2.5 at second. The effective-bits readout climbs as you do both, and you can feel directly why shaping is the lever and oversampling alone is merely the crank.

4Limit checks — read the scheme at its ends

The scheme earns its keep by behaving sensibly at every extreme, and reading it at its ends is the fastest way to internalise what it actually trades.

Limit check — read the scheme at its ends OSR → 1 (sample at Nyquist, no oversampling): you are back to a plain 1-bit converter — about one bit, useless. Every bit of resolution is bought above Nyquist; none of it lives at the signal rate. OSR → very large: resolution keeps climbing — 1.5 or 2.5 bits per doubling, depending on order — but you pay in clock rate and power, and you spend bandwidth. Delta-sigma trades bandwidth for bits, which is exactly why it rules low-bandwidth, high-resolution jobs (audio, weigh scales, precision sensors) and never GHz data acquisition — that belongs to pipeline and SAR. Loop order → higher (3rd, 4th order): steeper shaping, even more bits per octave — but past 2nd order a 1-bit loop can go unstable, so high-order modulators need careful design (feedforward paths, scaled integrators) to stay well-behaved. Input near full scale, or a steady DC input: the loop can fall into idle tones — faint periodic patterns in the bitstream that show up as spurious tones in the output; dither or a higher-order loop breaks them up.

Two consequences fall straight out of the mechanism, and they are what a real converter is actually made of. The first is what the modulator emits. It does not hand you numbers; it hands you a high-rate 1-bit stream whose density of ones encodes the input. Feed in a level at 75 % of full scale and about 75 % of the output bits come out as ones; the value lives in the average, never in any single bit. The second is the half that turns that stream back into samples: the decimation filter, the digital half of every delta-sigma ADC. It is a low-pass filter — which both averages the bitstream into multi-bit words and removes the shaped high-frequency noise the modulator pushed up there — followed by downsampling to the output rate:

fout = fsOSRthe decimation filter averages, kills the shaped HF noise, then drops to the output rate

So a delta-sigma ADC is really two cooperating halves: a tiny, fast, mostly-analog modulator that trades resolution for speed and linearity, and a digital decimation filter that trades the speed back for resolution. The analog part is trivial and robust — one comparator, a couple of integrators, components that can be sloppy because feedback corrects them — and the heavy lifting moved into cheap, exact digital logic. That relocation, from matched analog to free digital, is precisely why the technique won.

The bitstream's density of ones IS the value

Drag the input level and watch the first-order modulator run: the integrator ramps as it accumulates the error e = u − y, the comparator spits a 1 or a 0 each clock, and the fed-back bit is subtracted to keep the loop honest. No single bit means anything — but the density of ones tracks the input, and the running average you can see converging on the readout is the converted value: set the input to three-quarters of full scale and about three bits in four come out high. Toggle from a steady DC level to a slow sine and the density breathes up and down in step with it — the stream is the signal, encoded in time instead of amplitude.

5Where it lives: choosing a part, and how it disappoints

Delta-sigma is the default converter for precision, lower-bandwidth measurement: audio capture, weigh scales and load cells, thermocouple, RTD and bridge front-ends, power and energy metering, and most 16–24-bit “precision ADC” chips. You rarely design the modulator yourself — you select a chip and use it well, and that is a short, fixed sequence. Fix the signal bandwidth and the resolution (noise-free bits) you need; from those pick the OSR and modulator order — usually the chip’s job, exposed as a data-rate setting; choose the output data rate, which sets the decimation filter and the settling time; and match the input type — differential, usually with a built-in PGA for small sensor signals. When a part underperforms it does so in a short, recognisable vocabulary, and almost every entry is really “you spent the bandwidth/resolution budget somewhere you didn’t mean to”:

Noisy results at high data rate
You asked for too much bandwidth. Raising the output rate lowers the OSR, so noise-free bits drop — roughly 1.5–2.5 bits per octave of OSR you gave away. Slow the data rate back down and the resolution returns; it was never gone, just spent.
Sluggish or wrong readings right after a channel switch
The decimation filter has memory — it needs several conversions to settle. Multiplex channels without allowing that settling and you read stale, blended data: a bit of the new channel, a bit of the old.
A persistent tone or pattern in audio or spectra
Idle tones from a DC-ish input. Add dither, or move to a part with a higher-order loop; both break the periodic pattern up into broadband noise you can’t hear.
50/60 Hz mains hum in the reading
Pick an output rate whose decimation filter drops notches on 50 and 60 Hz — most precision ADCs offer exactly this. It is free rejection you paid for the moment you chose the right data rate.
Resolution worse than the datasheet
You quoted dynamic range where you meant noise-free bits, or your reference and PGA noise now dominate. At this resolution the ADC is often no longer the floor — something quieter than the modulator has to be, and it usually isn’t.

6How the architecture shapes the system

The whole personality of delta-sigma flows from one trade: bandwidth for resolution. That makes it exactly the right tool when the signal is slow and the accuracy demand is high, and exactly the wrong one when you need speed — there is no setting that gives you both. From that single trade, four system-level consequences follow. It relocates difficulty out of the analog domain (no trimmed ladders, no matched caps — just a comparator and some integrators, which tolerate loose components because feedback corrects them) and into the digital domain (the decimation filter), which rides Moore’s law and costs almost nothing. It bakes in anti-aliasing relief: because you sample at OSR×, the analog anti-alias filter only has to guard a band far above your signal, so a single RC does what a steep analog filter would otherwise demand. It hands you built-in filtering knobs — the output data rate directly sets your noise bandwidth and your mains-rejection notches, so filtering choices you would otherwise build in hardware become a register write. And the cost it imposes on the system is honest and unavoidable: latency (the decimation filter’s group delay) and a hard ceiling on bandwidth. Design around those two and the architecture gives you everything else nearly for free.

7Where it’s used, and what to spec

The same idea runs audio ADCs and — reversed — 1-bit and multi-bit audio DACs and class-D amplifiers; it runs precision instrumentation and sensor front-ends, weigh scales, power and energy meters, and sigma-delta-based isolated current sensors. When you pick one, these are the numbers that actually decide the outcome:

Output data rate & bandwidth
The first lever. It sets how fast you can sample and, inversely, your resolution — the two are the same knob read from opposite ends.
Noise-free / effective resolution (ENOB) at that rate
The honest resolution number at your data rate, not the headline bit count. Always quoted with a rate, or it means nothing.
Modulator order & OSR
More shaping is more bits per octave; higher order risks tones and instability, but most parts handle this internally and expose it only as a data-rate choice.
Integrated PGA & reference
A low-noise gain stage and a stable reference — because at 24 bits the reference and PGA noise usually set the floor, not the modulator.
Digital filter options
Selectable data rates with 50/60 Hz notch filters, and a choice between single-cycle-settled (sinc) and sharper filters for multiplexing.
Latency / settling
Group delay and settling time — critical the moment you multiplex channels or close a control loop around the converter.
Input configuration
Differential inputs, common-mode range, and channel count / mux — what the sensor actually needs to connect to.

8Field notes

What the datasheet won’t tell you
  • Resolution and speed are the same knob. Every “more bits” is “less bandwidth” and vice-versa; the chip’s data-rate setting is the OSR dial. Decide your bandwidth first, then read off the bits you are allowed to have — not the other way around.
  • The digital filter has memory — respect settling when you multiplex. After a channel change the sinc filter needs several conversions to flush the old data. Read too soon and you get a blend of two channels that looks like a real measurement. Either wait the settling time, or use a single-cycle-settled filter.
  • At 24 bits, the ADC is rarely your noise floor. The voltage reference, the PGA, and your own board — thermals, grounding, EMI — dominate. Spend your effort on the reference and the layout, not on hunting a “better” modulator.
  • Pick the data rate for free mains rejection. The right output rate lands the filter’s notches exactly on 50/60 Hz — often worth more than any analog filtering you would add, and it costs a register value.
  • Oversampling relaxes your anti-alias filter to a single RC. You still need one, but the steep analog filter a Nyquist converter would demand is gone — a real board-level saving people forget they already earned by oversampling.
  • Idle tones are a DC-input phenomenon. They appear with slow, steady inputs and vanish on busy signals; a little dither trades a hair of noise floor for their removal, which is almost always the right trade.
  • “24-bit” is marketing until you say at what rate. Always quote ENOB or noise-free bits at your data rate; the headline number is measured at the slowest, quietest setting you will never actually use.

9Build it: a 24-bit load-cell / weigh-scale front end

Enough principle — here is a real front end sized end to end, so you can copy the shape and reuse it. The job: digitise a strain-gauge load cell — a few millivolts full-scale, signal essentially DC to a few hertz — with about 20 noise-free bits and clean mains rejection. Every line below is one choice you plug in; the derivation already did the hard part.

Worked example — a 24-bit weigh-scale front end
Know the signal
A load cell outputs about 2 mV/V; on a 5 V bridge excitation that is roughly 10 mV full scale, bandwidth 0–5 Hz (weight settles slowly). Tiny, slow, high-accuracy — the textbook delta-sigma job.
Pick the part class
An integrated 24-bit delta-sigma with an on-chip low-noise PGA (gain 128×) and differential inputs — an ADS1232 / NAU7802-class weigh-scale ADC. The PGA lifts the 10 mV up to use the full reference, so the modulator sees a signal that fills its range.
Set the data rate
Choose 10 SPS (samples per second). For a 5 Hz signal that is a huge OSR, so noise-free bits are maximised — and a 10 SPS sinc filter lands notches on 50 and 60 Hz, so mains hum is rejected for free.
Resolution reality-check
The headline is “24-bit,” but at 10 SPS with the PGA at 128× you will see perhaps ~19–21 noise-free bits referred to the input — plenty to resolve grams on a many-kilogram scale. Quote the noise-free number, never the 24.
What actually limits you
The reference and PGA noise, the bridge-excitation stability, and thermocouple EMFs at the connections — not the modulator. Run ratiometric: use the bridge excitation as the ADC reference, and excitation drift cancels out of the reading entirely.
Anti-alias
A single RC per input, cutoff well below fs/2, is enough — oversampling pushed the alias bands so far away that nothing steep is needed.

That is the method made concrete: a sensor’s few millivolts, a bandwidth and a resolution target hand you the part class, the data rate, the gain, and — loudly — the fact that your real adversary is board noise and the reference, not the converter. The plug-in set is small: the oversampling ratio OSR = fs/(2·fB); oversampling-only gain ΔSNR ≈ 3 dB (½ bit) per doubling of OSR; with noise shaping of order L, ΔSNR ≈ (6L+3) dB per doubling — 9 dB (1.5 bit) first-order, 15 dB (2.5 bit) second; bits from SNR, ENOB ≈ (SNRdB − 1.76)/6.02; noise-free bits ≈ ENOB − ~2.7 (peak-to-peak against RMS); and the decimation relation fout = fs/OSR, where the digital filter both averages the stream and kills the shaped high-frequency noise.

Bring it up in this order. The sequence matters; skipping a step just relocates the pain — usually into a noise number you can’t explain later:

  1. Short the inputs and measure the noise floor. With the inputs shorted (or held at mid-scale), log the output and compute RMS and peak-to-peak noise → your real noise-free bits at this data rate. This is ground truth, taken before any sensor is attached.
  2. Sweep the data rate. Confirm the resolution rises as you slow down and falls as you speed up — you are watching the OSR / bits trade happen directly, in your own numbers.
  3. Check mains rejection. Pick the rate whose notches sit on your local 50 or 60 Hz; wave a hand near the wiring and confirm the reading barely twitches.
  4. Add the sensor, go ratiometric. Use the bridge excitation as the reference and confirm the reading is immune to excitation-voltage drift — wiggle the supply and watch the number hold.
  5. Test channel settling (if multiplexing). Switch channels and count how many conversions it takes to settle; budget that wait, or choose a single-cycle-settled filter.
  6. Calibrate. Offset (tare) and gain (a known mass or voltage) — the modulator is linear, so two points are all it takes to pin the whole transfer curve.

The forks that actually change the design. Most parameters you can trim later; these you decide up front:

Delta-sigma vs SAR vs pipeline
Delta-sigma for high resolution at low bandwidth (sensors, audio, metering); SAR for medium speed and resolution with low latency (easy multiplexing); pipeline for high speed (MS/s to GS/s). Latency and bandwidth decide, not raw bit count.
Data rate: fast vs slow
Slow for maximum bits and mains notches (metering, scales); faster when you need loop bandwidth or many channels, accepting fewer bits. The same knob, chosen for the job.
Sinc (single-cycle-settled) vs sharp filter
Sinc settles in one conversion — ideal for multiplexing — but rolls off gently; a sharper filter rejects close-in interferers but carries long group delay.
Integrated PGA / reference vs external
Integrated simplifies and often enables ratiometric operation; go external only when you need an ultra-stable reference or an unusual gain the chip can’t give you.
The last mile — what you still do on the bench This page gets you the right architecture, the right part class, the data rate, and a realistic resolution number — about 90 % of the job. The last 10 % is analog board reality and the digital filter’s fine print, and it is where a 24-bit part earns or loses its bits. Your noise floor is a layout and reference problem, not an ADC problem at this resolution: star grounds, guarding, keeping thermocouple junctions matched and isothermal, a genuinely low-noise excitation — and you measure it shorted first, before you trust any sensor reading. Know your decimation filter’s settling and group delay before you multiplex channels or close a control loop around the converter — read the filter section of the datasheet, not just the headline bits. Calibrate against a traceable reference — known mass, known voltage — over temperature, because the converter is linear but your sensor and reference drift. And validate mains rejection and real ENOB on the bench, at your actual data rate and inside your actual enclosure, not in the datasheet’s. Tools to stand on: the vendor’s precision-ADC eval board and PC GUI (they plot your noise, histograms and ENOB for you), a good DMM and reference for calibration, and the datasheet’s filter, settling and noise tables. Don’t hand-roll a modulator in silicon — use the integrated parts. The oversample-and-shape idea in Part I never changes; the last mile is giving a 24-bit part a board quiet enough to deserve its bits.

10Check yourself

Question 1

Your 24-bit delta-sigma reads beautifully at 10 SPS but gets noticeably noisier when you bump it to 1 kSPS to track a faster signal. Nothing else changed. Why is this expected, and what single quantity moved?

You traded bits for bandwidth, and the one knob that moved is the OSR. Raising the output rate raises fs relative to your band, but for a fixed modulator clock the practical effect is the same: less of the fixed quantization noise is being pushed out of band, because OSR = fs/(2·fB) fell when you widened the band. Fewer bits survive — noise-free resolution drops about 1.5–2.5 bits per octave of OSR you gave away, depending on the loop order. The resolution wasn’t lost; it was spent on bandwidth, and slowing back down to 10 SPS buys every bit of it back.
Question 2

You multiplex four thermocouples through one delta-sigma ADC and every channel reads a bit like a blend of itself and the previous channel. The ADC and the wiring are fine. What’s doing it, and what are two fixes?

The decimation (sinc) filter has memory. It averages many bitstream samples to make each output word, so after you switch the mux it needs several conversions to flush the old channel’s data out of its history — and you are reading before it has settled, so each result is a weighted blend of the new channel and the last one. The two fixes both respect that memory: either wait the filter’s settling time after every channel switch before you trust a reading, or switch to a single-cycle-settled (fully-settled sinc) filter or mode, which produces one valid, un-blended sample per conversion at the cost of a gentler roll-off.
Question 3

Someone proposes getting 16 bits from a 1-bit converter purely by oversampling — no feedback loop at all. Roughly what OSR would that need, and why does adding a single integrator change the answer so dramatically?

Oversampling alone buys about 0.5 bit per octave, so 15 extra bits is roughly 30 octaves of OSR — an oversampling ratio on the order of 109, which is absurd: no clock and no decimator could survive it. One integrator turns on first-order noise shaping, which is about 1.5 bits per octave, and a second integrator makes it 2.5, so the same 15 bits need only a few hundred× OSR instead of a billion×. The reason the lever is so much stronger is that the feedback loop doesn’t merely spread the quantization noise thinner — it high-pass-filters it out of the band entirely, bending the noise up to frequencies the decimation filter simply deletes. Spreading is a crank; shaping is a lever.
