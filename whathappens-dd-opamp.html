The Op-Amp & Negative Feedback: Why One Rule Builds Almost Every Analog Circuit

An op-amp’s own gain is enormous, sloppy, and drifts with everything — yet we build precise amplifiers, filters and controllers from it. The trick is to stop controlling the gain and start controlling the error: negative feedback makes the circuit fix itself, and one rule — the op-amp moves its output until its two inputs match — explains nearly the whole analog world.

≈ 18 min read op-amps · negative feedback · analog design companion to Control Systems and Sensors & Signal Chains

An operational amplifier is almost useless on its own. Its voltage gain is enormous — a hundred thousand, a million, more — but that gain is sloppy: it varies three-to-one between parts, drifts with temperature and supply, bends and distorts, and collapses as the frequency climbs. You cannot build a precise ×10 amplifier by “setting the gain to ten,” because the gain is neither settable nor stable. And yet almost every precise analog circuit ever built — amplifiers, active filters, references, the integrators inside every control loop — is made from exactly this untrustworthy part. The way out is a single move: stop trying to control the gain, and start controlling the error. Feed a fraction of the output back to the inverting input and the amplifier becomes a servo that drives its own output until its two inputs match; the monstrous gain stops being the answer and becomes the engine that forces a tiny error to zero. One line falls out — the closed-loop gain is set by two resistors, not by the op-amp — and with it the two golden rules, the gain-bandwidth trade, and most of analog design. This piece builds that line from the one constraint, then turns to the half the textbooks skip: how you pick a topology, why a real circuit saturates or oscillates or drifts, and the things that only bite once the ideal math meets a real part on a real board.

1The constraint: a gain block you can’t trust

Start with the raw device. An operational amplifier is, at its heart, one enormous voltage gain: it takes the difference between its two inputs and multiplies it, putting out A times that difference, where the open-loop gain A runs from about 100,000 to well over a million.

Vout = A · (V+ − V−)A ≈ 105 to 106 — and you can trust none of it

That sounds wonderful until you try to use it. A is not a number you can lean on: it varies three-to-one from part to part, drifts with temperature and supply voltage, is nonlinear, and falls away as frequency rises. Feed a one-millivolt difference into a gain of 200,000 and you have just asked for 200 volts out — the output instantly slams into the supply rail and sits there, saturated. You cannot build a precise ×10 amplifier by “setting A = 10”; A is neither settable nor stable, and a difference of a few millivolts is already enough to rail it.

So the raw part, used open-loop as an amplifier, is almost unusable. That is the whole tension of the subject: the device hands you a gain that is colossal but wildly imprecise, and the entire art is getting precise, repeatable behaviour out of it anyway. The question the rest of the piece answers is simple to state: how do you build a trustworthy ×10 (or ×100) out of a gain you cannot trust to within a factor of three?

2The reframe: don’t control the gain, control the error

Here is the move that unlocks everything, and like all the best moves it is a refusal. Stop trying to pin down A. Instead, take a fraction of the output and feed it back to the inverting (−) input, so the amplifier can see its own output. Feed it to the minus input and the feedback is negative: if the output drifts too high, the fed-back voltage at V− rises, the difference (V+ − V−) shrinks, and the huge gain drags the output straight back down. The circuit becomes a servo — it drives its output to exactly whatever value makes its two inputs (very nearly) equal, and then holds it there.

Notice what just happened to the problem. We wanted “amplify by a trusted factor,” which the op-amp cannot do, and we traded it for “null an error,” which a monstrous gain is perfect for. The sloppiness of A stops being the obstacle; a big, sloppy gain nulls an error just as hard as a big, precise one. The enormous untrustworthy gain is no longer the answer — it is the engine that makes a tiny error force a large correction. All that is left is to work out what value the servo settles on.

3The core equation: gain set by resistors, not by the op-amp

Write it down for the non-inverting amplifier: the signal goes to V+, and a resistor divider Rf–Rg from the output feeds a fraction back to V−. Two facts close the loop. The op-amp’s own law, Vout = A(V+ − V−) with V+ = Vin; and the feedback itself, V− = β Vout, where β = Rg/(Rf + Rg) is the fraction fed back. Substitute one into the other and solve for the closed-loop gain G = Vout/Vin:

G = A1 + Aβthe exact closed-loop gain

Now read what that fraction does when the loop gain Aβ is huge — which, with A in the hundreds of thousands, it almost always is. The A on top and the Aβ on the bottom cancel, and the whole answer collapses to something you can buy off a reel:

The whole payoff in one line
Aβ ≫ 1  →  G ≈ 1β = 1 + RfRg

The closed-loop gain is set by the ratio of two resistors — parts you can buy to 0.1% that barely drift — and is almost completely independent of A. The op-amp’s monstrous, untrustworthy gain has vanished from the answer; two resistors took its place.

It is worth seeing exactly how the sloppiness disappears, because it does not vanish by magic — it gets divided down. Factor the exact gain into the ideal answer times a correction, G = (1/β) · Aβ/(1 + Aβ), and the correction is the gain error:

gain error ≈ 11 + Aβ ≈ 1Aβthe sloppiness of A, divided down by the loop gain

Put numbers on it. With A = 200,000 and β = 0.1 (a gain of 10), the loop gain is Aβ = 20,000, so the real gain is low by about one part in 20,000 — 0.005%. Let A swing by its full three-to-one and that error wobbles between a hundred-thousandth and a few hundred-thousandths of the answer: invisible. The huge loop gain is a divider that takes the device’s worst sin and shrinks it below the noise.

The two famous “golden rules” you may have met first are consequences of this, not axioms. The virtual short — that V+ ≈ V− — is just the statement that V+ − V− = Vout/A, and since the output is finite while A is enormous, that difference is almost nothing. The inputs track each other not by decree but because any gap between them gets amplified hugely and corrected. The second rule, no input current, is just the op-amp’s very high input impedance. Both hold only while the loop is working — while Aβ is large — and Part II lives entirely on where that stops being true.

Crank the raw gain — the loop locks the output

Drag the raw open-loop gain A and the target gain 1/β (the resistor ratio). Watch what happens as A climbs: the inverting input V− rises to meet V+ — the virtual short snapping shut — and the closed-loop gain locks onto 1/β and stays there even as you keep sweeping A over its full three-to-one spread. The error 1/(1 + Aβ) falls toward zero as you turn the gain up. Crank A down toward 1 instead and the trick dies: the loop gives up, the two inputs drift apart, and the gain sags away from 1/β back toward the raw, useless A.

4Limit checks — read G = A/(1 + Aβ) at its ends

The exact gain earns its keep by behaving sensibly at every extreme. Three ends pin the physics down.

Limit check — read the equation at its ends Infinite loop gain (Aβ → ∞, the ideal): G → exactly 1/β. Gain perfectly set by the resistors, error → 0, output impedance → 0, distortion → 0 — the fantasy we design toward. Loop gain gone (Aβ → 0, as at high frequency where A has rolled off): G → A. The feedback does nothing, the golden rules evaporate, and you are back to the raw, unusable gain block — this is the single most important failure mode in Part II. Unity-gain follower (β = 1, output tied straight to V−): G = 1, maximum loop gain, maximum feedback — the most robust, highest-bandwidth, lowest-distortion configuration there is. A buffer is just feedback turned all the way up.

There is a fourth end, and it is the one that shapes real design: frequency. A is not a constant — it starts rolling off at about 20 dB/decade above a few hertz, and the product of gain and bandwidth stays fixed. That product is the gain-bandwidth product, GBW (also called the unity-gain frequency). Since the loop can only hold your closed-loop gain up to the point where A itself has fallen to meet it, the closed-loop bandwidth is simply GBW shared out by the gain:

f−3dB = GBWnoise gainbuy gain from one stage, pay with exactly proportional bandwidth

Want more gain out of a single stage? You pay for it with exactly proportionally less bandwidth. (Note the denominator is the noise gain, not the signal gain — a distinction that costs people whole afternoons, and one we return to in the field notes.)

Gain-bandwidth: buy gain, pay with bandwidth

The diagonal is the op-amp’s open-loop gain A falling at 20 dB/decade; its slope is fixed, because GBW is fixed. Drag the horizontal closed-loop gain line up and down and watch the corner — the −3 dB bandwidth — slide along the diagonal: higher gain pushes the corner left to a lower frequency, lower gain lets it run right. Drag GBW and the whole diagonal lifts or drops, carrying every corner with it. The intersection is the deal you actually get: f−3dB = GBW / gain, no negotiation.

Step back and the pattern across all four ends is one idea: feedback is a universal discount. Everything you want small gets divided by the loop gain (1 + Aβ), and everything you want large gets multiplied by it. Gain error, output impedance, distortion (the nonlinearity of A), and gain drift are all divided by (1 + Aβ); the non-inverting input impedance is multiplied by it. One mechanism — spending raw gain to buy loop gain — simultaneously buys precision, linearity, a stiff low-impedance output, and a high, defined input impedance. You are not tuning four knobs; you are turning one.

And here is the catch that ends Part I. That same loop, if its phase reaches −180° while |Aβ| is still greater than 1, stops being negative feedback and becomes positive — an oscillator. Phase margin is how much safety you have before that happens, and dominant-pole compensation (the deliberately large capacitor built into the op-amp) is what rolls A off early so that even a unity-gain follower stays stable. More loop gain buys more precision and spends phase margin; the two pull against each other, and they do so forever. That tension is the whole craft, and it is where the clean derivation hands off to the bench.

Part II · In practicethe half the textbooks skip

5Where it lives: picking a topology, and how it fails

The op-amp is the universal analog building block, and real design is a short, fixed sequence. Pick the topology for the job: non-inverting for high input impedance; inverting for a virtual-ground summing node and easy gain or filtering; difference and instrumentation amps for differential sensors; integrators and differentiators for control loops and filters. Then set the gain with a resistor ratio; check GBW gives the bandwidth you need at that gain; check slew rate survives your largest, fastest swing; check the DC errors (offset times noise gain, bias current times resistance) are small enough; and check stability (phase margin, especially into capacitive loads). When a circuit misbehaves it does so in a short, recognisable vocabulary — and almost every entry is really “the loop isn’t doing what you assumed”:

Output stuck hard at a rail (saturated)
No DC feedback path, an input shoved outside the common-mode range, or offset × gain has itself railed the output. The loop cannot null an error it has no path to correct — check that a DC feedback path exists and the inputs are in range before you blame the op-amp.
Oscillation or ringing
Insufficient phase margin: a capacitive load, stray feedback capacitance, or simply too much loop gain pushing the phase toward −180°. The feedback has gone positive. Isolate caps, tame the loop, or compensate.
Output won’t swing near the rail
Not a rail-to-rail part, or the output current is maxed into the load. The datasheet’s “rail-to-rail” always has fine print — read how close it really gets under load.
Distortion only on fast or large signals
Slew-rate limited — the output physically can’t change fast enough — or you have run out of GBW at that amplitude. The small-signal response looked perfect, which is exactly why this one hides.
Persistent DC error at the output
Input offset voltage × noise gain, plus bias current × resistance. At high gain this, not gain accuracy, is usually your dominant error — and no resistor tolerance fixes it.

6How negative feedback shapes the whole circuit

Feedback redraws nearly every property of the circuit at once, which is why “what op-amp and what resistors” is most of analog design. Gain accuracy moves out of the op-amp and into your resistor matching: a 0.1% ratio gives a 0.1% gain, regardless of which op-amp you solder in. Bandwidth is GBW divided by gain, which forces an architectural choice — one high-gain stage (narrow) versus several modest-gain stages cascaded (wide, but more parts and more noise). Noise, offset and drift are set by the op-amp itself and then amplified by the noise gain, so they define a precision floor you cannot resistor your way out of. Input and output impedance are set by the topology and then multiplied or divided by loop gain. Gain, bandwidth, precision and impedance are not four free knobs: the feedback network and the op-amp’s GBW tie them together into a single trade, and learning to feel where they pull against each other is most of the skill.

7Where it’s used, and what to spec

The same one rule runs sensor front-ends, active filters, the integrators inside every analog control loop, ADC drivers, current-sense amplifiers, precision references, audio stages and instrumentation amps — anywhere a signal must be conditioned before something else can use it. When you choose a part, these are the numbers that actually decide the outcome:

Gain-bandwidth product (GBW)
Sets closed-loop bandwidth = GBW / noise gain. The first thing to check once you’ve picked the gain — and you need real margin, not a bare pass.
Slew rate
Maximum output dV/dt in V/µs. For a sine, the demand is SR = 2πfVpeak. It guards large, fast signals and is a separate limit from GBW — a part can have plenty of bandwidth and still slew-limit on a big step.
Input offset voltage & drift
Multiplied by noise gain into a DC output error. The dominant error at high gain; “precision” and “zero-drift” parts live here, in the microvolt class.
Input bias & offset current
Flows through your resistances to make an error. Match the resistance seen by both inputs and the bias part largely cancels.
Input voltage noise density (nV/√Hz)
The noise floor, amplified by the noise gain. It’s what decides small-signal front-ends.
Supply range & rail-to-rail (in/out)
Single versus dual supply, and how close to the rails the inputs and output really work — under load, not on the headline.
Output drive, CMRR/PSRR, quiescent current
Current into the load; how well it rejects common-mode and supply noise; and how much power it burns doing so.

8Field notes

What the clean derivation won’t tell you
  • The virtual short is a lie that’s only true while the loop is working. At high frequency, in saturation, or during slewing, V+ ≠ V− — and those are exactly the moments your circuit misbehaves. Debug by asking one question: “is the loop still in control here?”
  • Noise gain is not signal gain. An inverting amp with a signal gain of −1 has a noise gain of 2 (1 + Rf/Rg = 1 + 1 = 2). It is the noise gain — not the signal gain — that sets stability, offset error and output noise. Get this one wrong and your numbers never add up.
  • Always provide a DC feedback path. A pure capacitor-feedback integrator has infinite DC gain and will drift to a rail on offset and bias current alone, with no input. Put a large resistor across the cap to bound the DC gain and give those offsets somewhere to go.
  • Capacitive loads eat phase margin and cause oscillation. Isolate the load with a small series resistor (an “out-of-the-loop” R), or pick a part explicitly rated to drive caps.
  • Decouple the supplies right at the pins — 0.1 µF close in. Half of all “mystery oscillation” is supply inductance, not the circuit you actually drew.
  • “Rail-to-rail output” still can’t quite reach the rail under load, and rail-to-rail inputs often have a small crossover glitch mid-range where two internal input stages hand off. Read the fine print, or stay away from the rails.
  • At high gain, offset dominates — not gain error. A 1 mV offset at a gain of 100 is 100 mV out, usually far worse than the 0.01% gain error you fretted over. When gain is high, spend your money on offset, not on 0.01% resistors.
  • More loop gain fights phase margin. Every dB of loop gain you add for precision is a dB you take from stability. The whole craft is choosing where on that trade to sit — and then proving it in simulation.

9Build it: a ×100 precision sensor amplifier

Enough principle — here is a real amplifier sized end to end, so you can copy the shape and reuse it. The job: take a small, slow sensor signal — say a ±10 mV bridge or thermocouple output — and bring it up to ±1 V (gain = 100), flat to about 1 kHz, on a single +5 V or a ±5 V supply. Every line below is one formula you plug into; the derivation already did the hard part.

Worked example — a ×100 precision sensor amplifier
Topology & gain
Non-inverting, so the sensor sees a high input impedance and isn’t loaded. G = 1 + Rf/Rg = 100 → Rf/Rg = 99. Pick Rf = 100 kΩ, Rg = 1.0 kΩ (G = 101, close enough), or 99 kΩ/1 kΩ for exactly 100. The noise gain is 100 too.
Bandwidth → GBW
Closed-loop BW = GBW / gain. For 1 kHz at a gain of 100 you need GBW ≥ 100 kHz minimum; take ~10× margin so the response is flat, not drooping, at 1 kHz → GBW ≥ 1 MHz. A jellybean MCP6002 (~1 MHz) just scrapes it; a TLV9062 (~10 MHz) is comfortable.
The real killer — offset
Output DC error = Vos × noise gain = Vos × 100. A garden-variety Vos = 2 mV gives 200 mV of output error on a ±1 V signal — 20%! This is why you reach for a zero-drift / precision part: an OPA333 (Vos ≈ 10 µV) gives just 1 mV of output error. Offset, not gain accuracy, is the design driver here.
Bias current
Ib × resistance makes another DC error. Match the resistance seen by both inputs — add a resistor in the + lead equal to Rf∥Rg — so the bias-current part cancels. With a CMOS precision part Ib is tiny and this is a non-issue.
Slew rate
For ±1 V at 1 kHz, needed SR = 2πfVpk = 2π·1000·1 ≈ 6.3 mV/µs = 0.006 V/µs. Utterly trivial — any op-amp clears it by 1000×. Slew only bites when the signal is both fast and large.
Supply & swing
On +5 V single supply you need a mid-rail reference (Vref = 2.5 V) for the bipolar signal, a rail-to-rail output, and inputs that stay in range. On ±5 V it’s simpler — ground is your reference.

That is the method made concrete: a topology, a resistor ratio and one hard look at offset hand you the whole design — and they tell you, loudly, that your money goes on a precision part, not on tight resistors. The plug-in set is small: non-inverting gain G = 1 + Rf/Rg, inverting gain G = −Rf/Rg; noise gain (always) NG = 1 + Rf/Rg — use this for bandwidth, offset and stability; closed-loop bandwidth f−3dB = GBW / NG; loop gain T = Aβ = A/NG with gain error ≈ 1/T; output DC error Vout,err ≈ Vos·NG + Ib·R; and the slew check SRneeded = 2πfVpeak ≤ SRspec.

Bring it up in this order. The sequence matters; skipping a step just relocates the pain:

  1. Power only, input grounded. Check the quiescent current is sane and measure the output DC — it should read ≈ Vos × noise gain. If it’s railed, you have a wiring, offset or common-mode problem before any signal goes in.
  2. Small DC in. Apply a known small DC (say 5 mV) and confirm Vout = gain × Vin + offset. The gain should match the resistor ratio, not the datasheet A.
  3. Sweep frequency. Inject a small sine, find the −3 dB point, and confirm it ≈ GBW / noise gain. If it’s low, your op-amp’s GBW is short for this gain.
  4. Look for ringing or oscillation on the scope, especially with the real load attached. Ringing means marginal phase margin → add a series R to a capacitive load, or a small feedback cap.
  5. Load and swing test. Drive the real load at full amplitude; check the output still reaches its needed swing and isn’t slew- or current-limited.
  6. Measure real offset & drift, not datasheet-typical — warm the part with a finger and watch the DC output move. That movement is your drift budget.

The forks that actually change the design. Most parameters you can trim later; these you decide up front:

Inverting vs non-inverting
Non-inverting gives high input impedance, but the input sees the full common-mode voltage (a range limit). Inverting gives a convenient virtual-ground summing node — easy to add and filter signals — but its input impedance is only Rg, so it loads the source.
Single vs dual supply
Dual (±V) is simplest for bipolar signals. Single supply needs a mid-rail reference and rail-to-rail parts, but it’s cheaper and battery-friendly.
Precision vs speed
Zero-drift parts give µV offsets but modest GBW and more noise up high; fast parts carry bigger offsets. You rarely get both cheaply — pick by whether DC accuracy or bandwidth rules the job.
Discrete op-amp vs instrumentation amp
For a true differential sensor with common-mode noise, a three-op-amp in-amp (or a monolithic one) gives high CMRR and one-resistor gain. Don’t fake it with a single-op-amp difference amp unless CMRR is loose.
The last mile — what you still do with real tools This page gets you to a correct schematic, the right class of part, and sane numbers — about 90% of the job. The last 10% is on the bench and in the simulator, and it’s where analog bites. Simulate stability in LTspice or TINA-TI with the manufacturer’s real op-amp model: run a loop-gain (STB) analysis and read the phase margin — aim for ≥ 45°, ideally 60°. The ideal-op-amp math simply cannot see this. Then face breadboard and PCB reality: proper supply decoupling at the pins, short feedback traces, a ground that isn’t a loop — a perfect schematic oscillates on a sloppy layout. And measure, don’t trust typicals: real offset, drift over temperature and noise are often 2–3× the datasheet “typical,” so budget with the max column. Tools to reach for: LTspice and TINA-TI (free, with vendor models), a scope (for ringing and oscillation), and the vendor’s op-amp selector, FilterPro and analog-design tools for filters and in-amps. The physics in Part I never changes; the last mile is making a real, imperfect part obey it on a real board.

10Check yourself

Question 1

An inverting amplifier is built for a signal gain of −9 (Rf = 90 kΩ, Rg = 10 kΩ). Its closed-loop bandwidth comes out lower than you’d guess from “9.” Why — and what number actually sets it?

Because bandwidth is set by the noise gain, not the signal gain. The noise gain is 1 + Rf/Rg = 1 + 9 = 10, so the −3 dB bandwidth is GBW/10, not GBW/9. An inverting stage always has a noise gain one larger in magnitude than its signal gain (the “+1” from the non-inverting input), and it’s that noise gain — the gain the feedback network presents back to the op-amp — that the loop actually works against. The same number sets the offset error and the stability margin, which is why you track it, not the headline “9.”
Question 2

You cascade the sloppy op-amp (its open-loop gain A varies three-to-one between units) into a non-inverting ×10 circuit with 0.1% resistors. How much does the ×10 gain actually vary unit-to-unit — and why isn’t it 3:1?

About 0.1% — it tracks the resistors, not the op-amp. The closed-loop gain is G ≈ 1/β = 1 + Rf/Rg, set by the ratio of two 0.1% resistors, so the gain’s spread is the resistors’ spread. The op-amp’s wild 3:1 variation in A doesn’t pass through: it survives only inside the tiny error term 1/(1 + Aβ), which for a gain of 10 is on the order of 1/(A/10) ≈ a few parts per hundred-thousand. A three-to-one swing in a part-per-hundred-thousand correction is still a part-per-hundred-thousand correction — the loop gain has divided the sloppiness down below anything you can measure. That division is the entire point of closing the loop.
Question 3

Your integrator (a capacitor in the feedback path) slowly drifts to the positive rail with no input at all. What’s happening, and what’s the one-component fix?

A pure capacitive feedback gives the op-amp infinite gain at DC — a cap is an open circuit at zero frequency, so there is no DC feedback path. With nothing to correct it against, the op-amp’s own input offset voltage and bias current get integrated indefinitely, and the output ramps until it hits a rail. The one-component fix is a large resistor across the capacitor: it bounds the DC gain to Rf/Rg and gives those DC offsets a path to be nulled, while leaving the integrating behaviour intact above the corner the resistor-cap pair sets. You’ve traded a little low-frequency response for a circuit that no longer walks itself into the rail.
