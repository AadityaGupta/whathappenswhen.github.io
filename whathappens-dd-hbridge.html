The H-Bridge, Gate Drive & Dead-Time: How Four Switches Spin a Motor Both Ways

You have one DC supply and a motor you need to run forwards and backwards, at any speed, without cooking everything as heat. Four switches in an H solve direction; switching fast (PWM) solves efficient speed control; and a tiny, mandatory sliver of time called dead-time is all that stands between a working drive and a bang. This is the output stage under almost every motor, inverter and class-D amp.

≈ 18 min read power electronics · gate drive · motor control companion to Control Systems and Field-Oriented Control

You have one DC rail — say +12 V and ground — and a brushed motor you need to run forwards and backwards, at any speed from zero to full, without pouring the difference away as heat. Each requirement defeats the obvious circuit on its own axis: a single switch in series with the motor can only interrupt one direction of current, never reverse it from a single-polarity supply; and throttling the speed with a partly-on switch, used as a dropping resistor, burns the excess voltage as heat until it melts. The way out is two reframes. Direction becomes a matter of which diagonal of four switches you close — the H-bridge. Speed becomes a duty cycle: switch fully on and fully off, fast, so the switch is almost never in the lossy middle and the motor’s inductance averages the chopped voltage. From those two moves the whole subject falls out — efficiency splitting cleanly into conduction and switching loss, the gate driver that exists only to ram charge into a gate fast, and one iron rule that governs every half-bridge: the two switches in a leg must never be on together. Because a real MOSFET turns off slower than you command it, honouring that rule forces a tiny, mandatory sliver of time — dead-time — and that sliver is all that stands between a working drive and a bang. This piece builds that chain from the one constraint, then turns to the half the textbooks skip: how you design a bridge, how it blows up, what to spec, and a 12 V, 5 A drive sized end to end.

1The constraint: one supply, both directions, no heat

Start with exactly what you’re given and what you need. The supply is a single DC rail, +12 V and ground — one polarity, nothing negative to reach for. The load is a brushed DC motor, and the spec is to drive it forwards and backwards, at a speed you can dial from zero to full, efficiently. Three requirements, and each one defeats the naïve circuit on a different axis.

Put a single transistor in series with the motor and you can switch it on and off — in one direction only. Current runs from the rail, through the motor, to ground; there is no way to make it run the other way without a negative supply or a second path, so one switch can never reverse. And suppose you try to vary the speed by holding that transistor partly on, using it as a variable resistor that drops the excess voltage. Now it dissipates P = Vdrop · I as heat: at half speed it is dropping roughly half the rail at the full motor current, burning about a quarter of the motor’s full power in one small component — which melts. So the obvious approaches each fail on a separate count: one switch cannot reverse, and linear control cannot be efficient. We need a circuit that reverses the current and controls speed without ever leaning on a lossy dropping element.

2The reframe: switch the polarity, and switch fast

Two ideas, each a reframe, and together they dissolve the whole problem. First, direction. Build an H-bridge: four switches arranged as two “half-bridges,” each a high-side switch to +V and a low-side switch to ground, with the motor bridged between the two midpoints — the crossbar of the H. Close the top-left and bottom-right switches and current runs through the motor left-to-right (call it forward); close the top-right and bottom-left pair and it runs right-to-left (reverse). Direction is no longer a property of the supply, which never changes polarity; it is simply which diagonal you close.

Second, speed — and here the move is a refusal. Do not throttle with a partly-on switch. Instead switch fully on and fully off, fast, over and over: pulse-width modulation. A switch that is fully on drops almost nothing (V ≈ I·Rds(on), a few milliohms’ worth); a switch that is fully off carries no current. In both states the product V·I is nearly zero, so the switch barely heats, no matter how long it sits there. The motor’s inductance can’t follow the fast chopping and averages it instead, so the motor sees an effective voltage set by the fraction of each cycle the switch is on — the duty cycle D:

Vavg = D · Vthe motor sees the duty-cycle fraction of the rail

So we have turned “reverse the motor and vary its speed efficiently” into “pick a diagonal, choose a duty” — two discrete choices, both lossless in principle, with the heat-burning middle ground designed out entirely.

Pick a diagonal, choose a duty — direction and speed from four switches

Drag the duty cycle and toggle the direction. Watch the conducting diagonal light up — top-left with bottom-right for forward, the other pair for reverse — and the motor terminal voltage chop between the rail and zero at the switching frequency. During each off-interval the current can’t stop, so it freewheels through the opposite path rather than dying; the average terminal voltage Vavg tracks the duty, D·V one way and its mirror the other. Direction is which diagonal; speed is the duty — two clean choices, and nowhere a lossy throttle.

3The core: why switching is efficient, and what it costs

Put real numbers behind the efficiency claim, because it is not free — it is cheap, which is different. A switch’s instantaneous dissipation is always P = Vswitch · I, and there are three operating points. Fully on: the voltage across the switch is only I·Rds(on) (tens of milliohms), so the loss is small and purely resistive. Fully off: the current is essentially zero, so the loss is essentially zero. Half on (linear): both V and I are large at once, so the dissipation is huge — and this is precisely the region PWM avoids by racing through it on every edge.

So the honest accounting splits a switch’s loss into two pieces. The first is conduction loss, paid the whole time the switch carries current:

Pcond = I2 · Rds(on)per conducting FET — small, because Rds(on) is milliohms

The second is switching loss, paid at every edge, because during the finite rise and fall the switch is forced through that lossy middle while both voltage and current are non-trivial:

Psw = ½ · V · I · (tr + tf) · fswpaid at every edge — and it climbs with switching frequency

That tradeoff — conduction against switching loss — is why fsw is a deliberate choice and never a free parameter: raise it and switching loss climbs linearly, lower it and you spend proportionally more of each period conducting (and you hear it whine). And this is exactly why a gate driver exists at all. A power MOSFET switches by charging its gate with a fixed charge Qg up to the gate voltage Vgs; the faster you ram that charge in and yank it back out, the shorter tr and tf become, and the less time the switch spends in the lossy middle. But doing it fast takes real current — the peak gate current is the charge divided by the switching time:

Ig = Qgtswitchthe peak gate current — amps, not milliamps

A microcontroller pin cannot source that; a gate driver can. And because the high-side switch’s source terminal swings all the way up to +V when it conducts, its gate must be held several volts above the rail to keep it on — which is the whole reason for the bootstrap capacitor (topped up through the low-side’s on-time) or a separate isolated supply. The gate driver is not an accessory; it is the thing that makes the switching cheap in the first place.

4The inevitability: dead-time is forced by real switches

Here is the punchline the whole topic turns on. In any half-bridge the high-side and low-side switches must never be on at the same time. If they are, you have wired a dead short from +V straight to ground through two closed switches — a shoot-through current limited only by a few milliohms, a spike of hundreds of amps that vaporises the FETs in microseconds. In an ideal world you would simply command “high-side off at the very instant the low-side turns on” and there would be no overlap at all.

But real MOSFETs do not obey instantly. A FET turns off slower than you command it: there is a turn-off propagation delay, and then the gate has to discharge through its Miller plateau, together tens to well over a hundred nanoseconds of lag between “off” on the logic pin and “off” in the silicon. If you turn one switch on the moment you command the other off, the one still finishing its turn-off overlaps the one already turning on — and that is shoot-through, by construction. So you are forced to insert a dead-time: a short interval, after turning one switch off, during which both switches are off, before the other is allowed on.

The payoff — one law under all the choices
tdead > toffthe one inequality a half-bridge may never break

A half-bridge is governed by one unbreakable rule — never both switches on at once — and one forced consequence — a dead-time longer than the FET’s real turn-off delay. Efficiency, direction and speed are all choices you get to make. This one is not: it is demanded by the asymmetry between how fast a FET turns on and how slowly it actually turns off. Everything else in the subject is engineering; this is law.

Shrink the dead-time below the turn-off delay — and both switches conduct

Zoom into one leg’s switching edge. Drag the dead-time — the gap where both gates are commanded off — and the FET turn-off delay, the time the outgoing switch really takes to stop conducting. While the dead-time stays comfortably longer than the turn-off delay the two never overlap and the bus current is clean. Shrink it below the turn-off delay and the outgoing FET is still on when the incoming one turns on: the gate drives overlap, and a shoot-through spike erupts across the supply — the verdict flips from safe to destructive. That one inequality, tdead > toff, is the whole ballgame.

Limit check — read the dead-time at its ends Dead-time → 0 (or below the turn-off delay): the two switches overlap → shoot-through, a destructive current spike straight across the supply, limited only by milliohms. The FETs die. This is the floor you must never cross. Dead-time too large: during the gap neither switch drives the node, so the inductive motor current is forced through a body diode (freewheeling), which drops more voltage, burns more loss, and leaves the output voltage wrong for that sliver — dead-time distortion, worst at low duty and high frequency, corrupting low-speed control and adding torque ripple. fsw → high: switching loss (∝ f) and gate-drive power (∝ f) come to dominate and the dead-time eats a bigger fraction of each period — but current ripple shrinks and you climb above the audible band. fsw → low: conduction-dominated and efficient, but it whines audibly and the ripple (hence inductor and capacitor size) grows. Rds(on) → 0 (the ideal switch): conduction loss vanishes — but a bigger, lower-Rds FET usually carries more gate charge Qg, so you pay it straight back in switching and drive loss. There is no free switch.

Follow the off-interval through and a quieter necessity appears. During the dead-time — and during the whole off-portion of every PWM cycle — the motor’s inductance refuses to let its current stop, so that current must freewheel: recirculate somewhere. Left alone it flows through a FET’s body diode; better, you deliberately turn the opposite FET on to carry it at a far lower drop (synchronous rectification). Either way, the freewheel path is not an afterthought — it is the reason an inductive load does not destroy the bridge at every turn-off.

Push this one step further and the H-bridge stops being a mere direction switch. By choosing which switches carry the freewheel current you can run the motor in all four quadrants — driving or braking, in either direction — and when the motor overhauls or brakes it acts as a generator, so the same bridge pumps that energy back into the supply (regenerative braking). This is where locked-antiphase PWM earns its keep: drive both legs out of phase and the motor’s average voltage becomes

Vavg = (2D − 1) · Vlocked-antiphase: one duty, both directions — D = ½ is rest

so a single duty number spans the whole range: D = ½ is rest, above it drives forward, below it reverses, with smooth four-quadrant control and easy regen. The H-bridge, read this way, is a bidirectional power converter that happens to be pointed at a motor.

5Where it lives: designing a drive, and how it blows up

The H-bridge is the output stage under almost everything that moves electrically: every brushed-DC drive, each phase of a BLDC or stepper (three half-bridges for a BLDC), every class-D amplifier, every motor inverter. And real design is a fixed sequence, not a flash of insight: size the FETs (a Vds rating comfortably above supply plus inductive margin; Rds(on) for conduction; Qg for switching); choose a gate driver (bootstrap for the high-side, enough peak current to switch fast, matched propagation delay); set fsw (above ~20 kHz to clear the audible band, below the switching-loss wall); set the dead-time (just above the FET turn-off delay); add current sense and protection. When a bridge fails it fails in a short, vivid vocabulary — and the worst entries are always shoot-through:

FETs explode / loud bang / supply collapses
Shoot-through: dead-time too short, or gate ringing re-triggering a FET that should be off. The single most destructive failure there is — find it with a current probe on the DC bus, which shows the spike at every edge.
FETs run hot but no bang
Plain dissipation: Rds(on) too high (conduction loss) or fsw too high / the gates too slow (switching loss). Thermal, not catastrophic, but it quietly ages the parts.
High-side won’t turn on, or droops at full throttle
The bootstrap cap isn’t being refreshed: it recharges only while the low-side is on, so near 100 % duty it starves the high-side gate. Needs a periodic low-side pulse or a charge pump.
Motor whines audibly
fsw is sitting in the audible band — raise it above ~20 kHz and the whine disappears.
Erratic or dead at very low speed
Dead-time distortion: the dead-time is a large fraction of a tiny pulse, so low duty cycles don’t come out linear. Compensate it in firmware.
Mystery EMI, resets, ringing
Fast dv/dt and di/dt ringing on layout parasitics. Tighten the loops, slow the gates slightly, add a snubber.

6How these choices shape the whole drive

Three knobs set almost everything downstream, and they are not independent. Dead-time sets the minimum controllable pulse width, and therefore low-speed linearity and torque ripple — too much and the drive goes lumpy near zero. Switching frequency trades four things at once: audible noise, EMI, switching loss, and current ripple (hence the size of your inductor and capacitors); raise it for quiet running and small magnetics, lower it for efficiency. Gate-drive strength — how fast you make the edges — trades switching loss against EMI: fast edges are efficient but radiate and ring, slow edges are quiet but hot. And the FET choice, Rds(on) against Qg, sets the conduction/switching balance and the whole thermal design. Pick fsw and you have constrained the FET; the FET constrains the driver; the driver constrains the layout. Designing a bridge is walking that loop until it closes.

7Where it’s used, and what to spec

The identical circuit drives brushed DC motors, BLDC and PMSM machines (with field-oriented control layered on top), steppers, solenoids and valves, class-D audio stages, DC–DC converters, and grid inverters — anywhere current must be steered and chopped under an inductive load. When you pick the parts, these are the numbers that actually decide the outcome:

FET Vds rating
At least supply plus margin for inductive overshoot and ringing — rule of thumb 1.5–2×. Under-rating it is a fast, loud death.
Rds(on)
Conduction loss, I2·R·(duty). This is the efficiency number at high current.
Gate charge Qg
Sets switching loss and the gate-drive current you need (Ipeak ≈ Qg/tswitch). It trades directly against Rds(on).
Gate driver: peak source/sink, bootstrap, prop-delay match, UVLO
Strong enough to switch fast, and with matched propagation delay so your dead-time stays the number you designed.
Dead-time (fixed vs programmable)
Just above the FET turn-off delay; programmable lets you tune it to the actual parts you soldered.
Switching frequency
Above audible, below the loss wall. It sets both the ripple and the magnetics.
Current sense & protection (OCP, SOA, thermal)
A bridge without fast overcurrent protection is a bridge waiting to shoot through exactly once.

8Field notes

What the clean derivation won’t tell you
  • Dead-time is never free. It distorts the output and is the number-one source of low-speed torque ripple. If low-speed smoothness matters, measure it and compensate it in firmware — don’t just pick a datasheet typical and hope.
  • A FET you turned off can turn itself back on. A fast rising voltage on the half-bridge node couples through the gate-drain (Miller) capacitance and can lift the gate above threshold → false shoot-through, with no logic error anywhere. Fix it with a gate resistor, a Miller clamp, or negative off-drive.
  • The high-side bootstrap must be refreshed. The cap recharges only while the low-side conducts, so a sustained 100 % duty starves the high-side gate. Plan a periodic refresh pulse or a charge pump if you need a true full-on state.
  • Layout is the circuit. The gate loop and the power commutation loop must be physically tiny; a sloppy layout rings, couples and shoots through no matter how perfect the schematic. This is where bridges actually fail.
  • Measure shoot-through, don’t guess it. A current probe on the DC bus shows the tell-tale spike at every switching edge if your dead-time is short; a scope on the two gates shows the overlap directly. Both are cheap to check and expensive to skip.
  • Synchronous rectification cuts loss but raises the stakes. Turning the opposite FET on during freewheel beats the body-diode drop, but now both FETs are being commanded near each other — so dead-time becomes even more critical, not less.
  • Body-diode reverse recovery is a hidden tax. At high fsw the silicon body diode’s recovery charge is real loss and a big EMI source; Schottky catch diodes, or GaN/SiC devices (no or benign body diode), change the whole calculus.
  • “Logic-level” does not mean “drive it from a GPIO.” A logic-level FET still needs real gate current to switch fast; driving a gate straight off a micro pin switches it slowly, parks it in the linear region, and cooks it. Always use a driver.

9Build it: a 12 V, 5 A bidirectional brushed-DC drive at 20 kHz

Enough principle — here is a real drive sized end to end, so you can copy the shape and reuse it. The job: run a 12 V, 5 A brushed DC motor both directions with PWM speed control at fsw = 20 kHz. Every line below is one formula you plug into; the derivation already did the hard part.

Worked example — a 12 V, 5 A H-bridge at 20 kHz
FET voltage
Vds must clear 12 V plus inductive and ringing margin → pick 30 V FETs (a comfortable 2.5× margin). A logic-level N-channel with Rds(on) ≈ 10 mΩ.
Conduction loss
Per conducting FET, P = I2·Rds = 52·0.010 = 0.25 W; two FETs conduct in a diagonal → ~0.5 W total. Trivial, thermally easy.
Switching loss
Per switching FET, ½·V·I·(tr+tf)·fsw = ½·12·5·(50 ns)·20 kHz ≈ 0.3 W. Comparable to conduction here — which is exactly why 20 kHz, not 200 kHz.
Gate drive
Qg ≈ 15 nC. Average drive power = Qg·Vgs·fsw·4 = 15n·10·20k·4 ≈ 12 mW (negligible). But the peak current to switch in 50 ns is Qg/t = 15n/50n = 0.3 A → choose a driver that sources and sinks ≥ 0.5 A.
Dead-time
FET turn-off delay is ~30–50 ns → set dead-time ≈ 100–150 ns (2–3× margin). Enough never to overlap, small enough that the distortion at 20 kHz (a 50 µs period) stays well under 1 %.
Bootstrap cap
Must supply Qg plus leakage over one on-time with little droop: Cboot ≥ Qg/ΔV ≈ 15 nC / 0.1 V = 150 nF → use 1 µF for margin.
Topology & parts
A full H-bridge (4 N-channel FETs) driven by a gate-driver IC with built-in bootstrap and dead-time (e.g. a DRV8701, or two IR2104 half-bridge drivers). A low-side shunt for current sense and overcurrent.

That is the method made concrete: a supply, a current, a frequency and a FET datasheet hand you every loss, the driver strength, the dead-time, and the bootstrap cap — and they tell you, loudly, that at these numbers conduction and switching loss are comparable and both tiny, so the real risk is never heat, it is the shoot-through you must engineer out. The plug-in set is small: average motor voltage Vavg = D·Vsupply (sign-magnitude) or Vavg = (2D−1)·Vsupply (locked-antiphase); conduction loss per FET Pcond = I2·Rds(on)·(duty); switching loss per FET Psw = ½·V·I·(tr+tf)·fsw; peak gate current Ig = Qg/tswitch and gate-drive power Pg = Qg·Vgs·fsw·(#FETs); the dead-time floor tdead > toff with 2–3× margin; and the bootstrap cap Cboot ≥ Qg/ΔVboot, then ×5–10 for margin.

Bring it up in this order. The sequence matters; skipping a step just relocates the pain — usually to the moment something bangs:

  1. Everything off, probe the bus. Power the bridge with all four FETs commanded off; a current probe on the DC bus should read ~0. Any spike means a wiring or logic error before you have switched anything.
  2. Scope the two gates of one half-bridge. Command a low duty and confirm on the scope that the high-side and low-side gates are never high together, with a clear dead-time gap between them. Measure that gap in nanoseconds.
  3. Scope the half-bridge node. Confirm a clean PWM waveform swinging rail-to-rail at fsw, with edges that are fast but not ringing up into the rails.
  4. Ramp the duty, watch the bus current for the shoot-through spike at the edges. If you see it, increase the dead-time or slow the turn-on — before you load anything.
  5. Load it and check thermals. Run the real motor, feel or measure the FET temperature; conduction plus switching loss should match your estimate (~1 W per FET here).
  6. Reverse and brake. Flip the diagonal, test a direction change, then force a braking or overcurrent event and verify the current limit actually trips.

The forks that actually change the design. Most parameters you can trim later; these you decide up front:

Discrete FETs + driver vs integrated motor-driver IC
An integrated DRV8xxx-class part hides the bootstrap, dead-time and protection and ships fast; discrete gives you higher current, voltage and control — but now you own the layout and the shoot-through risk.
N-channel high-side (bootstrap) vs P-channel high-side
N-channel is more efficient (lower Rds for the money) but needs a gate driven above the rail (bootstrap or charge pump); P-channel is simpler to drive but bigger and lossier for the same Rds.
Sign-magnitude vs locked-antiphase PWM
Sign-magnitude (one leg static, one chopping) is simpler and lower-ripple near zero; locked-antiphase (both legs chop out of phase) gives smooth four-quadrant control and easy regen but more ripple and loss at rest.
Si vs GaN/SiC
GaN/SiC switch faster with no nasty body-diode recovery → higher fsw, smaller magnetics, higher efficiency; but the fast edges demand near-perfect layout and tight dead-time, and cost more.
The last mile — what you still do with real tools This page gets you a correct topology, the right FET and driver class, and sizing numbers that are right to a factor well under 2 — about 90 % of the job. The last 10 % is layout and the scope, and it is where bridges actually live or die. Lay out the loops tight: the gate loop and the power commutation loop must be small and low-inductance — follow the driver datasheet’s layout page religiously, because most “random” shoot-through is layout ringing, not your dead-time number. Measure dead-time and shoot-through on real hardware — gates on a scope, bus current on a current probe — and tune the dead-time to the FETs you actually soldered, not the datasheet typical. Thermal-test under worst-case load and duty, with the real heatsink and airflow; switching loss in particular is easy to under-estimate. And chase the EMI from those fast edges, conducted and radiated — a gate resistor and a snubber are your tuning knobs between efficiency and noise. Tools to reach for: a motor-driver eval board to start, LTspice for the gate-drive loop and the loss estimate, a scope and current probe for dead-time and shoot-through, and the driver IC’s datasheet layout guidance. The four-switch idea in Part I never changes; the last mile is making real FETs commutate cleanly on a real board without ever letting both ends of a leg conduct at once.

10Check yourself

Question 1

Your bridge works fine at 50 % duty, but the high-side FET stops driving when you command 100 % (full speed). Nothing is overheating. What’s starving, and why does it only happen at full duty?

The high-side bootstrap capacitor is starving. That cap is what holds the high-side gate several volts above the rail, and it can only recharge while the low-side FET is on, pulling the half-bridge node down to ground so the cap tops up from the supply. At 50 % duty the low-side turns on every cycle and keeps the cap full; at 100 % duty the low-side never turns on, so the cap is never refreshed, its charge bleeds away, and the high-side gate supply collapses — the FET can no longer be held on. It’s duty-specific because the refresh mechanism is the low-side on-time itself. The fix is a periodic low-side refresh pulse (giving up true 100 %) or a charge pump that doesn’t depend on the low-side conducting.
Question 2

You shrink the dead-time to reduce low-speed distortion, and the FETs start running hot and occasionally bang. What did you cross, and what sets the floor you can’t go below?

You dropped the dead-time below the FET’s real turn-off delay, so the outgoing high- and low-side switches now overlap — shoot-through. At first it shows only as extra heat (brief current spikes at every edge, bleeding energy through both FETs); push it further and one of those spikes finishes a FET off, which is the bang. The floor is not a number you choose for smoothness — it is the actual turn-off time of the FET (propagation delay plus the Miller-plateau discharge) plus margin, tdead > toff. And you measure that on the parts you soldered rather than trusting a typical, because the whole failure is the gap between commanded-off and really-off.
Question 3

Two designs run the same motor: one at 8 kHz, one at 40 kHz. One whines audibly and has large current ripple; the other is quiet but its FETs are hotter. Which is which, and what’s the single trade behind both differences?

The 8 kHz design whines — 8 kHz is squarely in the audible band — and has the large current ripple, because the motor’s inductance has longer between edges to let its current drift. The 40 kHz design is quiet (above hearing) with small ripple, but its FETs are hotter. The single trade behind both is switching frequency: noise and current ripple both fall as fsw rises, while switching loss (paid at every edge) rises with it — so you buy quiet running and small ripple with heat, and buy efficiency with whine and ripple. There is no setting that wins all three, which is why fsw is a deliberate choice.
