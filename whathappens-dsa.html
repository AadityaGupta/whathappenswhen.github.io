Track 01

Heaps, merges & scheduling

Priority queues and k-way merges: how databases compact, how streams find their top-k and their running median, how schedulers pick what runs next.

01LC 23 · 378

Multi-way merge becomes the LSM-tree compaction engine

The problem Merge k already-sorted lists into one sorted stream with a size-k min-heap: pop the global minimum, then refill from the list it came from. LC 378 is the same move over a sorted matrix.

Analogy Several checkout lanes merging into one queue by a host who always waves through whoever has waited longest — you only ever compare the fronts of the lanes, never the whole crowd.

An LSM-tree (LevelDB, RocksDB, Cassandra) never updates in place: writes land in a memtable, flushed to an immutable, internally-sorted file — an SSTable. Files pile up, so a background compaction merges several sorted SSTables into one, and that merge is exactly LC 23 — a k-way merge over the files’ key streams driven by a min-heap on each file’s next key.

The heap is what makes it cheap: each output key costs O(log k) to pick the current minimum and advance one cursor, so the whole merge is O(N log k), streaming, with only k keys resident. On a tie (same key in several files) you keep the newest version and drop the rest — how updates and deletes (tombstones) are applied.

The merge is sequential I/O, which is why LSM-trees are write-optimised — but compaction competes with live traffic for disk and CPU: the classic write- vs read-amplification tension. Size-tiered compaction merges similar-sized files (cheap writes, more read amplification); leveled keeps one run per level (tighter reads, more write amplification). Get it wrong and compaction debt shows up as p99 stalls and space bloat.

THREE SORTED SSTABLES MERGING VIA A SIZE-K MIN-HEAP

Each input is a sorted run; the heap pops the global minimum and refills from that run. Raise k and per-step cost grows as log k, not k.

Check yourself Why a size-k heap rather than concatenating the files and sorting? The inputs are already sorted, so no global sort is needed: a size-k heap merges them in one pass at O(N log k) and O(k) memory, versus O(N log N) time and O(N) memory to re-sort — decisive when a file is multi-GB and cannot fit in RAM.

02LC 295

Two heaps track a running median — the seed of latency-SLO monitoring

The problem LC 295 maintains the median of a value stream under inserts. Keep a max-heap of the lower half and a min-heap of the upper half, rebalanced so their sizes differ by at most one; the median sits at the heaps’ tops, read in O(1), inserted in O(log n).

Analogy A librarian splitting returns onto two trolleys — everything below the middle on the left, everything above on the right — and nudging one book across whenever the trolleys get lopsided, so the middle book is always right where the two trolleys meet.

The two-heaps structure is the textbook online order-statistic: a max-heap holds the smaller half, a min-heap the larger half. Every insert pushes to one side, then a rebalance shuttles one element across so the sizes differ by at most one; the median is the top of the larger heap (odd count) or the mean of both tops (even). This is the clean mental model behind every ‘what is my current p50?’ a monitor answers over a latency stream.

But a latency dashboard doesn’t want just the median — it wants p50, p90, p99 and p999 at once, and two heaps give only one fixed rank while growing O(n): holding every request’s latency for a day is impossible. So production swaps exact two-heaps for bounded sketches. HdrHistogram buckets values into fixed-precision bins; t-digest (Elasticsearch, a Postgres extension) keeps variable-width centroids that pack densely near the tails where p99/p999 live.

The property the sketches add is mergeability: you cannot average per-host p99s, but you can merge each host’s t-digest or HdrHistogram into a cluster-wide one and read p99 off that — how fleet-wide SLOs are actually computed. The exact two-heaps median is still the right tool for an exact median over a bounded window, such as a sensor’s rolling median filter.

Two heaps are exact at O(log n) insert and O(1) median, but cost O(n) memory and yield a single quantile. Sketches trade a bounded error (t-digest ~1% near the median, tighter at the tails by design; HdrHistogram a configured significant-figure bound) for fixed memory and many quantiles at once. The classic failure is averaging quantiles across hosts — p99 is not linear, so a mean of p99s understates the real tail; merge the sketches, never the percentiles.

A VALUE STREAM SPLIT INTO A LOW MAX-HEAP AND A HIGH MIN-HEAP, REBALANCING AT THE MEDIAN

Watch each new value land in one heap, then a rebalance shuttle one element across so the two tops always straddle the median.

Check yourself Why can’t you compute a cluster’s p99 by averaging each host’s p99? Percentiles aren’t additive: an idle host still reports some p99, so averaging dilutes one genuinely hot host’s tail. You must merge the underlying distributions — combine the histograms/t-digests into one and read p99 off the merged structure — which is exactly why production quantile sketches are built to be mergeable.

03LC 347 · 703

A bounded min-heap of size k becomes the ‘trending now’ engine

The problem LC 347 asks for the k most frequent elements; LC 703 for the kth largest in a live stream. The streaming trick is a size-k min-heap: keep only the current top k, and when a newcomer beats the smallest of them (the heap root), evict the root and push the newcomer.

Analogy A club with room for only the ten tallest guests: the bouncer always knows the shortest person inside, and waves someone new in only if they’re taller than that shortest — who then gets shown the door.

The counter-intuitive move is using a min-heap to track the largest: the root is the weakest survivor, so one O(log k) compare-and-maybe-replace per element keeps the top k without ever sorting the whole stream. For ‘top-K frequent’ you first tally counts in a hash map, then stream the (item, count) pairs through the size-k heap — O(n log k), with only k entries resident. This is the shape of leaderboards, ‘most-viewed’, and trending-tag jobs.

At true scale (billions of events, millions of distinct keys) you can’t hold exact counts, so systems pair the heap with an approximate counter: a Count-Min Sketch or the Space-Saving / Misra-Gries algorithm estimates per-key frequency in fixed memory, and the size-k heap sits on top to surface the heavy hitters. Redis ZSET (a skiplist) answers ZREVRANGE top-k for exact leaderboards; Kafka Streams and Flink keep per-window top-k with exactly this heap over a state store.

Exact top-k needs the full frequency table — O(distinct) memory — with the heap adding only O(k); fine until ‘distinct’ is huge. Approximate heavy-hitters (Space-Saving) bound memory to O(k/ε) counters but can misorder keys near the boundary, guaranteeing only that truly frequent items are never missed (overestimate, never under). The sharp edge is churn: a size-k heap over a tumbling window must be rebuilt or decayed each window, or yesterday’s trend lingers as a zombie.

A STREAM FLOWING THROUGH A SIZE-K MIN-HEAP THAT EVICTS ITS ROOT WHEN BEATEN

Each arrival is compared to the heap’s root; only a bigger value displaces it. Shrink k and watch the admission bar rise.

Check yourself Why a min-heap, not a max-heap, to keep the top k largest? You compare each newcomer against the weakest current member, which must be instantly readable; a min-heap puts that weakest at the root for O(1) peek and O(log k) eviction. A max-heap would surface the strongest — useless for deciding who to evict — and finding the minimum in it is O(k).

04LC 355

Merging followees’ feeds is a k-way heap merge — fan-out on read vs write

The problem LC 355 (Design Twitter) asks for getNewsFeed: the ten most recent tweets across everyone you follow. With each user’s tweets already in time order, that’s a k-way merge — seed a max-heap with the newest tweet from each followee and pop ten times, pulling the next tweet from whichever feed you just drained.

Analogy Ten friends each hand you their diary open to today; you keep copying down whichever page shows the most recent entry, turn that one diary forward a page, and repeat until you’ve collected the latest ten stories overall.

The heap merge is the fan-out-on-read design: store each user’s tweets once, and assemble a timeline on demand by merging followees’ sorted tweet lists. It is cheap to write (one append) and the merge is O(k + 10 log k) per read — but a read now touches k feeds, brutal when you follow thousands and repeated on every refresh.

The opposite is fan-out-on-write (push): when you tweet, it is copied into every follower’s precomputed timeline, so a read is one sequential fetch. Reads become trivial; writes explode — a celebrity with 100M followers triggers 100M inserts per tweet (the classic fan-out / thundering-herd problem). Real Twitter runs a hybrid: push for ordinary users into per-user timeline caches (Redis lists), and fall back to the on-read heap merge for the handful of celebrities, merging their tweets in at read time.

So the LC heap merge is not the whole system — it is the read-path primitive you keep for the cases where precomputing would be wasteful. The decision is a write-amplification-vs-read-amplification trade, and the celebrity exception is the tell that no single strategy wins.

Fan-out-on-write turns a timeline read into one cache hit (great p99) at the cost of storage (every tweet duplicated across followers) and huge write amplification for high-degree accounts; fan-out-on-read keeps storage lean and writes cheap but makes reads O(k) and spiky. The hybrid’s failure mode is the boundary: misclassify a suddenly-viral user as ‘ordinary’ and the push path melts — the fix is a follower-count threshold that moves accounts onto the on-read path.

Check yourself Why does a pure fan-out-on-write (push) model break for celebrities, and what is the usual fix? Each of their tweets fans out to every follower’s timeline — 100M writes for one tweet — saturating the write path and delaying delivery. The fix is a hybrid: don’t pre-push celebrity tweets; instead merge them into each viewer’s timeline at read time with a small k-way heap, so write cost is bounded and only readers who follow them pay.

05LC 621 · 1834

A priority queue of ready tasks becomes the OS scheduler and the timer wheel

The problem LC 621 (Task Scheduler) packs tasks under a cooldown so the CPU idles as little as possible; LC 1834 (single-threaded CPU) runs the available job with the smallest burst next. Both are a priority queue over ‘what is runnable right now’, keyed by whichever metric the policy optimises.

Analogy A barista with one machine and a cooldown between pulls of the same drink: they always start the most-pending order that’s allowed right now, and when nothing is allowed they wipe the counter — a forced idle tick.

LC 1834’s ‘pick the shortest available job’ is Shortest-Job-First, built from a min-heap keyed on burst time plus a second structure, sorted by arrival, that releases jobs into the ready heap as the clock advances. That two-queue shape — a time-ordered waiting set feeding a priority-ordered ready set — is exactly how an OS scheduler separates ‘not yet runnable’ from ‘runnable, pick the best’.

Real schedulers generalise the key. Linux’s CFS keeps runnable tasks in a red-black tree ordered by virtual runtime and always runs the least-advanced — a priority queue by fairness rather than burst. Priority-based real-time schedulers use an explicit heap by priority. And the cooldown in LC 621 is the kernel of rate-limited scheduling: don’t rerun the same task until its window elapses.

Timers are the same idea rotated: ‘run this callback at time T’ is a priority queue keyed on deadline. A naive min-heap of timers is O(log n) per insert and expiry; at kernel scale (millions of timers) that is too slow, so the Linux kernel and libraries like Netty use a timer wheel — buckets indexed by time slot, giving O(1) amortised insert and expiry at the cost of bounded resolution, with a hierarchy of wheels for long delays.

A binary heap gives O(log n) scheduling and is simple, but Shortest-Job-First can starve long jobs — so production schedulers add aging or fair-share (CFS’s virtual runtime) to bound latency. Timer wheels beat the heap at O(1) but quantise time into ticks and need hierarchical wheels for a wide delay range; pick wheel granularity wrong and you either burn CPU on empty ticks or add jitter to every timer.

Check yourself Why do large-scale timer subsystems prefer a timer wheel over a min-heap of deadlines? A min-heap is O(log n) per insert and per expiry, which dominates when there are millions of short-lived timers. A hashed timer wheel buckets timers by their expiry slot, so insert and fire are O(1) amortised — you just advance a cursor and fire the current bucket — trading exact ordering within a tick for far lower per-timer cost.

Hashing & probabilistic structures

Hash tables, and the sketches that trade a little accuracy for enormous savings: membership, cardinality, and frequency at scale.

06LC 706 · 705

Building a HashMap is building the in-memory index every language ships

The problem LC 706 / 705 ask you to implement HashMap/HashSet from scratch: hash a key to a bucket, then handle collisions so put/get/remove stay O(1) on average. Every choice — bucketing, collision strategy, when to grow — is the one the standard library already made for you.

Analogy A coat-check with numbered hooks: a hash function turns each coat into a hook number. Sometimes two coats map to the same hook, so you either hang them on a little chain off that hook, or walk to the next free hook and remember you did.

Two collision strategies split the world. Separate chaining (Java’s HashMap, most std libs) hangs a list — or, once a bucket grows long, a balanced tree, as Java 8+ does past eight entries — off each bucket: simple, tolerant of high load, but with pointer-chasing and per-node allocation. Open addressing (Python’s dict, Google’s SwissTable, Rust’s hashbrown) stores entries inline and, on collision, probes to another slot (linear, quadratic, or Robin Hood): cache-friendly and allocation-light, but degrading sharply as the table fills.

The load factor α = entries / buckets is the dial. Average probe cost stays near O(1) while α is low, then blows up — open addressing especially, where cost scales like 1/(1−α) and clustering makes runs coalesce. So the table resizes: once α crosses a threshold (Java 0.75, Python ~0.66) it allocates a bigger array and rehashes every entry into it. That resize is an amortised O(n) event once in a while, which is why insertion is ‘amortised O(1)’, not worst-case.

Modern tables add tricks: SwissTable keeps a parallel array of one-byte control tags scanned with SIMD so a lookup checks sixteen slots at once; incremental ‘rehash-as-you-go’ tables (Redis) spread the resize across operations to dodge a latency spike. The hash matters too — a weak hash invites adversarial collisions (HashDoS), which is why many runtimes seed the hash with a random per-process key.

Chaining survives high load factors and bad hashes gracefully (extra entries just lengthen a list/tree) but pays cache misses and allocation; open addressing is faster and denser until α nears one, then clusters and must resize. Resizing is the latency landmine: a stop-the-world rehash of a huge table stalls the put that triggers it, so systems size up front, use incremental rehashing, or over-provision buckets. Worst case for any hash table is adversarial collisions collapsing it to O(n) — mitigated by tree-bins and seeded hashes.

KEYS HASHING INTO BUCKETS, COLLIDING, THEN TRIGGERING A RESIZE WHEN LOAD FACTOR CROSSES THRESHOLD

Insert keys and watch buckets fill; cross the load-factor line and the table doubles and rehashes everything at once.

Check yourself Why is a hash-table insert called ‘amortised O(1)’ rather than just O(1)? Most inserts are O(1), but occasionally one crosses the load-factor threshold and triggers a resize that rehashes all n entries — an O(n) operation. Spread over the n cheap inserts that led to it (and because the table typically doubles, so resizes get geometrically rarer), the per-insert average stays constant: amortised O(1), with an occasional worst-case spike.

07DESIGN

A Bloom filter saves the disk read an LSM lookup would otherwise waste

The problem A Bloom filter answers ‘is this key possibly in the set?’ with a bit array and k hash functions: to add a key, set the k bits it hashes to; to test, check those k bits. All set → ‘maybe present’; any clear → ‘definitely absent’. It can give false positives but never false negatives.

Analogy A guest-list check where each name flips a few light switches on a board. To check a name you look only at its switches: if any is still off they were definitely never added — if all are on they’re probably on the list, but some switches may have been flipped by other guests.

The one-sided guarantee is the whole point. In an LSM-tree (RocksDB, LevelDB, Cassandra, HBase) a key might live in any of dozens of on-disk SSTables, and a point lookup for a missing key would otherwise probe every one. Each SSTable carries a small Bloom filter in memory; a ‘definitely absent’ answer skips that file’s disk read entirely, turning most negative lookups into pure RAM checks — the single biggest read-amplification win for LSM stores.

The same ‘cheap maybe, authoritative no’ appears wherever a miss is common and a disk or network round-trip is expensive: CDNs and caches use it to answer ‘have we ever seen this object?’ before hitting backing store (the one-hit-wonder filter that avoids caching objects seen only once); dedup and backup systems test whether a chunk already exists before writing it; Chrome’s Safe Browsing kept a local Bloom filter of malicious URLs so only a hit escalated to a full server check. Databases also use them for join and semi-join pruning.

Sizing is a formula: for n items and m bits, the optimal hash count is k = (m/n)·ln2, giving a false-positive rate of roughly 0.6185^(m/n) — about 10 bits per element for a 1% FPR, independent of element size. You cannot delete from a plain Bloom filter (clearing bits would break other keys); counting Bloom filters or cuckoo filters add deletion, and cuckoo filters give better locality and lower FPR at high load.

The filter buys memory for I/O: ~10 bits/element for 1% false positives, ~15 for 0.1% — orders of magnitude smaller than the keys, and constant per element regardless of key length. A false positive costs only a wasted lookup (the real store behind it preserves correctness); false negatives are impossible, which is what makes it safe as a gate. It breaks when overfilled — FPR climbs toward one as the array saturates — so capacity must be planned, or use a scalable/partitioned Bloom filter that grows.

KEYS SETTING k BITS EACH; A QUERY HITS ALL BITS (MAYBE) OR A CLEAR BIT (DEFINITELY NOT)

Add keys to watch bits fill, then query present and absent keys; crowd the array and see false positives start to appear.

Check yourself Why is a Bloom filter safe in front of an SSTable even though it returns false positives? Because its error is one-sided: a wrong ‘maybe present’ just causes one unnecessary disk probe, after which the real SSTable reports the key absent — correctness is never compromised. What it guarantees is no false negatives, so it never claims a key is absent when it is actually there; that property is what lets it skip reads without risking a lost key.

08CLASSIC

HyperLogLog counts billions of distinct things in kilobytes

The problem HyperLogLog estimates the number of distinct elements in a stream without storing them. Hash each element and look at the run of leading zeros in the hash: a hash with ρ leading zeros hints the set is at least ~2^ρ big. Keep the max run seen across many independent buckets and combine them to kill the variance.

Analogy Guess how many people flipped coins by asking only for the longest run of heads anyone got. One person with ten heads in a row suggests a big crowd — and averaging the best streak across many separate groups turns a wild guess into a sharp estimate.

The intuition is the longest run of leading zeros as a probabilistic odometer: in uniform hashes a run of ρ zeros occurs about once per 2^ρ distinct values, so the longest run seen is a noisy log of the cardinality. A single estimate is wildly variable, so HLL uses the first few hash bits to pick one of m buckets (registers) and stores the max run per bucket; combining the registers with a harmonic mean, plus bias corrections, gives a tight estimate. It is idempotent — re-adding an element changes nothing — and mergeable: union two HLLs by taking the per-register max.

That mergeability is why it runs analytics at scale. Redis PFADD/PFCOUNT use HLL (about 12KB per key, <1% error) to count unique visitors; Presto, Spark and BigQuery (APPROX_COUNT_DISTINCT) and Druid shard the stream, build per-shard HLLs and merge them for a global unique count — no shuffle of raw IDs, no exact set to hold. Google’s original use was counting distinct search queries; the warehouse pattern is ‘roll up daily HLLs and union them for any date range’.

Standard error is 1.04/√m for m registers, so Redis’s m = 16384 registers × 6 bits ≈ 12KB gives ~0.81% error while counting up to billions — the memory is fixed, independent of cardinality. The cost: you get a count, not the elements, and the error is relative (poor for tiny sets, so HLL falls back to linear counting at low cardinality). You can’t ask ‘which items’ or compute intersections accurately, since inclusion-exclusion on unions amplifies the error.

HASHED ELEMENTS UPDATING PER-BUCKET MAX LEADING-ZERO COUNTS; ESTIMATE CONVERGING ON TRUE CARDINALITY

Feed in distinct elements and watch the estimate track the true count; add more registers to shrink the error band.

Check yourself What two properties let HyperLogLog roll up unique counts across many servers? Idempotence and mergeability. Re-adding an element never changes a register (it only ever keeps a max), so double-counting is impossible; and two HLLs over the same register layout merge by taking the element-wise maximum of their registers, which exactly equals the HLL you’d have built over the union. So each shard or day builds its own sketch and you union them with no raw data exchanged.

09CLASSIC

Count-Min Sketch counts every key’s frequency in fixed memory

The problem A Count-Min Sketch estimates how often each key appears in a stream using a small 2-D array of counters and d hash functions: to add a key, increment one counter in each of the d rows; to query, take the minimum of those d counters. Collisions only ever inflate a count, so the estimate is an upper bound — overestimate-only, never under.

Analogy d parallel tally sheets, each with fewer columns than there are names, so names share columns. Any one sheet overcounts a name (others landed in its column too) — but the smallest tally across all d sheets is closest to the truth, because the honest sheet got the fewest gatecrashers.

Taking the minimum across rows is the trick: every counter a key touches is ≥ its true count (other keys may have incremented it too), so the smallest of the d readings has suffered the least collision noise and bounds the error. With width w and depth d, error is at most ε·N with probability 1−δ for w = e/ε, d = ln(1/δ) — frequencies for millions of keys in a few KB. Misra-Gries is the sibling for the related heavy-hitters question, tracking k candidate counters and decrementing all on overflow.

This runs the measurement plane of networks and streams. It powers top-talker and heavy-hitter detection in switches and DDoS mitigation (count flows per source with no per-flow state), query-frequency and trending estimates, and cache admission — Caffeine’s W-TinyLFU uses a Count-Min Sketch, with periodic aging, to estimate access frequency and decide whether a new item deserves to evict a resident one. Pair it with a size-k heap and you get approximate top-K: the sketch estimates counts, the heap keeps the leaders.

Memory is fixed at w×d counters regardless of key count, updates and queries are O(d), and it is linear/mergeable (add two sketches cell-wise) for distributed aggregation. The price is overestimation that grows with load and skew — a hot key sharing a column inflates rare keys, so CMS is reliable for the frequent and noisy for the rare (usually fine, since you care about heavy hitters). Plain CMS can’t handle deletions meaningfully and needs aging (conservative-update or decay) so stale counts don’t dominate a long-running stream.

A KEY INCREMENTING ONE COUNTER PER ROW; A QUERY RETURNS THE MINIMUM ACROSS ROWS

Add keys until columns collide, then query a rare key and watch collisions inflate it — the row-minimum stays closest to the truth.

Check yourself Why take the minimum of the d counters, and why can the answer never be too low? Each of a key’s d counters is incremented on every occurrence of that key plus any other key colliding into the same cell, so each is an overestimate; the minimum is the one with the fewest colliding additions and thus closest to the truth. It can never undercount because a key always increments all d of its own counters, so every reading is at least the true frequency.

10LC 380 · 381

Array-plus-hashmap gives O(1) insert, delete, and uniform random pick

The problem LC 380 wants insert, remove and getRandom all in O(1). The structure: a dynamic array holds the elements (so a uniform random pick is one indexed read), and a hash map stores each element’s index in that array (so lookup and delete are O(1)). LC 381 extends it to duplicates with a set of indices per value.

Analogy A raffle drum that is also a phone book: the drum lets you grab a ticket at a random position instantly, and the phone book tells you exactly which position any given ticket sits at — so you can also pull a specific ticket without digging.

The one clever move is the swap-with-last delete. You can’t remove from the middle of an array in O(1) without leaving a hole, so instead you overwrite the victim with the array’s last element, fix that moved element’s index in the hash map, and pop the tail — O(1), no shifting, no gaps. The array stays dense, which is exactly what keeps getRandom a single arr[rand() % n].

Dense array plus index map is the structure behind uniform random sampling from a mutable set: pick-a-live-member problems, load balancers that choose a random healthy backend (and must add and remove backends as they come and go), feature-flag / A-B bucketing that samples from the current cohort, connection pools that grab any idle connection, and matchmaking pools. Whenever membership changes constantly yet you still need an unbiased instant pick, this is the pattern.

For weighted random selection (a backend proportional to capacity, or an ad proportional to bid) the O(1) trick generalises to the alias method — O(1) sampling after O(n) setup — or a Fenwick / segment tree for O(log n) sampling that supports fast weight updates. The plain array + map is the unweighted, fully-dynamic case.

You pay roughly double memory (the value in the array, its index in the map) to buy O(1) on all three operations with no tree or sort. The subtlety is correctness under delete: forget to update the moved element’s index after the swap and remove silently corrupts the map. It gives uniform randomness only; weighted or ordered sampling needs the alias method or a Fenwick tree, and if you also need sorted iteration, this structure can’t provide it.

Check yourself Why swap the element to delete with the last one instead of just removing it in place? Removing from an arbitrary array index is O(n) because everything after it must shift to close the gap, and a gap would also break the ‘pick arr[rand()%n]’ uniformity. Swapping the victim with the last element and popping the tail is O(1) and keeps the array dense — you just also update the hash map with the moved element’s new index so lookups stay correct.

Trees, tries & indexes

The ordered structures that make lookups and range queries fast on disk and in memory: tries, B-trees, Fenwick and segment trees.

11LC 208 · 211

A prefix tree becomes the trie behind autocomplete and IP routing tables

The problem Store a set of strings so membership, insert and — crucially — find every key sharing a prefix all cost time proportional to the key length, not the number of keys. LC 211 adds wildcard (.) matching, which turns a lookup into a small bounded DFS.

Analogy A filing cabinet where you spell a word one letter per drawer; everyone whose name starts ‘Sta-’ sits behind the same three drawers, so once you are there, offering ‘Stan, Stacy, Stark’ costs nothing extra — the path already found them.

A trie keys on the characters of the string itself: each edge is a symbol and the path from the root spells a prefix, so lookup is O(L) in the key length and completely independent of how many keys are stored. That is exactly the shape autocomplete wants — descend to the node for what the user has typed, and every leaf in the subtree below is a completion, which is why the suggestion list appears instantly even over a dictionary of millions of terms. The LC 211 wildcard variant, where . matches any character, becomes a DFS that branches at each dot — the same trick an in-memory term index uses for fuzzy matching.

The same structure, compressed, is the routing table in every IP router. A naive trie on 32 address bits would be 32 levels deep, so routers use a radix / PATRICIA trie that collapses any chain of single-child nodes into one edge labelled with several bits. Longest-prefix match — ‘route 10.1.0.0/16 unless a more specific /24 exists’ — falls out naturally: you walk the address bit by bit and remember the deepest prefix node you passed. Linux’s forwarding base (fib_trie) and most forwarding engines are exactly this.

Dictionaries, spell-checkers and T9 text input lean on the same prefix-sharing: a common prefix is stored once, so ‘international’ and ‘internationalize’ share a single spine. A DAWG (directed acyclic word graph) goes further and merges common suffixes too, shrinking a full Scrabble dictionary to a few hundred KB.

Tries trade memory for speed: a dense per-node child array gives O(1) branch steps but wastes space on sparse alphabets, so real implementations use hashed children, radix compression, or a double-array trie. The payoff is query time that depends only on key length — O(L) lookup and prefix enumeration with no hashing and no rebalancing — plus prefix queries a hash table simply cannot answer. The cost is pointer-chasing and poor cache locality versus a sorted array; at IP line rate that is why hardware uses TCAMs or multibit tries that spend memory to cut levels.

A balanced search tree becomes the B+ tree behind every database index

The problem Keep millions of keys sorted so lookups, inserts and range scans stay O(log n). On disk the constant factor is everything, so the branching factor is pushed from 2 (a BST) to hundreds (a B-tree), collapsing the tree’s depth to the handful of page reads a query can afford.

Analogy A library catalogue where one cabinet drawer points to a shelf of drawers, each pointing to a shelf of books — three openings and you are holding the book, because each drawer fans out to hundreds, not two.

A binary search tree is O(log₂ n) comparisons, but on disk each pointer hop is a near-random read of a 4–16 KB page, so depth is what kills you. A B+ tree fixes depth by matching node size to the page: each node holds hundreds of keys, so fanout is in the hundreds and a billion-row index is only 3–4 levels deep — 3–4 page reads to reach any row. This is the index behind PostgreSQL, MySQL/InnoDB and SQL Server, and the directory structures of filesystems like NTFS and XFS.

In a B+ tree only the leaves hold data (or row pointers) and the leaves are chained in a doubly-linked list, so a range scan walks the leaf list sequentially after a single descent — which is exactly why WHERE ts BETWEEN … and ORDER BY on an indexed column are cheap. InnoDB goes further with a clustered index: the table is the primary-key B+ tree, so the row lives in the leaf and a primary-key lookup needs no second hop, while every secondary index stores the primary key and pays one extra lookup to reach the row.

Inserts place the key in the correct leaf; when a node overflows it splits in two and pushes a separator key up to its parent, and that split can cascade to the root — the only way the tree grows a level, and the reason it stays perfectly balanced without the rotations a BST needs. Deletes can merge underfull nodes, though real databases often just leave them partly empty and reuse the free space.

Fanout is the lever: bigger nodes mean a shallower tree and fewer reads, but larger writes and more write amplification when a page splits. B-trees are read-optimised and update-in-place, which contrasts with LSM-trees (Cassandra, RocksDB) that are write-optimised and append-only. The B-tree’s cost is random-write I/O, fragmentation and page-split churn; its win is predictable low-latency point and range reads, which is why OLTP databases default to it. Concurrency adds latch-coupling or B-link-tree tricks so readers never block on an in-progress split.

A binary indexed tree becomes the prefix-sum engine behind time-series rollups

The problem Maintain an array under point updates while answering sum(0..i) — and therefore any range sum — in O(log n) each. A plain array reads in O(1) but recomputes a prefix in O(n); a prefix-sum array flips that. The Fenwick tree (binary indexed tree) gets both to O(log n).

Analogy A set of partial odometers where each counter secretly covers a power-of-two block of the road; change one mile and you only nudge the log n counters whose blocks contain it, instead of re-adding every mile from the start.

The trick is that index i stores the sum of a block whose length is the lowest set bit of i — so each cell is responsible for a power-of-two range, and i & (-i) walks you between them. A prefix query peels off set bits (i -= i & -i) to stitch together O(log n) disjoint blocks; an update adds the lowest bit (i += i & -i) to touch exactly the blocks that contain the point. Everything lives in one flat array with no pointers — which is why it is tiny, cache-friendly and trivially memory-mapped.

This is the shape of OLAP range aggregates and time-series rollups. A metrics store that must answer ‘total requests between 09:14 and 10:02’ while new points stream in is computing exactly rangeSum = prefix(hi) − prefix(lo−1) under constant point updates. Systems like Apache Druid and column stores precompute partial aggregates per segment for the same reason; a Fenwick tree is the in-memory, continuously-updatable version of that idea. Its 2-D extension powers range counts over spatial grids and the classic ‘how many points in this rectangle’ query.

Because it needs an invertible operation, a Fenwick tree does sums, counts and XOR cleanly; flipping the update/query direction also gives range-update / point-query. The order-statistics variant — a Fenwick tree over value frequencies — answers ‘how many seen so far are ≤ x’ and ‘find the k-th smallest’ in O(log n), which is how streaming percentiles and inversion counts get computed.

Fenwick trees are the lean choice when you only need invertible aggregates (sum, count, XOR): one n-sized array, O(log n) update and query, and far smaller constants and memory than a segment tree — which you must fall back to for non-invertible ops like range-max or for lazy range updates. Build is O(n). The limits: it cannot do range-min/max or arbitrary range assignment, and it assumes a fixed coordinate space, so unbounded or sparse keys need coordinate compression first. For truly huge append-only time series, fixed-granularity rollup tables usually beat keeping one giant live tree.

UPDATING A POINT — ONLY THE log n BLOCKS THAT COVER IT LIGHT UP

Check yourself Both a prefix-sum array and a Fenwick tree answer range-sum in roughly O(1)–O(log n) time. What does the Fenwick tree buy you, and at what cost? A prefix-sum array is O(1) to query but O(n) to update, because changing one element invalidates every prefix after it. A Fenwick tree makes both update and query O(log n) by storing power-of-two partial sums, so it wins whenever the data mutates under a stream of point updates — the cost is turning an O(1) read into an O(log n) one, worth it the moment writes are frequent.

A segment tree with lazy propagation becomes the engine behind booking and range ACLs

The problem Answer aggregate queries (sum, min, max, overlap count) over ranges and apply updates to whole ranges — ‘add 5 to [l, r]’, ‘book this interval’, ‘mark bytes 0–1M readable’ — each in O(log n). LC 715 Range Module and LC 732 (max concurrent bookings) are the canonical shapes.

Analogy A tournament bracket of summaries where each node knows the total for its slice of seats. To change a whole block you tag the single node that owns it with a sticky note — ‘everyone below, +1’ — and only unfold that note when someone actually looks deeper.

A segment tree keeps an aggregate for every node’s sub-range, so a range query merges O(log n) canonical nodes that exactly tile the query. The decisive idea is lazy propagation: a range update stops at those same O(log n) covering nodes, updates each node’s aggregate, and leaves a pending ‘to be pushed down’ tag instead of recursing to every leaf. The tag is applied to children only when a later query or update actually descends through that node — so a range update is O(log n), not O(n).

This is precisely a booking / calendar system: LC 732 asks for the maximum number of overlapping events, which is a range-add of +1 over each booked interval plus a global max-query — a sweep the tree does online as bookings arrive. LC 715’s Range Module (add, remove, query a contiguous range as tracked/untracked) is the same tool behind interval ACLs (‘bytes 0–4095 are readable’), memory and file range locks, and reserved-range allocators. Because coordinates can be large or sparse, real uses pair it with coordinate compression or a dynamically-allocated / implicit segment tree that creates nodes only where data exists.

The structure generalises far past sums: with the right merge it answers range-min/max, GCD, or ‘longest run of free seats’, none of which a Fenwick tree can do. In genomics and interval analytics the same tree underlies coverage and interval-overlap queries over a chromosome; interval trees and augmented balanced BSTs (storing a max-endpoint) are close cousins used when the intervals themselves, not a fixed coordinate grid, are the objects.

Segment trees are the heavyweight sibling of the Fenwick tree: O(n) space (~2–4n nodes), O(log n) range query and range update with lazy propagation, versus the Fenwick tree’s smaller constants but invertible-only, point-update world. The cost is real memory and code complexity, plus care that the lazy tag composes correctly (an ‘assign’ tag must override a pending ‘add’). For enormous or unbounded coordinate spaces you switch to a dynamic/persistent segment tree, trading extra pointers for materialising only touched ranges; for mostly-static data, precomputed sparse tables or rollups can beat it.

Check yourself A range-add-then-range-sum workload is O(n) per update with a plain prefix-sum array. How does lazy propagation make the range update O(log n), and when does the deferred work actually run? A range update touches only the O(log n) nodes whose sub-ranges exactly cover the target; each node’s stored aggregate is updated in place and a lazy tag records an update owed to its descendants, which are never touched then. The tag is pushed down to children only when a future query or update descends through that node — so each operation does O(log n) work and the deferred update is paid lazily, exactly once, on the path that needs it.

15LC 207 · 210

A topological sort becomes the scheduler inside every build system and package manager

The problem Given tasks with ‘must-run-before’ dependencies, produce an order in which every task comes after all it depends on — or prove none exists because there is a cycle. LC 207 asks ‘is it schedulable?’ (cycle detection); LC 210 asks for the actual order.

Analogy Getting dressed: socks before shoes, shirt before jacket. Some items have no order between them, but a valid sequence always puts prerequisites first — and if the rules ever say ‘A before B and B before A’, you simply cannot get dressed.

Model each unit of work as a node and each dependency as a directed edge; a valid build order is a topological sort of that DAG. Kahn’s algorithm repeatedly emits any node with in-degree 0 and decrements its successors — which is literally how a build runs: anything with no unbuilt dependency is ready now, and nodes that become ready together can run in parallel. If the queue empties before every node is emitted, the leftover nodes form a cycle — the dependency error every build tool reports.

This is the core of Make, Bazel, Gradle and npm / Cargo / pip: resolve the dependency DAG, topologically order it, build or install bottom-up. Incremental builds add the real magic — when a file changes, only its node and everything downstream (its transitive dependents) need rebuilding, which is a reverse-reachability walk on the same DAG. CI pipelines (GitHub Actions needs:, Airflow, Tekton) are DAGs executed in topological order, fanning out to the width of the ready set.

The same machinery drives spreadsheet recalculation: cells are nodes, formula references are edges, and on edit Excel or Google Sheets topologically recomputes only the dependents of the changed cell — and refuses a circular reference because it is a cycle in the DAG. Dataflow engines, linker symbol resolution and OS service managers (systemd units) all reduce to the same ‘order a DAG, detect cycles’ problem.

Topological sort is O(V + E) — linear in nodes plus edges — whether done by Kahn’s in-degree queue or DFS post-order (reverse finishing times). The width of the ready set at each step is the available parallelism, which is why tools report a critical path: total time is bounded below by the longest dependency chain no matter how many cores you add. The main failure mode is cycles, so robust tools print the offending cycle rather than just failing; at scale the hard part is not the sort but computing the DAG correctly (content hashing for sound incrementality) and caching results so unchanged subgraphs are skipped entirely.

PEELING OFF IN-DEGREE-ZERO NODES — THE READY SET IS YOUR PARALLEL BATCH

Add a back-edge to create a cycle and watch the algorithm stall with nodes it can never emit — that stuck set is the dependency error.

Check yourself Your CI pipeline must run thousands of jobs with needs: dependencies, maximise parallelism, and fail fast on a bad dependency. Why is topological sort the right core, and how does it give you both the parallelism and the error? Build the dependency DAG and run Kahn’s algorithm: every job with in-degree 0 is runnable immediately, so the set of zero-in-degree jobs at each step is the batch you dispatch in parallel; as each finishes you decrement successors and new jobs become ready. It is O(V+E). If some jobs never reach in-degree 0, they are exactly the ones trapped in a cycle — so the very pass that schedules the work also pinpoints the dependency error.

16LC 743 · 787

Dijkstra's algorithm becomes the shortest-path core of OSPF routing and map directions

The problem Find the minimum-cost path from a source to every node (LC 743, network delay) or to a target under a hop limit (LC 787, cheapest flight within k stops). Dijkstra handles non-negative weights greedily; Bellman-Ford handles negative weights and the bounded-hop constraint.

Analogy Water released from one city flooding a road network — it always reaches the nearest unflooded town next, and the moment it arrives by the cheapest road, that town’s shortest distance is settled forever. ‘At most k stops’ just asks how far the water gets in k rounds.

Dijkstra’s algorithm keeps a priority queue of tentative distances and repeatedly settles the closest unsettled node, relaxing its outgoing edges. With a binary heap it is O((V + E) log V); the greedy ‘closest node is final’ step is valid only when edge weights are non-negative, which is why it cannot handle credits modelled as negative cost. This is the shortest-path-first (SPF) computation at the heart of OSPF and IS-IS: every router floods link-state advertisements, builds the identical topology graph, and runs Dijkstra from itself to populate its forwarding table.

Maps and navigation (Google / Apple Maps, OSRM) are Dijkstra over a road graph with travel-time weights, but at continental scale plain Dijkstra explores far too much, so production systems add A* (a distance-to-goal heuristic to steer the search) and heavy preprocessing like contraction hierarchies that answer queries in microseconds. Weights encode live traffic, turn penalties and road class, which is why the ‘shortest’ route is rarely the fewest miles.

LC 787’s ‘cheapest flight within k stops’ breaks Dijkstra’s greedy invariant, because a longer-hop path can be cheaper yet exceed the stop limit. The fix is Bellman-Ford / BFS-by-levels: relax all edges exactly k+1 times, so after round i you hold the cheapest cost reachable in ≤ i edges. Bellman-Ford is O(V·E) but tolerates negative weights and detects negative cycles — exactly what distance-vector routing (RIP) needs, and why count-to-infinity is its failure mode.

The weight sign picks the algorithm: non-negative → Dijkstra (O((V+E) log V) with a binary heap); any negative edge, or a bounded-hop constraint → Bellman-Ford (O(V·E), and the only one that detects negative cycles). The k-stops twist is a reminder that adding a resource constraint (hops, fuel, budget) turns shortest-path into a layered search whose state is (node, resource-left). At internet scale the practical limits are convergence time (how fast every router re-runs SPF after a link flaps) and churn, which is why OSPF uses areas and incremental SPF rather than recomputing the whole graph.

SETTLING THE NEAREST UNSETTLED NODE — THE FRONTIER GROWS OUTWARD BY DISTANCE

Watch the priority queue always pop the closest node; add a stop limit and see why the greedy ‘settled forever’ rule stops being safe.

Check yourself Why does LC 787 (‘cheapest flight, at most k stops’) break Dijkstra, and what do you use instead? Dijkstra settles a node the first time it is popped as cheapest and never revisits it — but under a stop limit the globally-cheapest way to reach a node may use too many hops, while a pricier few-hop path is the one you actually need, so ‘settled’ is no longer correct. Use Bellman-Ford relaxed exactly k+1 times (BFS by levels): after round i each node holds the cheapest cost using at most i edges, so you read the answer off round k+1 — O(k·E) and hop-count-correct.

Union-Find becomes the identity-resolution and connectivity engine behind dedup and MST

The problem Maintain a partition of elements into disjoint sets under two operations — union(a, b) and find(x) (which set is x in?) — so you can answer ‘are these connected / the same entity?’ in near-O(1). LC 684 finds the edge that creates a cycle; LC 721 merges accounts that share any email.

Analogy Figuring out friend-groups at a party: when two people turn out to know each other you merge their circles, and each circle keeps one ‘representative’. To ask whether two guests are in the same group, you just check whether they point to the same representative.

A disjoint-set union (DSU) represents each set as a tree where every node points toward a representative root; find walks to the root and union links one root under another. Two optimisations make it almost free: union by rank/size keeps trees shallow, and path compression flattens the walked path on every find. Together they give an amortised cost of O(α(n)) — the inverse Ackermann function, effectively a small constant for any real n.

This is the workhorse of identity resolution and dedup: LC 721 Accounts Merge is entity resolution exactly — each shared email is an edge, and the connected components are the real people. Customer-data platforms, fraud graphs and record-linkage pipelines union records that share a phone, device or email, then read off components as unified identities. Cycle detection in an undirected graph is the same tool: LC 684 adds edges one by one, and the first edge whose endpoints are already in the same set is the redundant one that closes a cycle.

Union-Find is also the engine inside Kruskal’s MST: sort edges by weight and add each one unless it would join two nodes already connected (which DSU tests instantly), building a spanning tree with no cycles. More broadly it answers dynamic connectivity — ‘are these two network nodes in the same partition?’, image connected-components via union of adjacent pixels, percolation, and Kruskal-based clustering. The one thing it cannot do is un-union, which is why it suits incremental ‘things only merge’ workloads.

With union by rank and path compression, m operations cost O(m · α(n)) — essentially linear, over a flat array with superb cache behaviour, far cheaper than running a BFS/DFS per connectivity query. The hard limit is that it is incremental only: deletions or edge removals need a different structure (link-cut trees, or offline processing in reverse). It also yields only the partition, not paths or distances. At scale the real work is often generating correct edges (which emails/devices truly imply ‘same entity’) and handling it as a distributed or offline union when the graph does not fit in memory.

ADDING EDGES — SETS MERGE AND PATHS FLATTEN ONTO THEIR ROOT

Add an edge whose endpoints already share a root and watch it get rejected as a cycle — exactly LC 684’s redundant connection.

Check yourself You are merging millions of customer records that share emails, phones or device IDs into unified identities. Why is Union-Find a better fit than running a graph traversal per query? Each shared identifier is an edge; the unified identities are the connected components. A fresh BFS/DFS per ‘are these the same person?’ query is O(V+E) every time and recomputes everything as edges arrive. Union-Find instead maintains the partition incrementally — each new shared identifier is one union, each lookup one find, both amortised near-constant with path compression — so the whole batch is effectively linear and queries are instant. The trade-off is that you cannot cheaply split an identity apart later.

A minimum spanning tree becomes the backbone layout for networks and clustering

The problem Connect all nodes with the cheapest possible total edge weight and no cycles — a minimum spanning tree. LC 1584 (Min Cost to Connect Points) is MST on a complete graph of Manhattan distances. Prim’s grows one tree outward; Kruskal’s adds globally-cheapest safe edges.

Analogy Laying the least cable to wire up a town so every house is reachable. You never build a loop (that is wasted cable), and you always add the cheapest wire that connects a still-unwired house — when every house is on the grid, you have spent the minimum possible.

Both classic algorithms exploit the cut property: the cheapest edge crossing any partition of the nodes is safe to include in some MST. Prim’s grows a single tree, repeatedly pulling the cheapest edge leaving it via a priority queue — O(E log V), a natural fit for dense graphs. Kruskal’s sorts all edges and adds each unless it would form a cycle, using Union-Find to test that in near-constant time — O(E log E), ideal when edges are sparse or already sorted. LC 1584’s points become a complete graph, so Prim’s with distances computed on the fly is the memory-friendly choice.

The direct application is physical network and infrastructure design: least-cost cabling, pipeline and power-grid layout, connecting data centres, or wiring a chip with minimum total wire. Any ‘connect everything as cheaply as possible, no redundant links’ problem is an MST — though real networks then add redundancy on top, because a tree has no alternate path and a single cut disconnects it.

MST also underlies clustering: build the MST, delete the k−1 heaviest edges, and the remaining components are k clusters — this is single-linkage hierarchical clustering, good at finding non-spherical groups and outliers. And it gives a fast approximate TSP: a DFS pre-order walk of the MST is a tour at most 2× optimal (Christofides tightens this to 1.5×), which is why MST is a standard starting point for vehicle-routing heuristics.

MST is one of the rare greedy algorithms that is provably optimal, thanks to the cut property; both Prim’s and Kruskal’s land around O(E log V), with the choice driven by density (Prim’s + heap for dense, Kruskal’s + Union-Find for sparse or pre-sorted). The structural caveat dominates real use: an MST minimises total cost but gives a single path between any two nodes — zero fault tolerance, possibly long routes — so backbone designs blend MST with k-connectivity or Steiner-tree extensions (which may add new junction nodes to beat the MST). For clustering, single-linkage’s weakness is ‘chaining’ through bridge points.

GROWING THE TREE BY CHEAPEST SAFE EDGE — CYCLE-FORMING EDGES ARE SKIPPED

Delete the k−1 heaviest tree edges and the graph falls into k clusters — that is single-linkage clustering in one move.

Check yourself An MST gives the cheapest way to connect every site, yet no production backbone is built as a pure MST. Why, and what do engineers do instead? A spanning tree has exactly one path between any two nodes and V−1 edges, so it has no redundancy: any single link or node failure partitions the network, and the one path may be long. An MST minimises cable, not resilience or latency. So designers start from the MST for a cheap connected baseline, then deliberately add edges for k-edge-connectivity (survive any single failure) and shorter alternate paths, trading extra cost for fault tolerance — or use Steiner trees to add junctions that lower total cost further.

Flood fill becomes the connected-component labeller behind image segmentation

The problem Count (and label) the maximal connected regions of ‘filled’ cells in a grid — LC 200 counts islands of land in a sea grid. Each unvisited filled cell starts a BFS/DFS flood fill that claims its entire region before you move on.

Analogy Spilling paint on one tile of a tiled floor and letting it seep to every touching tile of the same colour — one spill colours one whole region. Count how many separate spills it takes to cover all the coloured tiles, and you have counted the regions.

Scan the grid; each time you hit a filled, unvisited cell, increment the region count and flood — BFS or DFS — marking every 4- or 8-connected filled neighbour as part of this region. Every cell is visited once, so the whole thing is O(rows × cols). Recursive DFS risks stack overflow on a huge single region (hence an explicit stack or a BFS queue in production), and the 4- vs 8-connectivity choice decides whether diagonally-touching cells count as one region — a real modelling decision, not a detail.

This is literally connected-component labelling (CCL) in computer vision: after thresholding an image into foreground/background, flood fill assigns each blob a distinct label so you can count cells, detect objects, or measure their area — OpenCV’s connectedComponents and cv2.floodFill are this exact algorithm, as is the paint-bucket tool in any image editor. The same flood is region growing in medical imaging (segment an organ from a seed voxel), watershed and terrain analysis in GIS, maze and reachability solving in games, and ‘select contiguous area’ everywhere. Generalise the grid to any graph and ‘number of islands’ is just counting connected components — the same answer Union-Find gives when the regions arrive incrementally rather than as a static grid.

Flood-fill CCL is O(N) in the number of cells with O(N) worst-case memory for the queue/stack or visited map — cheap, but the constants and failure modes matter: recursive DFS blows the call stack on a megapixel region, so use BFS or an explicit stack; and a static grid flood must re-scan from scratch if cells change. When regions merge incrementally (streaming pixels, or edges added over time) Union-Find (Hoshen–Kopelman) is the better engine because it merges labels online. Check yourself ‘Number of Islands’ can be solved with BFS/DFS flood fill or with Union-Find. When would you reach for Union-Find instead, and what does flood fill still win at? Union-Find maintains components online; flood fill would have to re-run from scratch after every change.

Tarjan's bridge-finding becomes the single-point-of-failure detector for networks

The problem Find the edges (and nodes) whose removal disconnects a graph — bridges and articulation points. LC 1192 asks for all critical connections in a server network: the links with no redundant alternate path. Tarjan’s single DFS finds them all in O(V + E).

Analogy Checking which roads, if washed out, would cut a town off entirely. A road on a loop is safe — traffic reroutes around the loop — but a lone bridge to an island has no alternate, so losing it strands the island. You want to flag exactly those lone bridges.

Tarjan’s algorithm runs a single DFS, stamping each node with a discovery time disc[v] and a low-link low[v] — the earliest-discovered node reachable from v’s subtree using tree edges plus at most one back-edge. An edge (u, v) is a bridge exactly when low[v] > disc[u]: the subtree below v has no back-edge climbing above u, so that edge is its only link to the rest of the graph. A back-edge (a cycle) pulls low-links up and immunises every edge on the cycle.

This is precisely network resilience / SPOF analysis: LC 1192’s ‘critical connections’ are the links in a data-centre or backbone whose failure partitions it — the ones with no redundant path — so finding bridges tells you exactly where to add a redundant link or which fibre has no diversity. Articulation points (the node version) flag critical routers, switches or hosts whose loss splits the network; the same analysis hardens power grids, pipelines and supply chains against single failures.

The deeper structure is 2-edge- and 2-vertex-connectivity: collapsing each biconnected component (a maximal chunk with no articulation point) yields a tree whose edges are exactly the bridges — a clean map of where the network is ‘loopy and safe’ versus ‘hanging by a thread’. The same low-link bookkeeping, in Tarjan’s SCC variant, finds strongly connected components in directed graphs, used in deadlock detection and dependency-cycle analysis.

Tarjan’s bridge, articulation and SCC algorithms are all a single O(V + E) DFS over discovery-time and low-link arrays — strikingly cheap for what they reveal, and far better than the brute force of removing each edge and re-testing connectivity (O(E · (V+E))). Caveats: it is a static-snapshot analysis, so a changing topology must be re-run (or handled with incremental 2-edge-connectivity structures); and ‘no bridges’ means it survives one failure, not two, so true resilience planning considers k-edge-connectivity and correlated failures (a shared conduit cutting several ‘independent’ links at once). The output is a prioritised list of exactly where redundancy is missing.

Check yourself In Tarjan’s algorithm, why is the test low[v] > disc[u] exactly the condition for edge (u, v) to be a bridge? low[v] is the earliest node the subtree rooted at v can reach using tree edges and at most one back-edge. Interval merging is what collapses a calendar, a firewall table, and a genome track

The problem Given a set of [start, end] ranges, some overlapping, produce the minimal set of non-overlapping ranges covering the same points. LC 56 merges a batch; LC 57 inserts one new interval into an already-sorted set.

Analogy A highlighter run over a page: wherever two strokes touch or overlap you see one continuous bar, not two. Sort the strokes left to right and you only ever compare each new one against the single band you’re currently extending.

Otherwise emit the current band and open a new one. The whole thing is O(n log n), dominated by the sort; the sweep itself is linear. This is exactly how a calendar computes free/busy: Google Calendar and Outlook merge everyone’s busy blocks into consolidated bands so the gaps between them are the schedulable slots.

The same merge compacts IP ranges. In genomics, bedtools merge collapses overlapping BED features before counting coverage, and the same ranges feed an interval tree when the question becomes “which features overlap this query?” rather than “merge everything.” Resource allocation (reserved time or bandwidth windows) is the same shape: merge to find what’s already committed, then the complement is what’s free.

For repeated overlap queries an augmented interval tree (O(log n + k)) beats re-merging from scratch. This is literally capacity provisioning. A monotonic deque gives the rolling max of a metric stream in amortized O(1)

The problem Report the maximum of every contiguous window of size k as it slides across a stream. The naive rescan is O(nk); a heap is O(n log k); a monotonic deque is O(n).

Analogy A line of people ordered by height where anyone shorter than a newcomer steps out (they can never be tallest while that newcomer is present). The front of the line is always the tallest person still inside the window.

Keep a deque of indices whose values are strictly decreasing. For each new element, pop from the back every index with a smaller value — a later, larger value dominates them, so they can never again be the window max — then push the new index. Pop from the front any index that has slid out of the window [i−k+1, i]. The front index is the current window’s maximum. Each index is pushed once and popped once, so the whole pass is O(n) time, O(k) space.

This is the engine behind rolling metrics over streams. Prometheus’ max_over_time/min_over_time, Grafana rolling-peak panels, and alerting on peak request rate (rate monitoring, crude DDoS detection) all want the extreme over a moving window cheaply. Stream processors (Flink, Kafka Streams) compute windowed max/min aggregations, and ML feature pipelines emit rolling-max/min features the same way.

The same monotonic structure powers “next greater element,” stock-span, and “largest rectangle in a histogram.” Its sliding-window-minimum form is how image processing makes grayscale erosion/dilation (morphological filters) run in O(1) per pixel independent of kernel size — the van Herk / Gil–Werman algorithm is exactly this deque applied along each row and column.

O(n) time, O(k) memory — strictly better than the O(n log k) heap for a pure max because it discards dominated elements instead of ordering them. A heap only earns its log factor when you need top-K (not just top-1) or deletions by arbitrary value. The streaming caveat: this assumes a count-based window with in-order arrival; a true max over a time window with out-of-order events needs eviction by event-time and watermarks, not simple front-pops. For distributed streams each partition keeps its own deque and a reducer combines the partial maxima — valid only because max is associative and commutative.

A sliding window becomes the rate limiter in front of every API

The problem Count events per key within a moving time window and reject the ones over the limit. The naive counter resets on a fixed boundary; the sliding window smooths the burst at the edge.

Analogy A nightclub with a strict last-60-minutes headcount: the doorman doesn’t clear the count at the top of each hour (that would let a crowd in at 12:59 and again at 1:00) — they keep a rolling tally of who entered in the trailing hour.

Fixed-window counting (LC 362’s hit counter) is cheap — one counter per key per window — but it allows a 2× burst across the boundary: fill the window at the end, then again at the start of the next. The axes are accuracy vs memory vs burst behaviour. Fixed window: O(1) but bursty at edges. Sliding log: exact but unbounded memory. Sliding counter / token bucket: O(1) and smooth, the usual pick. The expand-contract window carves sessions, QoS spans, and the tightest matching log burst

The problem Find the smallest window of a stream/string satisfying a constraint — containing every required character (LC 76) or having at most k distinct (LC 340). A fixed window can’t; the window must grow and shrink to fit.

Analogy A camera that widens its frame until everyone you need is inside, then tightens from the left edge as far as it can while still keeping the whole group in shot — the tightest crop that holds them all.

The two-pointer pattern: a right pointer expands the window, admitting elements until the constraint is met; then a left pointer contracts, dropping elements while the constraint still holds, recording the minimum each time. A counts hashmap plus a single “requirements still unmet” integer makes each satisfy-check O(1); each pointer advances at most n times → O(n) overall. It is the variable-size cousin of the fixed-window rate limiter.

In stream processing this is session windowization: Flink and Kafka Streams “session windows” grow while events keep arriving and close after an inactivity gap — a contract-on-gap window. Log analytics use it to find the smallest time span containing a required set of correlated events (a login, a transaction, an error) during incident forensics, and QoS/SLA monitors use it to pinpoint the shortest span that violates a burst threshold.

The at-most-k-distinct variant underlies “longest substring with k distinct,” used in tokenizer and dedup heuristics, cache working-set estimation (distinct keys seen in a window), and adaptive-compression dictionary sizing — any question of “how long a span stays within a diversity budget.”

O(n) time, O(Σ) or O(k) memory for the counts map — the win over brute-force O(n²) is that the left pointer never moves backward (monotone progress). The correctness precondition is that the predicate must be monotone under growing/shrinking: “contains all of T” and “at most k distinct” both qualify; “exactly k distinct” does not, so you compute it as atMost(k) − atMost(k−1). Over true event-time stream windows, out-of-order arrivals and watermarks complicate the contract step — you evict by event-time, not arrival order, and must hold state until a watermark says it’s safe to close.

KMP’s failure function is why grep and an IDS never re-scan a byte

The problem Find a pattern inside a text. The naive scan backs up the text pointer on every mismatch — O(nm). KMP precomputes where to resume in the pattern so the text pointer never moves backward — O(n + m).

Analogy Reciting a long passphrase you’ve half-typed: when you slip, you don’t restart from the first key — you jump to the longest beginning you’ve already typed correctly and carry on from there.

The failure (prefix) function of the pattern answers, for each position: after matching k characters and then failing, what is the longest proper prefix of the pattern that is also a suffix of what we just matched? That prefix already matches the text’s last k bytes, so we resume the pattern there and leave the text pointer put. Building it is O(m) (the pattern matched against itself); the scan is O(n). The whole trick is that a partial match already encodes what the recent text looks like, so those bytes are never re-examined.

This is the heart of single-pattern search. GNU grep’s fixed-string path, log scanners, and deep-packet-inspection / IDS signature engines all must match a known byte string against a line-rate stream without buffering or backtracking. The decisive property is no text backup: an IDS can’t rewind a packet stream off a socket, and KMP’s automaton form matches one byte at a time with no look-back, so it’s safe on an unseekable stream.

In practice Boyer-Moore and its bad-character / good-suffix heuristics are the speed champions for long patterns — grep leans on Boyer-Moore-style skipping to run sublinear in the common case by jumping ahead. KMP’s role is the guaranteed linear worst case and the clean streaming automaton, and it’s the natural stepping stone to Aho-Corasick when one pattern becomes thousands.

KMP is O(n + m) time, O(m) space, with the defining guarantee of zero text backup — essential for streaming/DPI where rewinding is impossible. Its constant factor is often beaten by Boyer-Moore (sublinear average via skipping) on large alphabets and long patterns, which is why grep prefers it; KMP wins the worst-case bound and one-pass streaming. Crucially, for many patterns you don’t run KMP P times — you build an Aho-Corasick automaton (next card), which is KMP generalised to a trie. Adversarial inputs with pathological repeats can wreck naive matching but never break KMP’s linear bound.

Aho-Corasick is KMP for thousands of patterns at once — the engine inside Snort and every WAF

The problem Match a stream against a whole dictionary of patterns simultaneously, reporting every hit, in time independent of the number of patterns. Running KMP once per pattern is O(n·P); Aho-Corasick is O(n + Σ|pattern| + matches).

Analogy A proofreader who has memorised a banned-words list as one branching map: each letter you read walks the map, and dotted shortcut lines let a dead end instantly jump to the longest still-live word without re-reading the sentence.

This is the multi-pattern matcher. Snort and Suricata compile their rule sets’ content strings into an Aho-Corasick automaton (Suricata’s MPM — multi-pattern matcher — ships Aho-Corasick variants) so each packet is scanned once against thousands of signatures. The automaton is built once and reused across every byte of input — that amortisation is what makes line-rate scanning against a huge signature set feasible. Adding or removing a signature means rebuilding (or incrementally patching) the automaton, so rule updates are a deploy-time cost, not a per-packet one.

Time is O(n + Σ|pattern| + matches); memory is the trie — O(Σ|pattern|) nodes, each with alphabet-sized transitions unless compressed (a double-array trie, or goto/fail tables, trades space for cache locality). The matches term is an adversarial lever: input crafted to trigger overlapping hits (e.g. aaaa… against patterns a, aa, aaa) can explode output, a real DoS vector that filters must bound.

Levenshtein DP is the arithmetic behind diff, spell-check, and DNA alignment

The problem Find the minimum number of single-character insertions, deletions, and substitutions that turn one string into another. Brute force tries every edit sequence (exponential); DP fills an (m+1)×(n+1) table in O(mn).

Analogy Two recipes side by side: to make the first into the second you either add a step, cross one out, or rewrite one. The fewest pen-strokes is a grid where each cell costs one move more than the cheapest of its neighbours.

The recurrence: dp[i][j] is the edit distance between the first i chars of A and first j of B. If the characters match, carry the diagonal unchanged; otherwise it’s 1 + min of three neighbours — up (delete from A), left (insert into B), diagonal (substitute). The bottom-right cell is the answer, and backtracing the chosen moves reconstructs the actual alignment — the edit script.

This is fuzzy matching everywhere. Spell-checkers and autocorrect rank candidates by edit distance (often weighted — a keyboard-adjacent substitution costs less); “did you mean” and fuzzy search are the same, and Lucene/Elasticsearch fuzzy queries run a bounded Levenshtein automaton (distance 1–2) to prune the dictionary fast. Bioinformatics alignment is the identical recurrence with biology-specific costs: Needleman-Wunsch is global edit distance with a scoring matrix, Smith-Waterman the local variant, both typically with affine gap penalties.

The diff that drives git diff and code review is the dual: longest common subsequence / shortest edit script, where only insertions and deletions (no substitutions) model added and removed lines. Myers’ O(ND) diff is this same DP family tuned so the common case — few differences, small D — runs near-linear.

Classic DP is O(mn) time and O(mn) space, but the distance alone needs only the previous row → O(min(m,n)) space with two rolling rows — at the cost of the backtrace, which Hirschberg’s divide-and-conquer recovers in O(mn) time and linear space. For bounded search (“distance ≤ k”) you fill only a diagonal band → O(kn), and Lucene’s Levenshtein automaton avoids the table entirely. The quadratic wall is fundamental: there is a conditional lower bound (no strongly sub-quadratic algorithm unless SETH fails), so whole-genome alignment uses heuristics — BLAST seed-and-extend, minimizers — rather than exact DP.

That insertion-shift resistance is the whole advantage over fixed-size blocks; LBFS pioneered it.

For similarity, plagiarism detectors and the MOSS code-similarity service use winnowing over rolling hashes (compute k-gram fingerprints, keep the minimum per window) to compare documents robustly against reordering, and the same k-shingling + MinHash pipeline estimates Jaccard similarity for near-duplicate web-page detection.

An LRU cache is how a CPU, Redis, and your DB buffer pool decide what to forget first

The problem Serve get and put in O(1) while always evicting the least-recently-used key once capacity is exceeded. A plain hash map gives O(1) lookup but no recency order; a list gives order but O(n) reordering.

Analogy A desk with room for only N papers: each time you touch one you move it to the top of the pile, and when a new paper arrives and the desk is full, the one at the bottom — untouched longest — goes in the recycling.

The trick is welding two structures together. A hash map maps each key to a node in a doubly-linked list; the list keeps recency order, most-recent at the head. On a get, the map finds the node in O(1) and you splice it to the front; on a put that overflows, you unlink the tail (the least-recently-used node) and delete its key from the map. Every operation touches a constant number of pointers. This is the shape of a page cache, a CPU cache line set, Memcached's slabs, and a database buffer pool.

Real systems rarely implement exact LRU, because the move-to-front write on every read is a contention and bookkeeping cost. Redis with maxmemory-policy allkeys-lru approximates it by sampling a few random keys (maxmemory-samples) and evicting the oldest of that sample. The Linux page cache uses two lists (active / inactive) as a second-chance variant, and hardware caches use pseudo-LRU (tree-PLRU) because tracking true recency bits per line is too expensive in silicon. PostgreSQL's buffer manager uses a CLOCK sweep — a ring of reference bits — as a cheap LRU approximation.

Time is O(1) for both operations; space is O(n) plus two pointers per node, and that pointer overhead can rival the stored value for tiny entries. The hard part is concurrency: a single global lock serializes every access, so production caches stripe into segments or use concurrent maps, and because exact LRU writes on every read, that hotspot is why Caffeine and Redis prefer approximate recency. LRU is also scan-vulnerable — one large sequential scan evicts the entire hot set — which is the reason LFU, LRU-K, 2Q, and ARC exist.

The problem Evict the least-frequently-used key on overflow, breaking ties by recency, all in O(1). Counting accesses is easy; finding and removing the current minimum-frequency key in constant time is the hard part.

The classic O(1) LFU keeps three things: a map from key to (value, freq), a map from each frequency to a doubly-linked list of keys at that frequency, and a minFreq pointer. On access you bump a key's frequency by moving its node from list[f] to list[f+1]; evicting means dropping the head of list[minFreq], and if that list empties you advance minFreq. Every step is constant-time pointer surgery, mirroring the LRU structure but ordered by count instead of recency.

Pure LFU has two notorious flaws: a key hammered a million times last week keeps a huge count and never leaves (no aging), and a fresh key enters at frequency 1 and gets evicted before it can prove itself. The production answer is Window-TinyLFU, used in Caffeine (and Go's Ristretto): a small LRU admission window feeds a frequency filter — a Count-Min Sketch that is periodically halved to age out stale popularity — which decides whether a new candidate is accessed more often than the victim it would replace. All operations are O(1), but with heavier constants and more memory than LRU: two maps plus per-frequency lists. The deeper lesson is that the admission policy matters more than the eviction policy — TinyLFU's filter refuses to let a one-hit sequential scan pollute the cache, which is precisely LRU's weakness.

A Lamport timestamp is a single counter each node increments on every event and bumps to max(local, received)+1 on receiving a message. This is strictly safer than last-write-wins on a timestamp, which silently drops a write whenever clocks are skewed. The problem Let many replicas accept writes independently — offline, multi-master, across a partition — and still converge to the same state when they sync, without locks, a leader, or manual conflict resolution. Naive last-write-wins loses updates; a central lock kills availability and offline use.

Analogy Several people keep separate tally sheets and later combine them by a rule that ignores order and duplicates — like pouring everyone's water into one jug: whatever order you pour, and however many times you re-pour the same cup, the final level is identical.

A Conflict-free Replicated Data Type is designed so its merge is commutative, associative, and idempotent — a least-upper-bound join on a lattice — which means replicas can apply updates in any order, any number of times, and still converge (Strong Eventual Consistency). A G-counter (grow-only) keeps a per-node count and merges by element-wise max, summing for the total; a PN-counter pairs two G-counters (increments and decrements) to allow subtraction; an OR-set (observed-remove set) tags each add with a unique token so a concurrent add and remove resolve deterministically — the add survives unless its exact token was the one removed — fixing the ambiguity of a naive add/remove set.

This is the engine of real-time collaboration and offline-first sync. Figma's multiplayer uses CRDT-like per-property last-writer-wins registers so two designers editing different attributes never clobber each other; Google Docs and sequence CRDTs (or OT) handle concurrent text insertion by giving characters stable fractional positions; Riak ships CRDT counters, sets, and maps as first-class values; and Redis Enterprise active-active geo-replication (CRDBs), Automerge, and Yjs are general CRDT libraries. The common thread: each site writes locally for instant, offline-capable UX, and background sync merges deterministically with no coordinator.

CRDTs buy availability and partition-tolerance (the AP corner of CAP) and guaranteed convergence with zero coordination — but at a metadata cost: tombstones and unique tags for removes accumulate (an OR-set must remember what it deleted), so garbage collection and causal-stability tracking become the hard engineering. They converge state only; they do not enforce global invariants (you cannot cheaply assert “balance stays ≥ 0” across replicas), and some intents — move an item, ordered text — need carefully designed types to avoid surprising merges (duplication, interleaving). Operation-based CRDTs cut payload size but demand reliable, exactly-once causal delivery; state-based ones are simpler but ship more.

Check yourself What three algebraic properties make a CRDT merge safe, and why does each matter for offline, multi-master sync? Commutativity (order-independent), associativity (grouping-independent), and idempotence (duplicate-safe). Reconnecting replicas deliver each other's updates in different orders, in different batches, and often more than once (retries, gossip). Commutativity and associativity mean any order or grouping of merges reaches the same result; idempotence means re-delivering the same update changes nothing. Together they form a join on a lattice, guaranteeing every replica converges to the identical state once all have seen the same set of updates — no locks, no leader, no lost writes.

Patience sorting turns ‘longest increasing subsequence’ into a better code diff

The problem Find the longest increasing subsequence in O(n log n) by placing each value on the leftmost pile whose top is ≥ it (binary search), starting a new pile otherwise — the number of piles is the LIS length.

Analogy Dealing cards into solitaire piles: each card goes on the leftmost pile with a larger top card, or starts a new pile. The number of piles you end up with is the longest increasing run hiding in the deck.

The textbook LIS is an O(n²) DP; the fast version is patience sorting — keep an array of pile tops, binary-search the leftmost top that is ≥ the new value, and replace it (or append a new pile). The pile count is the LIS length. That ‘binary search over an array of bests’ is a pattern in its own right, reused anywhere you track the best-so-far across ordered thresholds.

The system payoff is diffing. Git and Bazaar ship patience diff: it runs patience sorting over the lines that appear exactly once in both files to find the longest common subsequence of anchor lines, then recurses between anchors. The result is far more readable than naive LCS on code, which happily matches unrelated braces and blank lines. The same LIS/LCS core drives three-way merge and biological sequence alignment.

Patience sorting is O(n log n) time, O(n) space — the binary-search-on-pile-tops is the same move as finding an insertion point in a sorted run. Patience diff pays a little extra preprocessing (isolating unique anchor lines) for dramatically clearer output, the right trade for human code review. Plain LCS is O(nm) and can align the wrong lines; that is why ‘correct but unreadable’ diffs pushed the industry toward patience and histogram diff.

Check yourself Why does the pile count equal the LIS length, and why replace a pile’s top instead of only ever appending? Within a pile the tops decrease and across piles they increase, and any increasing subsequence can touch each pile at most once — so the number of piles equals the longest increasing subsequence. Replacing a top keeps it as small as possible, leaving the most room for later values; that greedy choice preserves the optimum.

Word Break is the segmentation DP behind tokenizers and CJK word splitting

The problem Given a string and a dictionary, decide whether (or how) it splits into known words. A DP over prefixes: position i is reachable if some reachable j plus a dictionary word j..i lands on it.

Analogy Reading a sign with the spaces rubbed out — GOODMANANDDOG. You scan left to right, and at each point ask: does a word I know end here, and could I legally have reached its start?

dp[i] = can the first i characters be segmented; for each i you check dictionary words ending there, which a trie turns into a single walk. This is exactly how a compiler’s lexer does maximal-munch tokenization, and how languages written without spaces — Chinese, Japanese, Thai — are word-segmented. The ‘list every segmentation’ variant (LC 140) is the same DP carrying backpointers.

In NLP the modern descendants are subword tokenizers — BPE, WordPiece, Unigram/SentencePiece. Unigram runs a Viterbi DP over a learned vocabulary to pick the most probable segmentation: the same left-to-right prefix DP as Word Break, but edges are weighted by probability and you take the max-likelihood path rather than mere feasibility. Every LLM prompt you send is segmented by a cousin of LC 139.

With a trie or hash set the feasibility DP is O(n²) (or O(n·L)); memoized recursion avoids recomputing suffixes. Feasibility is cheap; the weighted Viterbi version is what production tokenizers run, and ambiguity — many valid splits — is the real difficulty, resolved by probabilities or longest-match. The ‘enumerate all splits’ variant is exponential on pathological inputs (aaaa…) unless you keep it as a counting/DAG problem rather than materialising every string.

Check yourself Why is Word Break a DP problem rather than a simple greedy longest-match? Greedy longest-match can strand the rest of the string — take a long word early and the remainder no longer splits, even though a shorter first word would have worked. You must remember every reachable boundary (dp[i]) so later positions can build on any of them; that overlapping reuse is exactly dynamic programming.

Knapsack is the allocator behind ad budgets, capacity planning, and bin-packing

The problem Pick a subset of items that maximises value under a capacity budget (0/1 knapsack), via a DP over item and remaining capacity. Partition (LC 416) is the special case where an item’s value equals its weight.

Analogy Packing a carry-on against a hard weight limit: every item is a bet of value against grams, and the best packing is never ‘most valuable first’ — it is the combination that fills the bag most profitably.

The DP fills a table over capacities: dp[c] = best value reachable with budget c, updated once per item. That skeleton is constrained resource allocation everywhere: fitting jobs into a machine’s RAM/CPU, choosing which ads to serve under a budget and pacing cap, admitting entries to a cache under a size limit (cost-aware caching), or packing VMs onto hosts. Whenever you hear ‘maximise value subject to a capacity,’ a knapsack is lurking.

The exact DP is pseudo-polynomial — O(n·W), fine when W is a small integer, hopeless when the budget is huge or goods are divisible. So real systems branch: fractional-knapsack greedy by value density (provably optimal for divisible goods, and the basis of many schedulers), LP relaxation, or FPTAS approximations when near-optimal with a guarantee is enough.

0/1 knapsack is NP-hard in general; the DP is only pseudo-polynomial, so it helps only for modest integer budgets. The dual, bin-packing, is where cluster schedulers like Kubernetes and Borg live — and they emphatically do not run exact DP. Inputs are enormous and online (pods arrive continuously), so they use fast heuristics (first-fit / best-fit-decreasing, scoring functions) that are near-optimal in practice and decide in milliseconds; exactness is traded for speed and incrementality.

Check yourself Why can a cluster scheduler not just run the knapsack/bin-packing DP to place pods optimally? Exact bin-packing is NP-hard and the DP is pseudo-polynomial in capacity — infeasible at cluster scale and online, where pods arrive continuously and placements can’t be recomputed globally each time. So schedulers use fast heuristics (first-fit/best-fit-decreasing, weighted scoring) that run in milliseconds and are near-optimal in practice.

Kadane’s scan is the one-pass engine behind anomaly spans and max-profit windows

The problem Find the maximum-sum contiguous subarray in O(n) by carrying ‘best sum ending here’ and resetting when it turns negative (LC 53). The product variant (LC 152) tracks min and max together, because a negative flips them.

Analogy Walking a trail tracking your best net elevation gain over any stretch: you keep a running total, and the moment it dips below where that stretch began, you abandon it and start counting fresh from here.

cur = max(x, cur + x); best = max(best, cur). The reset — start fresh when the running sum goes negative — is the whole idea, and it generalises: swap ‘sum’ for a score and you have a one-pass detector for the highest-scoring contiguous span in a stream. That is anomaly detection (the strongest run of deviation), maximal-scoring segments in bioinformatics, and the classic max single buy/sell window — LC 121 is literally Kadane over price differences.

Because it is O(n), O(1), and single-pass, Kadane is a natural streaming primitive: it maintains the running best over an unbounded series with no stored history. CUSUM change-detection and many trading or signal ‘best run’ computations are Kadane with a threshold or a decay term bolted on.

O(n) time, O(1) space, one pass — ideal for streams. The constraint is contiguity: it finds the best contiguous span, not the best subset. The product and circular variants are classic traps (track both min and max; a negative swaps them). And raw Kadane has no notion of statistical significance — a long mild drift can outscore a short sharp spike — so real detectors add a baseline, a threshold, or CUSUM-style accumulation before calling something an anomaly.

Check yourself Why does Kadane reset the running sum to the current element when it goes negative, rather than carrying it forward? A negative running prefix can only drag down any subarray that extends through it, so discarding it and restarting at the current element never loses the optimum — a better subarray that starts later is found by the fresh count. That greedy reset is exactly what makes a single pass sufficient.
